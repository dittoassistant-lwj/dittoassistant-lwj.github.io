Most important paper of the day

Completion-Path Credits: Multi-Resource Control for Scale-Up Fabrics

Authors & affiliations: Fan Yang, Jiaqi Liu, Tao Jiang, and Zhan Wang. Fan Yang, Tao Jiang, and Zhan Wang — Institute of Computing Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China; Jiaqi Liu — Institute of Computing Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China, and Hangzhou Institute for Advanced Study, UCAS, Hangzhou, China.

Tags: Scale-up Fabrics, Receiver Credits, GPU/AI Interconnects

Why this is the top pick: This is the strongest signal because it treats scale-up flow control as a multi-resource endpoint problem, not just a link-buffer problem. That is exactly where GPU fabric designs get subtle: remote-memory operations can be tiny in bytes but expensive in completion-path service time.

Deeper explanation: SemaCredit is interesting because it attacks a mismatch at the receiver rather than another sender-side rate knob. A tensor write, a remote read, a small atomic, and a completion notification can have very different pressure points even if their byte counts are similar. The paper’s controller admits each operation against the resource vector it will consume — HBM partition service, serialized atomic execution, and response injection — then returns each component when that stage actually completes. For AI scale-up fabrics, that is a practical abstraction shift: flow control should protect the completion path that makes remote-memory operations usable, not only the ingress buffer that stores packets.

  • Problem: Scale-up fabrics carry large tensor writes, remote reads, atomics, responses, and synchronization over shared receiver-side resources, but byte-denominated credit schemes do not capture small operations that are bottlenecked by atomic engines or response injection rather than HBM bandwidth.
  • Key contributions: Introduces SemaCredit, a receiver-side controller that admits each remote-memory operation against a vector of target resources rather than a single byte budget.; Returns credits when the corresponding HBM, atomic, or response stage completes, aligning flow-control accounting with the resource actually released.; Evaluates the design in a deterministic event simulator with multipath queues, eight HBM partitions, a serialized atomic engine, and response injection, reporting P99 latency reductions for atomic contention, response incast, and AllReduce/read-shaped mixes while matching byte credits on HBM-dominated traffic.
  • Why it matters architecturally: It makes endpoint resource accounting a first-class part of fabric control, which is highly relevant for accelerator supernodes mixing tensor movement, atomics, reads, and notifications.

Completion-Path Credits: Multi-Resource Control for Scale-Up Fabrics

Authors & affiliations: Fan Yang, Jiaqi Liu, Tao Jiang, and Zhan Wang. Fan Yang, Tao Jiang, and Zhan Wang — Institute of Computing Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China; Jiaqi Liu — Institute of Computing Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China, and Hangzhou Institute for Advanced Study, UCAS, Hangzhou, China.

Tags: Scale-up Fabrics, Receiver Credits, GPU/AI Interconnects

Problem: Scale-up fabrics carry large tensor writes, remote reads, atomics, responses, and synchronization over shared receiver-side resources, but byte-denominated credit schemes do not capture small operations that are bottlenecked by atomic engines or response injection rather than HBM bandwidth.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces SemaCredit, a receiver-side controller that admits each remote-memory operation against a vector of target resources rather than a single byte budget.
  • Returns credits when the corresponding HBM, atomic, or response stage completes, aligning flow-control accounting with the resource actually released.
  • Evaluates the design in a deterministic event simulator with multipath queues, eight HBM partitions, a serialized atomic engine, and response injection, reporting P99 latency reductions for atomic contention, response incast, and AllReduce/read-shaped mixes while matching byte credits on HBM-dominated traffic.

Why it matters: This is the strongest signal because it treats scale-up flow control as a multi-resource endpoint problem, not just a link-buffer problem. That is exactly where GPU fabric designs get subtle: remote-memory operations can be tiny in bytes but expensive in completion-path service time.

Key insights:

  • Bytes are a weak currency when the bottleneck is atomic execution or reverse-path response injection.
  • Receiver-driven credits become more useful when they model endpoint resources explicitly: HBM partitions, atomic units, and response engines each need their own accounting.
  • The application-shaped results matter because AllReduce-shaped traffic and remote-read-heavy traffic stress different completion paths; one global byte window hides that distinction.
  • For NVLink/UALink/UEC-like fabrics, endpoint admission policy can be as important as path selection once remote memory operations and notifications share the same target.

Actionable lessons: Design fabric credit loops around the constrained resource that determines completion, not around packet size alone. For collective/runtime evaluations, include small-control/atomic/read-response mixes next to bulk tensor transfers; otherwise the design may look stable in bandwidth tests and still fall over in completion-heavy phases.

Predict Before Replay: Joint FEC and Flight Control for Reliable Scale-Up Links

Authors & affiliations: Fan Yang, Jiaqi Liu, Tao Jiang, and Zhan Wang. Fan Yang, Tao Jiang, and Zhan Wang — Institute of Computing Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China; Jiaqi Liu — Institute of Computing Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Beijing, China, and Hangzhou Institute for Advanced Study, UCAS, Hangzhou, China.

Tags: Scale-up Networking, Reliability/FEC, Collectives

Problem: High-rate scale-up accelerator links rely on FEC followed by replay for residual errors. When burst errors occur, delayed feedback can let many additional flits enter the replay window, turning one corrupted flit into a larger suffix retransmission event.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Identifies replay exposure as the missing coupling between adaptive FEC and delayed standard replay in high-rate scale-up links.
  • Presents PREFACE, a pre-FEC adaptive coding and exposure controller that uses corrected-symbol observations to estimate burst risk before an uncorrectable replay event occurs.
  • Implements an ns-3 model with UALink 200G 1.0-inspired replay semantics and evaluates fixed FEC, post-decode adaptation, predictive FEC, and joint FEC/flight-control baselines, including a modeled ring AllReduce schedule.

Why it matters: Reliability policy is usually discussed as a coding choice, but this paper makes the transport-facing point: the amount of outstanding traffic during a risky interval is part of the reliability cost function.

Key insights:

  • Corrected pre-FEC symbols are an early warning signal; waiting for an uncorrectable decode is already late for flight-size control.
  • Adaptive FEC without exposure control can still leave large replay tails because the replay window keeps growing during a burst.
  • The AllReduce experiment is useful because it asks whether link-level reliability decisions survive synchronized collective traffic rather than only random flit streams.
  • The clean design property is that PREFACE changes coding and admission but leaves standard replay correctness intact.

Actionable lessons: Treat FEC strength and outstanding-flight caps as one coupled controller for scale-up links. In supernode simulations, measure replay amplification and collective completion sensitivity under bursty channels, not just raw bit-error correction rates.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is the useful mental model: one pre-FEC burst posterior drives both FEC profile selection and outstanding-flight control while standard replay remains the correctness mechanism. Figure 5 maps the effect onto a modeled synchronous ring AllReduce.

Collective Communication for Distributed LLM Systems: Planning, Runtime Adaptation, and Computation Coordination

Authors & affiliations: Xuebin Song, Menghao Zhang, Yuezheng Liu, Jinyi Xia, Shucan Yang, Xiaohe Hu, Chunming Hu, and Mingwei Xu. Xuebin Song, Menghao Zhang, Yuezheng Liu, Jinyi Xia, Shucan Yang, and Chunming Hu — School of Software, Beihang University, Beijing, China; Xuebin Song, Menghao Zhang, and Mingwei Xu — State Key Laboratory of Internet Architecture, Tsinghua University, Beijing, China; Xiaohe Hu — Infrawaves, Beijing, China.

Tags: Collective Communication, LLM Training/Serving, Runtime Adaptation

Problem: LLM systems increasingly depend on AllReduce, ReduceScatter, AllGather, and AlltoAll across heterogeneous GPU interconnects, multi-NIC servers, mixed parallelism, and latency-sensitive serving paths, but the optimization space is fragmented across planning, runtime, and compute/communication overlap.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds a tutorial-style taxonomy around three layers: topology-aware communication planning, runtime execution/adaptation on real GPU/network hardware, and computation-communication coordination.
  • Connects collective choices to LLM parallelism modes including data, tensor, pipeline, and expert parallelism.
  • Surveys open challenges around heterogeneous clusters, multi-NIC networking, low-latency inference, and turning collective micro-optimizations into end-to-end training/serving wins.

Why it matters: This is a survey rather than a new mechanism, but it is useful as a map of where collective optimization is moving: away from one static NCCL plan and toward topology-aware, runtime-adaptive, workload-coordinated communication.

Key insights:

  • Collectives are no longer a hidden library detail; they are a common substrate shaping training throughput and inference SLOs.
  • Planning, execution, and overlap are separable layers, but real wins require closing the loop across all three.
  • Expert-parallel AlltoAll and tensor-parallel reductions stress the fabric differently, so one collective policy cannot be judged in isolation.
  • The paper explicitly foregrounds heterogeneous GPU interconnects and multi-NIC networking, which matches where production superpods are heading.

Actionable lessons: Use the taxonomy as a checklist for new collective proposals: specify the topology model, runtime adaptation signals, and where compute/communication overlap is created or lost. Benchmark at iteration/SLO level, not only collective bandwidth.

Bounded-State Restoration: Decoupling Local Restore Capacity from External LLM State

Authors & affiliations: Zixuan Li. Zixuan Li — China Academy of Railway Sciences Corporation Limited, Beijing, China.

Tags: LLM Serving, KV Cache/State Restoration, Disaggregated Storage

Problem: Hierarchical KV-cache systems can retain huge long-context execution state in host memory, SSD, or remote memory, but restoring that state can require a large local staging working set before the request becomes executable again.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Defines the restoration working set (RWS) as a distinct resource from external retained state size.
  • Introduces Bounded-State Restoration, which probes the complete reusable prefix without materializing it all locally, then installs confirmed state through a bounded reusable window.
  • Extends LMCache and vLLM with probe-only lookup, bounded retrieval, staging reuse, failure invalidation, and force-local recovery; reports fixed 500.75 MiB/rank local RWS while external state grows to 31.277 GiB/rank in the evaluated setup.

Why it matters: For disaggregated serving, “can I store the KV/cache state somewhere?” is not the same as “can I safely and quickly make it live again.” This paper isolates the restore-side memory bottleneck, which matters for long-context reuse across GPU/CPU/SSD/RDMA tiers.

Key insights:

  • External capacity and live restoration capacity are different resources; scaling one does not automatically scale the other.
  • Request-level commit semantics are important: partial installed state must not be advertised as a valid reusable prefix.
  • The measured result separates restore capacity from restore throughput — SSD tuning reduced TTFT without changing the bounded RWS.
  • The system angle is relevant even if the exact implementation is narrow: KV-cache disaggregation needs failure semantics and staging bounds, not just a larger backing store.

Actionable lessons: When designing KV-cache transfer or remote-state reuse, budget local restore staging explicitly. Add all-or-nothing prefix validity, tier/rank failure invalidation, and separate metrics for retained-state capacity, restoration working set, and restore TTFT.
