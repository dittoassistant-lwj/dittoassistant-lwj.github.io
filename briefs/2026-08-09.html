AFD-Ledger: Deployment Provisioning for Attention--FFN Disaggregation

Authors & affiliations: Chengyu Qiu, Xiao Fu, Fengcun Li, Yulei Qian, Yuchen Xie, Xunliang Cai, Yingdi Shan, Yongwei Wu, and Mingxing Zhang. Affiliations: Tsinghua University; Meituan.

Tags: LLM Serving, MoE Disaggregation, Heterogeneous AI Infrastructure

Problem: Attention–FFN disaggregation can look attractive from local FFN utilization, but the real decision is deployment-level: under the same model, TPOT SLO, hardware budget, hardware catalog, and runtime constraints, does AFD beat the best collocated deployment?

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces AFD-Ledger, an offline analytical provisioning system that independently searches collocated and AFD deployments under a fixed deployment specification.
  • Uses an evaluation-bounded hardware search to cut complete deployment evaluations by 68.8%–83.5% while recovering the exhaustive optimum in feasible search spaces.
  • Validates the model on three physical LongCat 2.0 deployments, preserving the correct AFD-vs-collocated decision and predicting throughput ratios within 6.6%–9.6% of measurements.

Why it matters: This is a useful antidote to “disaggregation is automatically better.” It treats MoE serving as a full cluster provisioning problem where KV capacity, attention workers, expert workers, batching, replicas, device prices, and TPOT boundaries interact.

Key insights:

  • Local FFN MFU gains can be erased by losing request-serving capacity on attention/KV workers.
  • The best role-specific device choice is not necessarily the best end-to-end hardware pair; hardware complementarity only appears after complete deployment provisioning.
  • Homogeneous AFD improves fixed-budget throughput only in a minority of the studied settings, while heterogeneous AFD needs the right capability–price boundary crossing.
  • Memory capacity and device cost often matter more than raw compute or bandwidth once they unlock more replicas, larger resident batches, or different worker allocations.

Actionable lessons: For production MoE serving, compare AFD against a fully reprovisioned collocated baseline, not a reported/default collocated layout. Model attention/KV capacity as a first-class resource, and evaluate sensitivity to device price, memory, runtime overhead, and TPOT target before buying into disaggregation.

AsymSpec: Efficient Cloud-Edge Speculative Decoding over Asymmetric Networks

Authors & affiliations: Guotao Yang, Hao Chen, Rui Guo, Xinyu Li, Liang Zheng, Sheng Chen, Yitao Hu, and Keqiu Li. Affiliations: Tianjin University, China.

Tags: LLM Serving, Edge/Cloud Networking, Speculative Decoding

Problem: Cloud-edge speculative decoding inserts network communication into every speculative block. When the uplink is constrained, candidate uploads queue while cloud verification idles; if the edge runs ahead on the same request, later drafts can be invalidated by rejection or bonus tokens.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes an asymmetric verification protocol that keeps common-path acceptance uploads compact and shifts richer rejection-only correction information to the downlink.
  • Uses a total-variation certificate to decide when a small target top-K response is enough, escalating only when needed toward exact recovery or full distribution fallback.
  • Introduces a confirmed-prefix pipeline that schedules only independent, valid requests at the edge while the cloud re-batches arrived blocks.
  • Reports 2.82×–28.03× output-token throughput over the strongest baseline across draft/target pairs, workloads, and asymmetric network profiles.

Why it matters: The paper is not superpod scale-up networking, but it is directly about network-facing LLM serving: it designs the decoding protocol around asymmetric link capacity rather than treating network transfer as a fixed tax.

Key insights:

  • For edge/cloud inference, uplink bottlenecks are more damaging than raw RTT because they serialize candidate traffic and starve cloud verification.
  • Same-request runahead is tempting but unsafe: unresolved prefixes create dependency chains that can waste draft compute.
  • Protocol asymmetry matters; put small, frequent acceptance-path data on the constrained direction and reserve larger correction payloads for the direction with more capacity.
  • Ready-pool scheduling is a clean way to hide verification waits without violating speculative-decoding correctness.

Actionable lessons: When designing disaggregated or edge/cloud serving, make the protocol aware of directional bandwidth. Separate “confirmed prefix” work from speculative descendants, and measure throughput under asymmetric uplink/downlink profiles rather than symmetric datacenter assumptions.

MARS: Multipath Adaptive Reliable Service

Authors & affiliations: Yitong Li, Xinjiao Li, and Dirk Kutscher. Affiliations: The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou).

Tags: Transport Protocols, Multipath Networking, WAN/Cloud Pipelines

Problem: End-to-end multipath transports such as MPTCP and MPQUIC are deployable but limited by endpoint-visible paths and delayed congestion feedback, while forwarder-assisted designs can expose more paths but risk unsafe coordination or infrastructure dependence.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents MARS, a receiver-driven, forwarder-assisted multipath transport deployed as an incremental UDP overlay at clients, servers, relays, or CDN-like nodes.
  • Combines tier-synchronized overlay path discovery with coupled consumer/forwarder congestion control so forwarding choices expand path diversity without unsafe loops or stale bottleneck feedback.
  • Implements MARS in simulation and as a prototype; evaluation reports up to 81.5% lower max p95 flow completion time than ECMP-limited baselines with cooperating forwarders.

Why it matters: This is more WAN/cloud-service than AI superpod, but the mechanism is relevant for geo-distributed AI pipelines, checkpoint movement, dataset/cache transfer, and multi-site service backbones where endpoint-only multipath underuses available path diversity.

Key insights:

  • Multipath gains depend heavily on deployment: endpoint-only MARS stays competitive, while cooperating forwarders expose the larger benefit.
  • Receiver-driven synchronization avoids local forwarding decisions that create loops, backward paths, or unnecessarily long detours.
  • Coupling congestion control between consumers and forwarders lets the transport react nearer to bottlenecks than pure endpoint feedback.
  • Failure recovery is part of the transport design, not a separate routing afterthought.

Actionable lessons: For AI infrastructure that moves large state across sites, consider whether the transport can see enough path diversity. If not, overlay forwarders or service-managed relays may be more effective than only tuning endpoint CCAs. Keep loop-safety and bottleneck-local feedback in the design from day one.

verified arXiv paper · cxl/coherence, heterogeneous systems, scale-up architecture

Automated Synthesis of Heterogeneous, Hierarchical, Scoped Coherence Protocols

Authors & affiliations: Fletch Rydell, An Qi Zhang, Nicolai Oswald, Andres Goens, Vijay Nagarajan, and Daniel Sorin. Affiliations: Duke University; University of Utah; NVIDIA; TU Darmstadt.

Tags: CXL/Coherence, Heterogeneous Systems, Scale-up Architecture

Problem: Modern heterogeneous shared-memory systems combine clusters with different memory consistency models through global protocols such as CXL or AMBA CHI. Manually writing shims between cluster-local and global coherence protocols is subtle, error-prone, and can leave performance on the floor.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Defines a shim API that classifies protocol transactions by semantic coherence guarantees instead of forcing every transaction into a conservative SWMR mapping.
  • Builds ShimGen, an automated synthesis engine that composes hierarchical protocols with both SWMR and relaxed accesses, including scoped memory accesses and lazy invalidation.
  • Shows a case where a manually designed AMD APU-style hierarchical protocol fails compound consistency, while ShimGen’s synthesized protocol preserves it.
  • Demonstrates performance benefits from accommodating non-SWMR global behavior rather than mapping everything to strict ownership.

Why it matters: This is adjacent but important for scale-up AI infrastructure: CXL-style coherent fabrics, CPU/GPU/NPU memory sharing, and multi-chip modules all need correct and performant protocol boundaries. Bad shims become invisible latency, correctness, or interoperability traps.

Key insights:

  • The key abstraction is semantic: shims should map the guarantees a transaction provides, not just the operation name.
  • GPU-style relaxed/self-invalidating behavior does not fit cleanly into CPU-centric SWMR coherence assumptions.
  • Correctness across hierarchy is compound consistency; preserving each cluster’s memory model through the global fabric is harder than preserving per-protocol invariants in isolation.
  • Conservative coherence composition can be correct but unnecessarily slow, especially when global protocols support one-off or scoped accesses.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating CXL or coherent accelerator fabrics, ask how local accelerator protocols compose with the global fabric and what assumptions the shim makes. Treat coherence translation as a design surface, not glue logic. For AI nodes with heterogeneous accelerators, correctness and performance both live at the protocol boundary.

verified arXiv paper · network traffic, compression, programmable rules

Learning Compression Rules for Network Traffic

Authors & affiliations: Quentin Lampin, Éloi Sainte-Beuve, Louis-Adrien Dufrène, Guillaume Larue, and Massih-Reza Amini. Affiliations: Orange Research; Université Grenoble Alpes.

Tags: Network Traffic, Compression, Programmable Rules

Problem: Rule-based header compression standards such as SCHC rely on shared sender/receiver rule contexts, but efficient rule sets are usually hand-written and hard to maintain as protocols, deployments, and traffic patterns change.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Casts rule learning as unsupervised structure discovery followed by constrained rule selection under a hard budget on installable rules.
  • Introduces RECAP, using normalized entropy-ratio recursive partitioning plus dynamic programming to select the rule subset with maximum expected compression gain.
  • Evaluates on four real IoT and 5G core-network datasets and reports better held-out compression than expert-style baselines with small learned rule budgets.

Why it matters: This is lower priority than the LLM-serving papers, but it is a real networking systems paper with a practical data-plane angle: learning compact rule sets for structured traffic could matter wherever rule-table budget and protocol evolution collide.

Key insights:

  • The rule budget is a real systems constraint; maximizing compression without limiting installable rules is the wrong objective.
  • Separating structural templates from value-based entropy splits makes the learned rules more robust to small samples.
  • The paper’s 5G core-network datasets are a useful reminder that header compression and traffic structure are not only IoT problems.
  • Learned rules can outperform expert-written profiles, but operational safety would still require validation against protocol corner cases.

Actionable lessons: If considering traffic compression or rule-table offload, optimize for compact installable rules and held-out traffic, not just training-set compressibility. Keep rule generation auditable: learned data-plane rules need explainability, constraints, and fallback behavior.
