AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s useful signal is less about new transports and more about where bytes and operators should live in AI serving clusters: disaggregated MoE worker pools, KV movement out of HBM, operator-level scaling, and high-capacity near-accelerator memory.

Most important paper of the day

Janus: Disaggregating Attention and Experts for Scalable MoE Inference

Authors & affiliations: Zhexiang Zhang, Ye Wang, Yumiao Zhao, Jiayu Xiao, Qianjing Yang, Xiangyu Wang, Jingzhe Jiang, Qizhen Weng, Ruichuan Chen, Shaohuai Shi, Yin Chen, and Minchen Yu. Affiliations: The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen; Institute of Artificial Intelligence (TeleAI), China Telecom; Shenzhen Loop Area Institute; Nokia Bell Labs; Harbin Institute of Technology, Shenzhen.

Tags: MoE Serving, Disaggregated Inference, Inter-Worker Communication

Why this is the top pick: Janus is the most fabric-facing paper in today’s batch: it takes the attention/expert split seriously as a distributed-systems boundary, then designs an adaptive two-phase communication path so disaggregation does not devolve into many tiny cross-node transfers.

Deeper explanation: The paper’s core move is to stop serving MoE models as monolithic replicas. Attention layers and MoE layers have different bottlenecks: attention is sequence/compute sensitive, while experts dominate memory footprint and have activation-dependent latency. Janus places them in separate GPU worker pools, then coordinates the layer-wise data exchange with a two-phase transmission mechanism that aggregates intra-node intermediate data before bulk cross-node transfer. That is exactly the architectural tension Ethan cares about: disaggregation improves resource fit, but only if the communication pattern is reshaped to avoid O(m × n) small-message pain between attention and expert pools.

  • Problem: Large MoE inference wastes GPUs when attention and expert layers are forced into the same resource shape, but naively separating them creates layer-by-layer m-to-n communication between worker pools and can blow up latency.
  • Key contributions: Disaggregates attention and MoE layers into separately provisioned GPU worker pools; introduces adaptive two-phase communication to reduce small cross-node transfers; adds microsecond-scale activated-expert scheduling and fine-grained SLO-aware scaling/expert placement. The authors report up to 4.7× per-GPU throughput over state-of-the-art monolithic systems while satisfying token-level SLOs.
  • Why it matters architecturally: This is a clean example of application-level topology shaping: the serving runtime changes the traffic pattern before the network has to carry it.
  • Diagram note: The PDF includes Table 1 on MoE expert memory footprint and architecture/scheduling figures for the Janus disaggregation path; the most useful visual idea is the attention-pool/MoE-pool split plus the two-phase transmission used to collapse many small exchanges into larger transfers.

Janus: Disaggregating Attention and Experts for Scalable MoE Inference

Authors & affiliations: Zhexiang Zhang, Ye Wang, Yumiao Zhao, Jiayu Xiao, Qianjing Yang, Xiangyu Wang, Jingzhe Jiang, Qizhen Weng, Ruichuan Chen, Shaohuai Shi, Yin Chen, and Minchen Yu. Affiliations: The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen; Institute of Artificial Intelligence (TeleAI), China Telecom; Shenzhen Loop Area Institute; Nokia Bell Labs; Harbin Institute of Technology, Shenzhen.

Tags: MoE Serving, Disaggregated Inference, Inter-Worker Communication

Problem: MoE serving has a resource mismatch: expert layers dominate memory footprint and are activation-load sensitive, while attention layers have different compute/sequence behavior. Monolithic deployment over-provisions one side, but disaggregating attention and experts introduces frequent cross-pool communication.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds Janus around separated attention and MoE GPU worker pools so each layer type can be provisioned independently.
  • Uses adaptive two-phase transmission: intra-node aggregation followed by bulk transfer between sub-clusters, reducing the many-small-message overhead of naive m-to-n attention/expert exchange.
  • Adds a GPU-kernel activated-expert scheduler plus fine-grained SLO-aware resource scaling and expert placement, reporting up to 4.7× per-GPU throughput versus monolithic baselines and about 40% lower resource cost under real-world traces.

Why it matters: Janus is a good systems/networking read because its gains depend on matching resource disaggregation with communication-shape control. It is not enough to split the model; the runtime also has to coalesce, place, and schedule the resulting cross-worker data movement.

Key insights:

  • Attention/MoE disaggregation creates a classic resource-efficiency versus communication-latency trade-off; Janus attacks both rather than treating communication as a fixed tax.
  • The two-phase transmission is a practical pattern: trade a modest increase in total data volume for fewer, larger network-visible transfers.
  • Activated-expert balancing is time-critical because layer-wise MoE compute can complete in hundreds of microseconds; CPU-side scheduling would be too slow.
  • Expert placement and scaling are coupled: changing the number of MoE instances without reshaping expert placement can recreate hotspots.

Actionable lessons: For MoE serving architectures, expose attention/expert layer types as separate placement domains, but make inter-domain communication a first-class design objective. Measure transfer count, message size distribution, and layer-wise synchronization tails, not only aggregate GPU utilization.

Useful diagram: The PDF includes architecture/scheduling figures around the Janus disaggregated MoE path; the key diagram to inspect is the attention-pool/MoE-pool communication flow with two-phase aggregation and transfer.

OasisKV: Scaling In-Decode KV Cache Beyond HBM with Lookahead Sparse Prefetching

Authors & affiliations: Can Xiao, Sukmin Cho, Junbong We, Zhixiong Niu, Jianyi Cheng, Yiren Zhao, Youngjin Kwon, Yongqiang Xiong, Rui Ma, and Junyi Liu. Affiliations: Imperial College London; KAIST; Microsoft Research; University of Edinburgh.

Tags: LLM Serving, KV Cache, Memory Disaggregation

Problem: During long-context decode, KV cache capacity and bandwidth dominate HBM pressure. Moving KV to host or remote memory helps capacity but can destroy time-per-output-token if needed entries arrive late.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Decouples full KV-cache storage from HBM during decode and keeps only predicted-important KV entries in accelerator memory.
  • Uses lookahead tokens from speculative decoding to predict future important tokens, then prefetches selected KV blocks from higher-capacity tiers into HBM before use.
  • Implements the design in vLLM; reports roughly 2× dense throughput under prefill-decode disaggregation while admitting requests with 6.5–9.7× less KV transfer and 2.2–2.6× less decode-node host memory than full KV transfer.

Why it matters: This is directly relevant to disaggregated serving: KV is becoming a distributed memory object, and the byte path between HBM, host DRAM, and remote memory determines whether long-context serving scales or stalls.

Key insights:

  • The paper frames KV movement with a memory roofline: throughput is bounded by token-KV intensity times the relevant KV-cache bandwidth tier.
  • Sparsity only helps if the system can prefetch the right KV before decode needs it; otherwise off-HBM capacity simply turns into latency.
  • Speculative decoding becomes a systems signal, not just a decoding optimization: draft tokens inform future KV demand.
  • The prefill-decode disaggregation result is the most network-facing piece because it reduces KV transfer volume at the boundary between prefill and decode nodes.

Actionable lessons: Treat KV-cache movement as a bandwidth-tier scheduling problem. For disaggregated serving, instrument KV bytes per admitted request, prefetch hit timing, host/remote-memory pressure, and TPOT sensitivity to even small off-HBM fetch fractions.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is especially useful: it presents a KV-cache memory roofline showing dense, sparse, HBM, CPU-GPU I/O, and remote-memory bandwidth ceilings.

Potential Applications of HBF in LLM Serving Systems

Authors & affiliations: Yihan Yin, Yilun Zhao, Zhixin Yun, Guanying Wu, Feng Zhu, Kai Tao, Shu Li, Fei Huang, Zhe Zhang, Shuangchen Li, and Hongzhong Zheng. Affiliations: School of Integrated Circuits, Peking University; Alibaba DAMO Academy; Alibaba Cloud Computing; Hupan Lab.

Tags: LLM Serving, Memory Hierarchy, High-Bandwidth Flash

Problem: HBM bandwidth is scaling faster than HBM capacity, while serving systems need more resident model weights, experts, KV state, and model variants. The paper asks where a NAND-flash-based High-Bandwidth Flash tier could help without breaking the HBM execution path.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Explains HBF as a capacity-oriented stacked NAND tier packaged near the compute die, intended to expand resident model-state capacity rather than replace HBM bandwidth.
  • Models HBF value for read-mostly model-state objects, especially MoE expert replicas and multi-model serving residency.
  • Shows through simulations that gains depend on preserving HBM-resident execution while using HBF to widen the resident set and reduce model loading or hot-model replication pressure.

Why it matters: This is a hardware/software co-design signal for future serving nodes: if model-state capacity becomes the harder dimension than bandwidth, the serving runtime needs explicit policies for what lives in HBM versus a slower but much larger near-accelerator tier.

Key insights:

  • HBF is most plausible for read-mostly weight/expert residency, not arbitrary hot decode-state access.
  • For MoE, added capacity can buy more expert replicas, which is a placement/load-balancing lever rather than merely a storage optimization.
  • The paper’s warning is important: HBF should expand the resident set while HBM remains the fast execution path.
  • Multi-model serving benefits come from avoiding load churn and enabling hot-model replication under active-model volatility.

Actionable lessons: Model future serving nodes as multi-tier memory systems with explicit object classes: hot activations/KV in HBM, read-mostly weights and cold experts in HBF-like tiers, and remote/storage paths for deeper capacity. Placement policy should be workload-aware, not just capacity-greedy.

vToken: Token-Level Virtualization for Reclaimable KV Caches

Authors & affiliations: Yuanhang Gao, Xiangrui Yang, Yuanfeng Chen, Hongjia Chen, Qianru Lv, Wenfei Wu, and Dongsheng Li. Affiliations: National University of Defense Technology; Peking University.

Tags: LLM Serving, KV Cache, Runtime Memory Management

Problem: PagedAttention manages KV memory in fixed-size blocks, but token-level eviction policies operate at finer granularity. That mismatch leaves live and dead tokens mixed inside allocated blocks, so memory can remain unreclaimable even after eviction decisions.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces a token-level virtualization layer that decouples logical token liveness from physical block placement through token-table indirection.
  • Reclaims physical KV space by asynchronously repacking live tokens while preserving PagedAttention kernels and CUDA Graph compatibility.
  • Implements vToken in vLLM and reports 27.2%–72.3% fewer retained KV blocks per request, up to 1.37× higher SLA-constrained throughput, and up to 2× feasible concurrency under constrained active-KV budget.

Why it matters: vToken is not a network paper, but it affects the memory pressure that often drives disaggregated KV movement. Better local reclamation can delay or reduce the need to spill KV across PCIe, host memory, or remote memory.

Key insights:

  • Allocator granularity and policy granularity must align; token-aware eviction loses value if physical reclamation is block-bound.
  • The indirection layer is deliberately compatibility-preserving, which matters for production inference stacks built around vLLM/PagedAttention kernels.
  • Asynchronous repacking converts dead-token holes into usable KV capacity without forcing every policy to implement its own memory movement machinery.
  • There is an architectural complementarity with OasisKV: reclaim locally first, then decide what must move to slower tiers.

Actionable lessons: Before designing remote KV-cache tiers, quantify unreclaimable in-block KV fragmentation. Add metrics for logical live tokens, retained physical blocks, repacking bandwidth, and CUDA Graph compatibility so memory-policy wins translate into actual capacity.

Useful diagram: The PDF includes figures explaining the token-table indirection and repacking path; these are useful for understanding how logical token liveness is separated from physical KV blocks.

OpScale: Operator-level Provisioning and Autoscaling for LLM Serving

Authors & affiliations: Xingqi Cui, Chieh-Jan Mike Liang, Ziang Tang, Jiarong Xing, and Haoran Qiu. Affiliations: Rice University; Microsoft Research; Microsoft Azure Research.

Tags: LLM Serving, Operator Placement, Cluster Orchestration

Problem: Model-level autoscaling is too coarse and slow for bursty LLM serving traffic. Scaling full replicas wastes GPU resources, while operator-level scaling introduces placement, interference, and interconnect-awareness challenges.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Characterizes operator heterogeneity and argues for operator-level provisioning/scaling rather than monolithic model replicas.
  • Builds a profiling, control, and execution framework that scales bottleneck operators and places them with contention and inter-operator communication in mind.
  • Evaluates on up to 40 NVIDIA A100 GPUs and 24 GB200s, reporting SLO attainment with up to 36.3% fewer GPUs or 44% higher throughput under fixed cost budgets.

Why it matters: This is adjacent to networking but still relevant: once operators become placement units, cross-GPU and cross-server communication becomes part of the autoscaling objective rather than a hidden side effect.

Key insights:

  • Different operators respond differently to scaling; attention, linear, and normalization stages do not share one elasticity curve.
  • Traffic bursts can change at sub-10-second timescales, while loading a full model replica can take too long to respond cleanly.
  • Operator colocation saves resources but can create contention across SMs, memory, and interconnects.
  • Fine-grained elasticity is only useful if the control plane can avoid creating new communication bottlenecks.

Actionable lessons: For serving schedulers, keep a communication-cost term in operator placement. Track which scaled operators become remote to each other, how often routing crosses server boundaries, and whether elasticity increases interconnect pressure during traffic spikes.
