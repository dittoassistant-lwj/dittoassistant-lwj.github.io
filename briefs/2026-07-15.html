From Tensor Buffer to Distributed Memory Hierarchy: A Survey of KV Cache Management for LLM Serving

Authors & affiliations: Jie Li, Tongyang Wang, and Yong Chen. Affiliations: Texas Tech University, USA.

Tags: LLM Serving, KV Cache, Distributed Memory Hierarchy

Why this is the top pick: This is the most useful map in today’s batch because it turns KV cache from “GPU memory pressure” into an explicit distributed-systems design space. For disaggregated serving, long-context inference, and agentic workloads, KV placement and ownership determine whether the network is an accelerator or the new bottleneck.

Deeper explanation: The survey is useful because it gives a vocabulary for decisions that otherwise get mixed together: where KV lives, who owns it, when it moves, how long it persists, and what substrate backs it. That is exactly where modern LLM serving becomes a networking problem. A local-paged design optimizes allocator behavior; a disaggregated-pipeline design optimizes prefill/decode state transfer; a shared-store or memory-pool design adds a real data plane for reusable KV. The paper does not claim one archetype wins universally, which is the right answer: the network, failure model, reuse pattern, and isolation requirements determine the best point in the design space.

  • Problem: KV cache has shifted from a per-request tensor buffer into a large, persistent, distributed memory object, but the systems literature still evaluates many designs with local-cache metrics that miss ownership, placement, isolation, and network-transfer behavior.
  • Key contributions: Classifies more than thirty KV-management systems along four axes: locality, lifetime, ownership, and substrate. Identifies five recurring architectural archetypes: local-paged, disaggregated-pipeline, shared-store, memory-pool, and hybrid-tier. Audits current evaluations and calls out missing KV-specific measurements around fault tolerance, isolation, tiered eviction, speculative decoding, MoE serving, and shared-cache semantics.
  • Why it matters architecturally: KV state is becoming a distributed memory hierarchy problem. That means serving architectures need explicit control over remote transfer, ownership, isolation, and recovery—not just larger HBM pools.

From Tensor Buffer to Distributed Memory Hierarchy: A Survey of KV Cache Management for LLM Serving

Authors & affiliations: Jie Li, Tongyang Wang, and Yong Chen. Affiliations: Texas Tech University, USA.

Tags: LLM Serving, KV Cache, Distributed Memory Hierarchy

Problem: KV cache has shifted from a per-request tensor buffer into a large, persistent, distributed memory object, but the systems literature still evaluates many designs with local-cache metrics that miss ownership, placement, isolation, and network-transfer behavior.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Classifies more than thirty KV-management systems along four axes: locality, lifetime, ownership, and substrate.
  • Identifies five recurring architectural archetypes: local-paged, disaggregated-pipeline, shared-store, memory-pool, and hybrid-tier.
  • Audits current evaluations and calls out missing KV-specific measurements around fault tolerance, isolation, tiered eviction, speculative decoding, MoE serving, and shared-cache semantics.

Why it matters: This is the most useful map in today’s batch because it turns KV cache from “GPU memory pressure” into an explicit distributed-systems design space. For disaggregated serving, long-context inference, and agentic workloads, KV placement and ownership determine whether the network is an accelerator or the new bottleneck.

Key insights:

  • The clean mental model is ownership: who creates, mutates, transfers, evicts, and is allowed to reuse KV state. Many serving systems differ more in ownership semantics than in attention kernels.
  • The survey’s five archetypes are a helpful checklist for architecture reviews: local paging, prefill/decode pipeline transfer, shared stores, pooled memory, and hybrid tiers each fail differently.
  • The missing-measurement section is valuable: hit rate and throughput are not enough; Ethan-scale systems need transfer-byte accounting, isolation cost, tail-latency impact, recovery semantics, and stale/shared-cache correctness.
  • KV and network design are now coupled. A “better” KV policy can be worse if it creates cross-node fan-out, unbounded cache lookup traffic, or remote eviction storms.

Actionable lessons: For any disaggregated serving experiment, separate local KV allocation metrics from network-visible KV operations: bytes moved, cache-ownership transitions, remote lookup latency, eviction amplification, and recovery behavior after worker failure. Treat KV as a stateful data plane object, not just attention-kernel memory.

HCRMap: Pressure-Aware Hot-Expert Residency Mapping for 3.5D MoE Chiplet Inference

Authors & affiliations: Yongqin Zhang. Affiliations: Nanjing Vocational College of Information Technology, 99 Wenlan Road, Xianlin College Community, Nanjing, Jiangsu, P.R.C..

Tags: MoE Inference, Chiplet Fabrics, Expert Placement

Problem: MoE inference creates persistent hot-expert skew. On 3.5D multi-chiplet systems, the skew is not just compute imbalance; it also stresses inter-chiplet communication, memory bandwidth, I/O paths, and execution queues.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes HCRMap, a hot-expert residency mapping framework that decides when experts should be promoted, retained, demoted, or evicted across memory tiers.
  • Combines expert hotness, weight-loading cost, migration overhead, and runtime pressure signals when mapping routed token groups to resident replicas.
  • Reports large simulated latency reductions over Hydra, MoEntwine, and PIMoE baselines in both prefill and decode stages.

Why it matters: The paper is relevant because it frames MoE placement as a cross-resource pressure problem instead of a pure routing/load-balancing problem. That framing matches scale-up AI fabrics: expert locality, memory hierarchy, and dispatch/gather traffic are inseparable.

Key insights:

  • Hot experts are a locality opportunity and a congestion hazard at the same time: replicating them helps only if memory-tier capacity and I/O pressure are controlled.
  • The two-timescale control idea is sensible: slow residency decisions should not chase every request, while fast token-to-replica mapping must react to transient pressure.
  • The reported gains are simulation-based and should be treated as directional, but the resource-pressure decomposition is a useful design lens for 3.5D / rack-scale MoE accelerators.

Actionable lessons: When designing MoE placement for scale-up fabrics, track expert hotness together with fabric utilization, memory-bank pressure, and migration cost. A placement policy that minimizes token hops can still lose if it concentrates weight loads or queueing on a small memory/I/O region.

Useful diagram: Figure 4 shows the target 3.5D multi-chiplet substrate. Figures 2 and 3 are also useful: they illustrate why simple communication-oriented placement can leave residual hot-load concentration and why replica placement must respect near-tier capacity.

Beyond Task-Agnostic: Task-Aware Grouping for Communication-Efficient Multi-Task MoE Inference

Authors & affiliations: Zhiyao Xu, Aoxue Liu, Zhanjie Ding, Dan Zhao, Yong Jiang, and Qing Li. Affiliations: Tsinghua Shenzhen International Graduate School, Shenzhen, China; Pengcheng Laboratory, Shenzhen, China.

Tags: MoE Serving, Communication-Aware Placement, Load Balancing

Problem: Distributed MoE inference suffers from cross-GPU expert communication and routing-induced imbalance. Existing expert placement methods often aggregate all traces into one global co-activation graph, which hides task-specific expert locality.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces Task-Aware Coactivation Grouping (TACG), which uses task-family-specific dispatch and co-activation traces to derive task-aware expert grouping.
  • Adds Generic Expert Shared Replication (GESR), a lightweight replication mechanism for experts that are central across task families.
  • Reports 31.39% lower average communication cost versus baseline while maintaining a high Jain fairness index, including under distribution shift.

Why it matters: This is a practical MoE systems paper: the important move is recognizing that communication locality is workload-conditioned. If a serving cluster handles multiple task families, one static average placement can be systematically wrong.

Key insights:

  • Task-conditioned co-activation turns expert placement into something closer to workload-aware topology mapping than generic load balancing.
  • Replication should be reserved for generic/high-centrality experts; blind replication can waste scarce HBM and increase migration/control overhead.
  • The paper’s fairness metric matters: reducing bytes is not enough if the placement creates GPU hotspots or degrades tail latency.

Actionable lessons: For MoE serving telemetry, log expert co-activation by task family or traffic class, not only globally. Then test whether different traffic mixes would choose different expert placements before baking a static placement into deployment.

Useful diagram: The paper includes framework and grouping figures; the useful diagrammatic idea is the task-aware co-activation graph that drives expert grouping and selective shared replication.

Parallelizing Large-Scale Tensor Network Contraction on Multiple GPUs

Authors & affiliations: Feng Pan, Hanfeng Gu, Paul Springer, and Xipeng Li. Affiliations: Singapore University of Technology and Design; NVIDIA Corporation.

Tags: Multi-GPU Systems, NVLink/InfiniBand, Communication-Aware Scheduling

Problem: Tensor-network contraction is often parallelized by slicing, which creates independent tasks but repeats work exponentially. The authors ask whether distributing intermediate tensors with explicit communication can beat slicing on modern GPU fabrics.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds a multi-GPU contraction framework that converts a fixed contraction path into a communication-efficient schedule.
  • Uses GEMM-oriented mode reordering and communication-aware mode-distribution planning, implemented around cuTENSORMp-style distributed tensor operations.
  • Reports 7–173× extra speedup beyond slicing within an 8-GPU DGX H100 over NVLink, and much larger extra speedups in modeled 1024-H100 InfiniBand scaling for selected workloads.

Why it matters: Not an LLM paper, but it is a good fabric-aware systems result: when interconnect bandwidth is high enough, explicitly communicating intermediate state can dominate embarrassingly parallel recomputation. That is the same design trade-off behind KV transfer, activation exchange, and distributed optimizer state.

Key insights:

  • The paper is a concrete example of “communicate to avoid redundant compute” working because NVLink bandwidth keeps communication small relative to saved FLOPs.
  • The planner matters: the same mathematical contraction can expose very different communication/computation trade-offs depending on mode ordering and distribution.
  • The InfiniBand scaling result is a reminder that network-aware partitioning can outperform naive task-parallel scaling even when the workload is not a classic collective.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating recomputation-versus-transfer trade-offs in AI systems, model the intermediate tensor layout and fabric explicitly. Average FLOPs saved is not enough; the schedule must preserve locality and avoid generating a pathological communication pattern.

Bandwidth-Aware and Cost-Efficient Pipeline Parallel Scheduling in Geo-Distributed LLM Training

Authors & affiliations: Han Zhang, Jianchun Liu, and Hongli Xu. Affiliations: not clearly listed in the arXiv metadata/PDF.

Tags: Geo-Distributed Training, Pipeline Parallelism, Network-Aware Scheduling

Problem: GPU scarcity motivates geo-distributed LLM training, but pipeline-parallel scheduling across regions must handle heterogeneous WAN bandwidth, cross-region placement, and multi-tenant head-of-line blocking.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes BACE-Pipe, a framework that jointly considers job characteristics and real-time network utilization when prioritizing geo-distributed pipeline-parallel jobs.
  • Uses a bandwidth-aware pathfinder to choose feasible cross-region pipeline paths that avoid communication stalls.
  • Adds a cost-minimizing allocator that prefers cheaper regions among feasible placements and reports lower average JCT and total electricity cost than baselines.

Why it matters: Even though the paper also optimizes cost, the useful technical signal is bandwidth-aware pipeline placement. It treats inter-region bandwidth as a first-class scheduling constraint rather than assuming the WAN is a slow but passive link.

Key insights:

  • Pipeline parallelism can be more WAN-friendly than data parallelism because it exchanges activations/gradients between adjacent stages rather than full-model synchronization.
  • Bandwidth heterogeneity creates pipeline bubbles; the scheduler has to reason about path feasibility, not just GPU availability.
  • Multi-tenant ordering matters: long, bandwidth-heavy jobs can create head-of-line blocking if the scheduler ignores network occupancy.

Actionable lessons: For cross-region or cross-fabric training experiments, expose network utilization and per-stage activation volume to the placement scheduler. A GPU-only scheduler will place stages on “available” devices and then discover the real bottleneck in the WAN.

Useful diagram: Figure 2 shows BACE-Pipe’s end-to-end workflow: dynamic job prioritization, bandwidth-aware path construction, and cost-aware GPU allocation. Figure 1 is a compact illustration of why placement strategy changes under heterogeneous regions.

Fast take

Read the KV-cache survey first for the architecture map. HCRMap and TACG are the MoE placement pair: one focuses on hot experts in chiplet memory hierarchies, the other on task-aware expert locality across GPUs. The tensor-network paper is a nice reminder that explicit communication can beat redundant compute when the fabric is good enough. BACE-Pipe is the WAN-scheduling entry.
