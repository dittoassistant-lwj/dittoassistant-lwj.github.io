Most important paper of the day

ASAP: A Disaggregated and Asynchronous Inference System for MoE Prefill

Authors & affiliations: Weiwei Chen, Shuang Chen, Lele Li, Qiang Hu, Han Li, Xin Ye, Ming Yan, and Zhibin Yu. Affiliations: Huawei.

Tags: LLM Serving, MoE Communication, Scale-Up AI Clusters

Why this is the top pick: ASAP directly attacks a network-facing serving bottleneck in sparse MoE inference: attention data-parallel groups and expert-parallel MoE stages are forced through global synchronization barriers even when online requests are highly skewed. The paper’s core move is architectural, not just scheduling polish: disaggregate attention and MoE execution, then use asynchronous communication primitives so slow DP groups and congested experts stop pacing the whole prefill instance.

Deeper explanation: In production MoE serving, wide expert parallelism reduces expert memory pressure but creates a rigid join point between attention and MoE layers. When request lengths, arrivals, and prefix-cache hits vary, attention DP groups naturally finish at different times; synchronous systems then turn that variance into TTFT inflation and idle accelerators. ASAP reframes the prefill path as an asynchronous pipeline over disaggregated attention and expert stages. The interesting cluster-design lesson is that MoE prefill wants a fabric/runtime contract for fragmented, out-of-order expert work rather than one global per-layer lockstep.

  • Problem: Hybrid DP + TP attention and EP MoE serving creates frequent global barriers; online request skew causes DP imbalance and straggler amplification.
  • Key contributions: Disaggregates attention and MoE stages; introduces asynchronous communication primitives and coordinated scheduling/execution optimizations; evaluates on CloudMatrix384 super-nodes and reports 90% higher SLO-compliant prefill throughput versus synchronous serving.
  • Why it matters architecturally: It treats MoE serving as a distributed pipeline and fabric-coordination problem, not merely a GPU kernel or request batching problem.
  • Useful diagram: Figure 1 is the money diagram: it contrasts synchronous barriered execution with the ideal asynchronous pipeline under heterogeneous request batches.
Fresh arXiv CS paper · MoE serving / asynchronous communication / CloudMatrix-style supernodes

ASAP: A Disaggregated and Asynchronous Inference System for MoE Prefill

Authors & affiliations: Weiwei Chen, Shuang Chen, Lele Li, Qiang Hu, Han Li, Xin Ye, Ming Yan, and Zhibin Yu. Affiliations: Huawei.

Tags: LLM Serving, MoE Communication, Scale-Up AI Clusters

Problem: Modern MoE serving often combines DP/TP attention with expert-parallel MoE layers. That saves memory and keeps experts distributed, but it creates strict global synchronization before and after MoE layers; stochastic online prefill workloads then turn DP imbalance into idle time, congested experts, and high TTFT.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes ASAP, an asynchronous MoE prefill system that disaggregates attention and MoE stages instead of forcing all DP groups through rigid per-layer barriers.
  • Introduces specialized asynchronous communication primitives plus four coordinated optimizations across request scheduling and model execution to support fragmented and out-of-order execution.
  • Evaluates on CloudMatrix384 super-nodes and reports a 90% improvement in SLO-compliant prefill throughput over state-of-the-art synchronous serving solutions.

Why it matters: This is directly in the AI-superpod lane: MoE serving performance is being limited by synchronization structure and communication semantics between parallelism domains. The paper says the serving runtime must expose asynchrony to the model/data-flow boundary, not just tune batches inside a lockstep loop.

Key insights:

  • DP imbalance is not an edge case; sequence-length skew, stochastic arrivals, and prefix-cache variation make it a normal operating condition for online prefill.
  • Expert load balancing alone does not solve the barrier problem if attention DP groups still synchronize globally around every MoE layer.
  • Disaggregating attention and MoE creates smaller, fragmented expert batches, so compute efficiency has to be recovered with runtime-level batching and execution coordination.
  • The relevant fabric abstraction is asynchronous stage-to-stage movement with correctness around out-of-order progress, not just faster all-to-all.

Actionable lessons: When designing MoE serving on rack-scale nodes, profile barrier idle time separately from expert imbalance. Treat attention-to-expert traffic as a pipeline with backpressure and out-of-order completion, and test whether the fabric/runtime can preserve accelerator utilization under heavy-tailed prefill lengths.

Fresh arXiv CS paper · cluster simulation / OS support / infrastructure design

LiveStack: OS Support for Cluster-Scale Full-Stack Live Simulation

Authors & affiliations: Yiliang Wan, Haifeng Sun, Yihan Yang, and Jialin Li: National University of Singapore; Jonas Kaufmann and Antoine Kaufmann: Max Planck Institute for Software Systems.

Tags: Cluster Simulation, Operating Systems, AI Infrastructure

Problem: Cluster operators and hardware designers need to evaluate distributed stacks, accelerators, memory systems, and interconnects before deployment, but existing simulators usually trade off full-stack fidelity against speed.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds a live simulation substrate on the Linux virtualization stack, aiming to run unmodified production software while coordinating simulated time.
  • Adds four OS-level mechanisms: simulation-oriented scheduling, live memory hierarchy management, simulation-aware IPC, and distributed simulation orchestration.
  • Reports that a preliminary prototype runs TPC-C in 90.4 seconds while a comparable modular gem5 setup did not finish within a week, with promising physical-behavior fidelity across representative workloads.

Why it matters: AI cluster design needs credible what-if testing for new interconnects, accelerators, memory fabrics, and software stacks. LiveStack is not AI-specific, but the motivation explicitly includes emerging accelerators and interconnects; the core value is making full-stack cluster experiments fast enough to iterate.

Key insights:

  • The paper argues simulation control belongs below the user-space boundary because time advancement, interference control, IPC ordering, and multi-host orchestration all cross OS/runtime layers.
  • KVM/QEMU/libvirt provide near-native execution, but they need simulation-native scheduling and communication semantics to become a cluster-scale simulator.
  • For AI fabrics, this suggests a useful evaluation path between pure analytical models and expensive physical testbeds.
  • The simulator’s hardest problem is not just speed; it is preserving cross-layer behavior while controlling shared-host interference.

Actionable lessons: For proposed AI-cluster topologies or accelerator-memory fabrics, require evaluation plans that can run unmodified software stacks or clearly explain what is modeled away. Simulation time control and interference isolation should be explicit design goals, not benchmarking footnotes.

Fresh arXiv CS paper · in-network computing / synchronization / exascale networks

From the NYU Ultracomputer to Modern Exascale: A Historical and Architectural Survey of In-Network Computing and Scalable Synchronization

Authors & affiliations: Lars Warren Ericson. Affiliations: Catskills Research Company.

Tags: In-Network Computing, Synchronization, Interconnect Architecture

Problem: Massively parallel systems repeatedly hit two bottlenecks: interconnect latency and synchronization hot spots. The paper surveys how hardware combining, message-passing synchronization, and modern in-network computation evolved across decades.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Connects the NYU Ultracomputer and IBM RP3 hardware combining networks to modern in-network computation models such as NVIDIA SHARP and HPE Slingshot.
  • Analyzes synchronization primitives, MPI operation behavior, RMA atomics, PCIe atomics, GPU cache interactions, and the software/hardware boundary in heterogeneous deep-learning systems.
  • Provides historical architecture diagrams for Omega networks, Dragonfly topology, and group-lock lineage to frame synchronization design choices.

Why it matters: It is a survey rather than a new system, but it is directly relevant to collective acceleration and in-network reduction debates. The useful signal is the continuity: today’s SHARP-style reductions are not a one-off trick; they are the latest point in a long design space of putting synchronization semantics into the fabric.

Key insights:

  • Hardware combining is fundamentally about removing hot-spot serialization at memory or synchronization targets, which maps cleanly to collective-reduction pressure in AI training.
  • The old shared-memory versus message-passing split still echoes in modern GPU clusters: where do you place ordering, atomicity, and reduction semantics?
  • In-network computing helps only when the primitive matches the workload’s synchronization structure; otherwise the fabric feature becomes a special-case offload.
  • The paper’s historical lens is useful for avoiding “new magic box” thinking around programmable switches and collective engines.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating in-network collective proposals, ask which synchronization primitive is being accelerated, where ordering is guaranteed, how fallback paths behave, and whether the primitive composes with GPU memory semantics and one-sided operations.

Fresh arXiv CS paper · NCCL / AllReduce / communication-avoiding training

Mixed-Precision Communication-Avoiding SGD for Generalized Linear Models on GPUs

Authors & affiliations: Aditya Devarakonda: Department of Computer Science, Wake Forest University; Irene Simó Muñoz and Giulia Guidi: Department of Computer Science, Cornell University.

Tags: Collective Communication, GPU Training, Mixed Precision

Problem: Distributed SGD can become synchronization-limited because every mini-batch iteration performs an AllReduce. Communication-avoiding SGD reduces the number of synchronization points, but increases local Gram computation and collective payload size.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Gives a finite-precision analysis of communication-avoiding SGD that decomposes one outer iteration into nine independent precision choices.
  • Designs an A100 mixed-precision recipe using BF16 storage and BF16-input/FP32-accumulate tensor-core math for selected operations, while retaining FP32 where error sensitivity requires it.
  • Evaluates on NERSC Perlmutter A100 GPUs and reports matching FP32 SGD loss within 0.5% with 5.1–6.8× speedups over FP32 SGD across several generalized linear model workloads.

Why it matters: This is not LLM training, but it is a clean collective-communication lesson: sometimes the right way to reduce synchronization pressure is to change the algorithmic shape of communication, then use accelerator math to pay for the extra local work.

Key insights:

  • The latency term of many small AllReduces can dominate, so batching synchronization across multiple steps can be more valuable than shrinking a single collective.
  • Mixed precision is not a blanket BF16 switch; the paper identifies where low precision is safe and where FP32 is needed for inner corrections, residuals, weights, and Gram AllReduce.
  • The authors measure NCCL AllReduce constants on Perlmutter, making the communication model more grounded than a purely asymptotic analysis.
  • The idea is workload-limited today, but the pattern is relevant to broader training-system design: trade controlled extra compute for fewer global synchronization points.

Actionable lessons: For distributed training algorithms, separate bandwidth reduction from synchronization-frequency reduction. If collectives are latency-bound, consider algorithmic reformulations that amortize global reductions, then verify numerical stability per operation rather than per datatype.
