AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s strongest signal is transport and memory-path co-design: MLCC uses training phase information inside congestion control, while the HBF/ReRAM papers all say the same thing from the memory side — raw bandwidth only matters if the runtime sends the right traffic over the right path.

Most important paper of the day

MLCC: A Congestion Control Technique to Accelerate ML Training

Authors & affiliations: Anton A. Zabreyko, Sanjoli Narang, Sudarsanan Rajasekaran, and Manya Ghobadi. Affiliations: MIT CSAIL.

Tags: Transport/Protocols, Congestion Control, AI Clusters

Why this is the top pick: MLCC is the most useful paper for Ethan today because it makes congestion control explicitly aware of the periodic compute/communication structure of DNN training. Instead of treating synchronized training flows as ordinary fair-share bulk transfers, it tries to move communication bursts into other jobs’ compute windows.

Deeper explanation: The key mechanism is distributed phase steering. MLCC increases or decreases a flow’s aggressiveness according to how far its job has progressed through the current communication phase, normalized by that job’s maximum communication volume. A job closer to finishing communication can push harder, which nudges competing jobs’ communication into its following compute period. Repeating this over training iterations produces stable interleaving without a centralized scheduler, switch support, priority queues, or hardware changes. The paper’s evaluation is also in the right shape for AI fabrics: iteration-time speedups on a testbed and packet-level simulations on a 36-node, 288-GPU fat-tree.

  • Problem: Fair-share congestion control can preserve synchronized DNN communication bursts, causing recurring contention and GPU idle time.
  • Key contributions: Phase-aware congestion-control augmentation, simulated annealing for escaping unstable schedules, and measured/simulated speedups for shared GPU clusters.
  • Why it matters architecturally: It suggests transport protocols for AI superpods should reason about iteration phase and exposed compute slack, not just per-flow fairness and link utilization.
  • Diagram note: Figure 1 explains interleaving; Figure 5 is the most design-useful view because it shows MLCC sliding jobs toward a stable interleaved state.

MLCC: A Congestion Control Technique to Accelerate ML Training

Authors & affiliations: Anton A. Zabreyko, Sanjoli Narang, Sudarsanan Rajasekaran, and Manya Ghobadi. Affiliations: MIT CSAIL.

Tags: Transport/Protocols, Congestion Control, AI Clusters

Problem: Shared GPU clusters suffer network contention when periodic DNN communication phases collide; conventional fair-share congestion control can keep all jobs synchronized in congestion rather than moving traffic into compute slack.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes MLCC, a distributed congestion-control augmentation that changes sending rates based on communication progress within the current training iteration.
  • Adds a simulated-annealing phase so jobs can escape unstable or suboptimal interleavings and converge over a few iterations.
  • Reports up to 1.9× average and 2.7× P99 training-iteration speedups on a testbed, plus 1.35× training-throughput improvement in packet-level simulation on a 36-node, 288-GPU fat-tree.

Why it matters: This is directly in the AI-cluster transport lane: it treats the collective/training traffic pattern as a first-class congestion-control signal, rather than pretending DNN flows are generic bulk transfers.

Key insights:

  • The core mechanism is phase shifting: let a job closer to finishing communication be more aggressive so other jobs’ communication gets pushed into that job’s compute phase.
  • The design needs no switch changes, priority queues, centralized flow scheduler, or hardware support; the authors state the protocol change is under 60 lines for a given congestion-control scheme.
  • Fair sharing can be actively bad for synchronized DNN jobs because it preserves coincident communication bursts and leaves compute-period network slack unused.
  • The paper separates “compatible” interleavings from more general MLCC-induced schedules, which matters because real cluster jobs rarely have perfectly nestable compute/communication phases.

Actionable lessons: Expose training-iteration phase to transport logic when possible. For superpod transport experiments, compare not just average FCT or link utilization but iteration-time distributions, convergence time to stable interleaving, and behavior under ECMP collisions or failures. MLCC also suggests a useful design principle for RoCE/UEC-like transports: fairness should be workload-phase aware, not purely flow symmetric.

Beyond Capacity: Scalable MoE LLM Inference via High-Bandwidth Flash with Direct GPU and HBM Paths

Authors & affiliations: Seeyeon Kim, Juhyeong Jin, and Joo-Young Kim. Affiliations: KAIST, Daejeon, Republic of Korea.

Tags: LLM Serving, Memory/Fabric Paths, MoE Infrastructure

Problem: MoE expert weights now exceed HBM capacity by a wide margin, but moving HBF-resident expert weights only through HBM leaves a direct GPU–HBF path idle and can create a relay bottleneck.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces DASH, an HBF/HBM organization that uses two concurrent expert-delivery routes: direct HBF→GPU and relay HBF→HBM→GPU.
  • Assigns whole experts to routes and overlaps transfer with early expert determination, while separating immutable expert-weight traffic from mutable KV-cache data.
  • Uses an event-driven continuous-batching serving simulator with measured GPU compute latencies; reports 1.94× higher throughput and 1.90× end-to-end speedup over an HBF-through-HBM-only design for a representative workload.

Why it matters: This is a good counterpoint to yesterday’s HBF warning: the lesson is not “HBF is bad,” but that the wiring and traffic-class split determine whether the extra bandwidth is actually reachable.

Key insights:

  • The paper frames MoE expert weights and KV cache as different traffic classes: weights are immutable and mostly read-streamed, while KV is mutable and request-driven.
  • Whole-expert route assignment avoids fine-grained replication while still exploiting independent bandwidth paths.
  • Early expert determination is essentially communication/computation overlap for sparse expert fetch; it moves expert selection earlier so HBF latency hides behind preceding work.
  • The design assumes enough GPU/package path diversity to make direct and relay transfers concurrent; if the two routes share a bottleneck, the gain would collapse.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating new memory tiers, draw the actual data paths and shared bottlenecks. Separate expert-weight movement from KV-cache movement in counters and schedulers; a single “offload bandwidth” number hides whether a tier is serving immutable reads, mutable writes, or both.

MoE Expert Execution in Disaggregated LLM Serving with a High-Bandwidth ReRAM Near-Memory Architecture

Authors & affiliations: Kunming Shao, Ming Zeng, Xin Yuan, Binbin Liao, Yangming Zhang, Wei Wang, Tim Kwang-Ting Cheng, and Chi-Ying Tsui. Affiliations: The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Hong Kong SAR, China; Alibaba Cloud Computing, China.

Tags: Disaggregated Serving, Near-Memory Computing, MoE Load Balancing

Problem: Attention–FFN disaggregation can place MoE expert weights in a specialized FFN pool, but decode SLOs cap batch size while sparse routing expands the activated-expert union and creates hot/cold expert skew.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents ReXpert, a ReRAM near-memory architecture for resident MoE FFN execution with bounded core-local multicast pooling.
  • Factors actual MFU into ideal MFU and occupancy, then improves occupancy via coactivation-aware placement and load-aware fetch.
  • Reports side-4 pooling raising occupancy from 0.328 to 0.519; at iso-peak compute, the FFN pool is modeled as 9.5× faster than H20 with 20× lower weight-movement energy, and an H20-attention + ReXpert-FFN system lowers decode TPOT across tested models.

Why it matters: The interesting part is the “after residency” problem: once expert weights are local, the bottleneck shifts to bandwidth density and skew, so placement and bounded sharing become the architecture levers.

Key insights:

  • Sparse MoE routing makes batching less effective than dense FFN intuition suggests: the activated expert union grows quickly, so weight traffic does not amortize linearly with batch.
  • Global sharing would be expensive; the paper instead uses physically bounded multicast pooling to recover occupancy locally.
  • The architecture keeps routing fixed and absorbs skew inside the FFN pool, which is a useful separation of concerns for disaggregated serving.
  • Sizing each communication level from induced demand is the right discipline; near-memory designs can quietly create new internal network hot spots.

Actionable lessons: For attention/FFN-disaggregated designs, model expert-union growth and skew under SLO-limited decode batches. Do not stop at “weights fit near compute”; include occupancy, multicast radius, placement, reduction order, and induced on-die traffic in the design budget.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 shows the attention/FFN-disaggregated serving split. Figure 4 is useful for understanding sorted expert load and how skew drives placement and pooling decisions.

Exploring High-Bandwidth Flash for Modern LLM Inference: Opportunities and Challenges

Authors & affiliations: Dowon Son, Yonggon Park, Hyunuk Cho, Hyungkyu Ham, Onur Mutlu, Sungjin Lee, Gwangsun Kim, and Jisung Park. Affiliations: POSTECH, Pohang-si, Gyeongsangbuk-do, Republic of Korea; ETH Zürich, Zurich, Switzerland.

Tags: LLM Serving, High-Bandwidth Flash, Memory Hierarchy

Problem: HBF is being proposed as a main GPU-memory component for LLM inference, but its asymmetric read/write behavior, endurance, and effect on batch size and TPOT need system-level analysis.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Analyzes HBF-based serving across dense and MoE decoder models, diverse memory configurations, and operating scenarios.
  • Shows HBF can improve per-GPU batch size, throughput, and deployment flexibility while reducing minimum GPU requirements when read bandwidth is high enough.
  • Identifies write performance and endurance as critical blockers, especially because KV-cache updates make HBF a read/write serving component rather than a pure weight store.

Why it matters: This is the survey-style complement to the more specific DASH paper: it maps the conditions under which HBF helps instead of assuming package-local flash is automatically a win.

Key insights:

  • HBF’s value is strongest when it can sustain HBM-comparable read bandwidth for model weights while allowing larger batches under TPOT constraints.
  • KV-cache writes are not a footnote; Figure 5’s runtime breakdown shows write-side cost can become visible under tight TPOT SLOs.
  • The paper’s endurance metric, PEC, turns serving traffic into a device-lifetime constraint, which is easy to ignore in accelerator-centric designs.
  • For some configurations, HBF changes the minimum viable GPU count/capacity point, not just raw tokens/s.

Actionable lessons: Treat HBF as a full memory-system candidate with read bandwidth, write bandwidth, endurance, thermals, and capacity displacement. For architecture studies, include TPOT-constrained batch size and per-tier write amplification; otherwise HBF looks better on paper than in a serving loop.

Robust Constraint-Aware Bayesian Tuning of BBRv2 for QUIC under Tactile Internet Constraints

Authors & affiliations: Muhammad Hanif Lashari, Shakil Ahmed, Wafa Batayneh, and Ashfaq Khokhar. Affiliations: Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Iowa State University, Iowa, USA; Department of Computer Science, College of Computing, Grand Valley State University, Michigan, USA.

Tags: Transport/Protocols, QUIC, Congestion Control

Problem: BBRv2 parameters can strongly affect tail latency, jitter, loss, and goodput, but the mapping from knobs to behavior is an expensive black-box function that varies by network scenario.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Formulates BBRv2 tuning for QUIC as robust constraint-aware black-box optimization over multiple emulated network profiles.
  • Uses Bayesian optimization with a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator to search bounded BBRv2 parameter space under noisy measurements.
  • Evaluates tuned configurations against standard QUIC congestion-control baselines using throughput, tail latency, loss, and jitter-oriented metrics.

Why it matters: This is not AI-cluster-specific, so it is the lowest-priority pick today. Still, it is relevant as a transport-methodology paper: cluster transports also face multi-objective tuning where tail latency and loss matter more than average goodput alone.

Key insights:

  • The useful idea is constraint-aware tuning: preserve throughput while explicitly bounding latency/loss rather than collapsing everything into one average-performance objective.
  • Scenario-set optimization is a guardrail against overfitting one emulated path, which maps well to fabric profiles with different RTT, queue, and loss regimes.
  • The work reinforces that BBR-family behavior is implementation- and parameter-sensitive; default knobs should not be treated as universal.

Actionable lessons: For datacenter transport experiments, use multi-scenario black-box tuning only after defining hard constraints that match the workload: P99/P999 iteration delay, loss recovery cost, ECN/queue stability, and coexistence. Do not let a tuner “win” by trading away tail behavior for goodput.

Useful diagram: No obvious architecture/system diagram was found in the inspected first pages; the value is mainly in the optimization formulation and evaluation methodology.
