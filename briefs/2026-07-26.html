STORM: RDMA-based Monte Carlo Transport Scheme for Distributed-Memory Particle Simulations

Authors & affiliations: Maor Mizrachi, Barak Raveh, and Elad Steinberg. Affiliations: School of Computer Science and Engineering, The Hebrew University, Jerusalem; Racah Institute of Physics, The Hebrew University, Jerusalem.

Tags: RDMA, One-sided Communication, Distributed Systems

Why this is the top pick: This is today’s strongest systems signal even though the application is computational astrophysics rather than AI training: it is a clean, measured example of replacing matched two-sided messaging with one-sided RDMA to remove CPU progress overhead in a highly irregular distributed workload.

Deeper explanation: STORM is valuable because it isolates a transport mechanism Ethan cares about—one-sided RDMA—and shows exactly where it wins: irregular, many-peer communication where receiver-side MPI progress becomes a scaling tax. The application is particle transport, not LLM training, so I would not over-claim direct MoE speedups. But the mechanism maps cleanly to AI infrastructure questions: when should a rank/GPU/NIC be able to deposit work remotely without waking the receiver, and how much of the communication layer can be made passive, lock-free, and topology-independent?

  • Problem: Large distributed Monte Carlo particle transport codes hit a communication-scaling wall because two-sided MPI requires receive posting and progress polling across many irregular rank partners.
  • Key contributions: Introduces STORM, an open-source one-sided RDMA communication layer for Monte Carlo transport on general meshes, physics models, and boundary conditions.; Uses per-rank-pair single-producer/single-consumer ring buffers so RDMA writes can transfer particles directly into remote memory while receivers remain passive.; Reports more than 97% weak-scaling and more than 88% strong-scaling efficiency up to 13,440 cores, plus 1.14–1.27× speedups over the two-sided backend and 1.41× in a Hohlraum IMC benchmark.
  • Why it matters architecturally: For superpod fabrics, CPU progress overhead and receiver polling can quietly become latency and scalability killers. STORM is a concrete design point for avoiding that class of overhead with a one-sided write path.

STORM: RDMA-based Monte Carlo Transport Scheme for Distributed-Memory Particle Simulations

Authors & affiliations: Maor Mizrachi, Barak Raveh, and Elad Steinberg. Affiliations: School of Computer Science and Engineering, The Hebrew University, Jerusalem; Racah Institute of Physics, The Hebrew University, Jerusalem.

Tags: RDMA, One-sided Communication, Distributed Systems

Problem: Large distributed Monte Carlo particle transport codes hit a communication-scaling wall because two-sided MPI requires receive posting and progress polling across many irregular rank partners.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces STORM, an open-source one-sided RDMA communication layer for Monte Carlo transport on general meshes, physics models, and boundary conditions.
  • Uses per-rank-pair single-producer/single-consumer ring buffers so RDMA writes can transfer particles directly into remote memory while receivers remain passive.
  • Reports more than 97% weak-scaling and more than 88% strong-scaling efficiency up to 13,440 cores, plus 1.14–1.27× speedups over the two-sided backend and 1.41× in a Hohlraum IMC benchmark.

Why it matters: This is today’s strongest systems signal even though the application is computational astrophysics rather than AI training: it is a clean, measured example of replacing matched two-sided messaging with one-sided RDMA to remove CPU progress overhead in a highly irregular distributed workload.

Key insights:

  • The useful abstraction is not “RDMA is faster”; it is “remote writes make the receiver passive,” which cuts polling/progress work when traffic is sparse, irregular, and many-to-many.
  • The ring-buffer design is deliberately mesh- and physics-independent, which is exactly the kind of separation AI communication libraries also need when payload semantics and transport mechanics evolve at different speeds.
  • The reported benefit appears when MPI progress overhead dominates; for bulk bandwidth-saturated collectives, the same design would need a different bottleneck analysis.
  • The fallback two-sided backend is architecturally important: portability is preserved while RDMA becomes the fast path where the fabric supports it.

Actionable lessons: For AI cluster transport work, treat CPU progress and receiver-side polling as first-class scaling costs, not incidental implementation noise. One-sided rings are worth considering for irregular expert/token movement, sparse activation exchange, or telemetry/event streams where the receiver should not spin on every peer.

Codec-Gauge: Learning Compression-Friendly Gauges for Transformer KV Caches

Authors & affiliations: Yitao Jiang, Yaoqing Yang, Luyang Zhao, Muhao Chen, and Devin Balkcom. Affiliations: Department of Computer Science, Dartmouth College; Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Clemson University; Department of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering, University of Houston.

Tags: KV Cache, LLM Serving, Compression

Problem: Long-context inference is increasingly limited by KV-cache storage and movement, but cache compression/quantization quality depends on the coordinate basis presented to the backend codec.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces Codec-Gauge, a post-training cache-coordinate layer that learns small orthogonal channel transforms around existing compression or quantization backends.
  • Optimizes a frequency-distribution objective combining token-channel DCT spectral-centroid loss with a smooth rate proxy to concentrate KV energy into codec-friendly layouts.
  • Across six models and 3/4/6 bits per value, reduces zfp KL divergence by 44.0% on average versus raw coordinates and also improves block-uniform and KIVI-style quantization.

Why it matters: For disaggregated or long-context serving, KV is not just a memory problem; it becomes a bandwidth and placement problem. This paper attacks the byte/fidelity trade-off without changing model weights or attention semantics, which makes it potentially composable with serving-system work.

Key insights:

  • The core idea is that KV vectors have a “gauge freedom”: an orthogonal basis change can make the same semantic cache state friendlier to a fixed codec.
  • Because the learned transform wraps the backend rather than replacing it, it can improve multiple codecs/quantizers instead of becoming another bespoke compression format.
  • The paper measures actual compressed bytes and compressed-history scoring, which is more useful for systems design than reporting only reconstruction error.
  • For networked KV movement, a better cache coordinate system could reduce fabric pressure while preserving output distributions, especially when caches cross GPU, CPU, SSD, or remote-memory tiers.

Actionable lessons: If you are designing KV transfer paths, do not treat compression as an isolated codec choice. Benchmark coordinate transforms, quantization, and placement together, because the “shape” of cache tensors affects both fidelity and bytes-on-the-wire.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 gives the clearest architecture view: a one-time gauge-training stage learns an orthogonal matrix from frozen-model KV tensors, then inference applies the gauge before compression and the inverse gauge after decompression.

Dropping the Anchor: Statistical Context Summarization for Distributed Systems via Pulsar Attention

Authors & affiliations: Aryan Sood and Shantanu Acharya. Affiliations: Department of Electrical Engineering, Indian Institute of Technology Roorkee; NVIDIA.

Tags: Distributed LLM Inference, Long Context, KV Sharding

Problem: Distributed blockwise long-context methods such as Star Attention shard context across hosts, but duplicate a static anchor block on every host, increasing Phase-1 FLOPs and carrying no content-specific information from intermediate blocks.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Replaces the static anchor with a small attention-sink prefix plus content-aware cross-block summaries selected with a Max-IDF rare-token heuristic.
  • Cuts Phase-1 per-GPU FLOPs by up to 3.3× over Star Attention while retaining the same KV-cache footprint.
  • Reports better RULER/BABILong results than Star Attention and dense attention up to 128K context, including up to 4.7 percentage-point gains over dense attention.

Why it matters: This is relevant because long-context serving keeps pushing KV cache and attention work across devices. The paper is not a network protocol, but it changes the distributed-inference communication/computation contract: less replicated context work, same cache footprint, and more useful cross-block summaries.

Key insights:

  • The anchor block in Star Attention is a systems convenience: it stabilizes softmax, but it is content-blind and replicated everywhere. Pulsar splits stabilization from information transfer.
  • The Max-IDF summary is cheap and causal, so it gives each shard a compressed view of prior blocks without layer-by-layer ring communication.
  • This is a reminder that distributed LLM inference can reduce fabric pressure by changing what state is worth sharing, not only by making the network faster.
  • The evaluation is at 128K context, so the million-token regime still needs caution; summary quality and cache placement may become more workload-dependent as contexts stretch further.

Actionable lessons: For distributed long-context systems, separate three concerns explicitly: softmax stabilization, cross-block information propagation, and KV storage. A static replicated prefix is simple, but workload-aware summaries may give a better compute/network trade-off.

PagedWeight: Efficient MoE LLM Serving with Dynamic Quality-Aware Weight Quantization

Authors & affiliations: Yuchen Yang, Yifan Zhao, Anisha Dasgupta, and Sasa Misailovic. Affiliations: University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign.

Tags: MoE Serving, GPU Memory, KV Cache

Problem: MoE serving has a three-way memory trade-off: expert weights occupy large GPU memory, KV cache grows with context and batch, and static quantization cannot adapt when runtime cache pressure changes.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces PagedWeight, a runtime management method that dynamically quantizes MoE weights to free GPU memory for growing KV caches.
  • Uses quality-aware expert/block selection to lower precision where the expected output-quality impact is smallest.
  • Reports FP16-equivalent accuracy with up to 72.0% GPU memory savings and 1.94× throughput improvement, and up to 39.3% quality improvement over quantization baselines at similar memory budgets.

Why it matters: This is an AI-infrastructure paper rather than a networking paper, but it matters for heterogeneous serving architecture: reducing resident expert-weight pressure changes how much KV can stay local versus spill to CPU, SSD, CXL, or remote memory.

Key insights:

  • PagedWeight treats MoE expert weights like pageable runtime objects, not fixed model baggage. That is a useful mental shift for serving systems where active experts and context sizes vary per request.
  • The important systems loop is dynamic: as KV grows, the runtime trades expert precision for cache headroom instead of simply evicting KV or failing admission.
  • The asynchronous page-movement pipeline is critical; without overlap, memory savings could easily become latency spikes.
  • This interacts with disaggregated serving: if local GPU memory can be reshaped dynamically, the threshold for remote KV transfer or weight offload changes.

Actionable lessons: When modeling MoE serving capacity, include expert-weight residency and KV cache growth in the same allocator/control loop. Static quantization, KV paging, and remote-memory offload should be compared under one memory budget instead of as separate tricks.
