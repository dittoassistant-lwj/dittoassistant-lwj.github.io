New arXiv CS submission · AI training networks / topology synthesis / all-to-all throughput

Throughput-Optimized Networks at Scale

Authors & affiliations: Conor James Green and Mithuna Thottethodi (Elmore Family School of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Purdue University)

Tags: AI cluster networks, Topology synthesis, Collectives/routing

Problem: AI training fabrics need high all-to-all and random-traffic throughput at thousands of accelerators, but hand-designed torus-style TPU pod topologies can leave large aggregate throughput unused under physical, routing, and OCS-fault constraints.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces TONS, an automated network-synthesis framework that formulates topology generation as a scalable linear-optimization problem over a throughput-centric proxy metric.
  • Targets Google TPU v4/v5p-style direct interconnects and optically reconfigurable links, with constraints that reflect implementability rather than only graph-theory cleanliness.
  • Adds a deadlock-free routing scheme compatible with limited virtual channels and optical switch faults, then reports 2.1× geomean speedup for uniform random traffic and 1.6× for all-to-all over the best TPU v4/v5p torus variants in simulation.

Why it matters: This is the strongest signal today because it treats AI-cluster networking as a joint topology + routing + collective-throughput problem, not as “add more bandwidth and hope NCCL/CCL fixes it.” It is directly in Ethan’s wheelhouse: superpod-scale all-to-all, OCS-like reconfiguration, deadlock-free routing, and fabric design under real deployment constraints.

Key insights:

  • The paper uses maximum-concurrent-flow-style reasoning as a throughput proxy, which is a better mental model for collective-heavy fabrics than diameter or bisection bandwidth alone.
  • Topology is still a hard limiter even when collective libraries improve; no schedule can escape insufficient cut capacity, poor path diversity, or load concentration.
  • Reconfigurable optical links are most useful when the control plane can instantiate richer topologies than regular/twisted tori, not merely partition or switch among a few canned layouts.
  • Fault-aware routing is not an afterthought: TONS explicitly considers optical switch faults and limited virtual channels, which is the kind of gritty constraint that often kills elegant fabric designs.

Actionable lessons: For AI-fabric design, evaluate topology, routing, and collective schedules together. Use traffic patterns that include all-to-all and trace-driven collective mixes, and score designs by realized throughput under routing constraints and failures — not only by nominal link count or bisection bandwidth.

Figures 5–8 are also worth scanning because they connect the synthesized topology to saturation throughput, collective schedules, trace-driven simulation, and OCS fault sensitivity.

Updated arXiv CS paper · MoE all-to-all / communication-computation overlap / expert parallelism

FarSkip-Collective: Unhobbling Blocking Communication in Mixture of Experts Models

Authors & affiliations: Yonatan Dukler, Guihong Li, Deval Shah, Jiang Liu, Vikram Appia, and Emad Barsoum (Advanced Micro Devices Inc. (AMD))

Tags: MoE communication, Collective overlap, LLM training/inference

Problem: Distributed MoE models expose blocking all-to-all communication in training and inference. Existing overlap tricks are limited because the next compute step often waits on communicated activations, especially under expert parallelism.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes FarSkip-Collective, a model-architecture modification that lets computation proceed using available activations while collective communication runs in parallel, then reconnects the communicated result through later residual paths.
  • Shows converted 16B–109B MoE models can retain comparable accuracy after self-distillation, including a Llama 4 Scout 109B conversion within about 1% average accuracy of the instruction-tuned release across evaluated tasks.
  • Reports systems gains from optimized implementations: 32.6% TTFT speedup for DeepSeek-V3-style serving in SGLang, 97.3% prefill communication-computation overlap, and 88.9% all-to-all overlap during DeepSeek-V3 MoE-layer pretraining.

Why it matters: This paper is not a transport design, but it attacks one of the most expensive network-facing phases in MoE systems: expert-parallel all-to-all. The interesting move is architectural — change the dependency graph so the fabric latency is hidden instead of merely optimized.

Key insights:

  • FarSkip turns “communication is on the critical path” into “communication can arrive through a later residual,” which is a model/runtime co-design lever rather than a pure CCL optimization.
  • The accuracy question is central: overlapping collectives by changing connectivity is only useful if distillation can recover model quality at frontier-ish scales.
  • For serving, TTFT benefits more than decode because prefill has larger parallel compute windows in which all-to-all can be hidden.
  • This kind of technique changes what the network must deliver: the fabric still needs bandwidth, but exposed latency tolerance improves if the model can absorb delayed collective outputs.

Actionable lessons: When profiling MoE systems, separate raw all-to-all duration from exposed all-to-all stall. Architecture/runtime changes that increase the overlap window can be as valuable as faster NICs or better routing, but they must be validated against model quality and dependency correctness.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 cleanly explains the connectivity change that avoids waiting for collectives. Figure 2 shows the FarSkip MoE block, and Figures 12–14 provide useful execution timelines showing how communication is overlapped in inference and training.

Recent arXiv CS paper · MoE serving / PIM scheduling / inter-device communication

Sieve: Dynamic Expert-Aware PIM Acceleration for Evolving Mixture-of-Experts Models

Authors & affiliations: Jungwoo Kim, Rubens Lacouture, Genghan Zhang, Gina Sohn, Qizheng Zhang, Swapnil Gandhi, Christos Kozyrakis, and Kunle Olukotun (Stanford University; NVIDIA affiliation also listed for Christos Kozyrakis)

Tags: MoE serving, HBM-PIM, Heterogeneous acceleration

Problem: Modern MoE inference creates highly skewed token-to-expert distributions. Static PIM offload policies miss the combined effects of expert load imbalance, GPU/PIM arithmetic intensity, and intra-/inter-device communication overhead.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Identifies a trend toward increasingly bimodal expert distributions in modern MoE models, where a few experts receive many tokens while many experts receive one or a few.
  • Builds a runtime scheduler that partitions expert execution between GPUs and attached HBM-PIM stacks using runtime token distribution, GPU/PIM throughput, memory bandwidth, and interconnect overhead.
  • Presents Sieve, a runtime framework that coordinates GPU compute, PIM compute, and communication while preserving expert-parallel dependencies; reports 1.3×–1.6× throughput/interactivity improvement over prior PIM-style MoE systems in cycle-accurate simulation.

Why it matters: This is a useful heterogeneous-infrastructure paper because it treats MoE serving as a placement and data-movement problem across GPU, PIM, memory bandwidth, and interconnects. The network angle is inside the scheduler: moving expert work is only profitable if communication overhead does not eat the win.

Key insights:

  • Expert popularity changes arithmetic intensity: hot experts look more GEMM-like, while tail experts can be memory-bound GEMV/skinny-GEMM cases that PIM may handle better.
  • Static “offload all memory-bound work” rules are brittle for MoE because token routing changes across requests and models.
  • The right scheduler objective is cross-device: GPU throughput, PIM throughput, memory bandwidth, and interconnect overhead must be optimized together.
  • PIM does not remove communication; it changes where communication and dependency management show up in the serving pipeline.

Actionable lessons: For heterogeneous AI serving, make runtime placement decisions from live token/expert distributions, not from model-level averages. Any GPU+PIM/NPU+memory-tier design should include explicit interconnect-cost terms in the scheduler, otherwise “near-memory” execution can silently become remote-data movement.

Updated arXiv CS paper · distributed GPU data systems / RDMA / NVMe storage

PystachIO: Efficient Distributed GPU Query Processing with PyTorch over Fast Networks & Fast Storage

Authors & affiliations: Jigao Luo, Nils Boeschen, Muhammad El-Hindi, and Carsten Binnig (TU Darmstadt; TU Darmstadt & hessian.AI; TU Munich; TU Darmstadt & hessian.AI & DFKI Darmstadt)

Tags: GPU-centric data systems, RDMA/storage paths, Compute-I/O overlap

Problem: GPU-centric datacenters now combine fast RDMA networks with high-bandwidth NVMe storage, but naive tensor-runtime query processing underutilizes GPU, NIC, and storage bandwidth because computation and data movement are not overlapped well.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents PystachIO, a PyTorch-based distributed OLAP prototype that uses tensor-computation runtime abstractions for network and storage I/O.
  • Studies how PyTorch collectives, GPU storage reads, distributed joins, dynamic buffering, and query execution interact when the working set is larger than aggregate GPU memory.
  • Reports up to 3× end-to-end speedups over existing distributed GPU query-processing approaches by improving overlap and utilization across GPU, network, and storage paths.

Why it matters: This is adjacent rather than AI-training-specific, but it is relevant to heterogeneous AI infrastructure: the same GPU + RDMA + NVMe pipeline shows up in checkpointing, data loading, feature stores, retrieval systems, and storage-backed inference paths. The core lesson is that fast devices do not compose automatically.

Key insights:

  • Tensor runtimes can expose fast collectives and storage primitives, but naive use leaves bandwidth idle because operator boundaries serialize movement and compute.
  • Network, storage, and GPU overlap must be designed at the query/runtime level; local microbenchmarks can overstate realized end-to-end throughput.
  • NIC bandwidth timelines in the paper are useful because they reveal bursty underutilization that aggregate query runtime alone would hide.
  • The system framing is a good reminder for AI clusters: storage-resident data paths can become part of the accelerator fabric’s performance envelope.

Actionable lessons: For AI infrastructure pipelines that use GPU-direct storage, RDMA, or distributed cache movement, profile the full timeline: GPU kernels, NIC utilization, storage bus bandwidth, buffering, and synchronization. Optimize for sustained overlap, not peak microbenchmark bandwidth.

Useful diagram: Figure 2 shows the PystachIO architecture. Figures 3 and 6 are especially useful for Ethan-style performance analysis because they show fast networking/storage primitives and NIC bandwidth timelines during distributed joins.

Fast take

The common theme is hiding or removing exposed data-movement cost: synthesize better fabric topologies, reshape MoE dependencies to overlap all-to-all, place experts dynamically across GPU and PIM, and keep GPU/network/storage pipelines busy end to end.
