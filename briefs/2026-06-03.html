Most important paper of the day

NetKV: Network-Aware Decode Instance Selection for Disaggregated LLM Inference

Authors & affiliations: Mubarak Adetunji Ojewale. Affiliations: not clearly listed in the arXiv metadata/PDF.

Tags: Disaggregated LLM serving, Network-aware scheduling, KV-cache transfer

Why this is the top pick: This is the most Ethan-shaped paper in today’s batch because it treats the datacenter network as a first-class input to disaggregated inference scheduling. The core claim is simple but important: once prefill and decode are split, the KV cache has to traverse the fabric, so decode placement is not just a GPU-load or prefix-locality problem anymore.

Deeper explanation: NetKV introduces a network cost oracle that exposes topology tier, effective bandwidth, latency, self-contention, and dynamic congestion to the decode-instance selector. The scheduler then estimates transfer, queueing, and decode cost for each candidate. That makes the policy compatible with existing engines and transports while still accounting for fabric distance. The paper’s most useful mental model is that cache-aware scheduling can be arbitrarily suboptimal as context length grows if the chosen decode target sits across an oversubscribed or congested path.

  • Problem: Disaggregated LLM inference pushes large KV caches from prefill instances to decode instances, but production schedulers often route by compute load and prefix locality while ignoring topology distance and congestion.
  • Key contributions: Formalizes decode-instance selection over compute load, prefix-cache locality, and network transfer cost; proposes an O(|D|) greedy scheduler using a network cost oracle with robust tier rankings under stale telemetry; evaluates on a 64-GPU four-tier fat-tree simulator driven by Mooncake traces, reporting up to 21.2% lower mean TTFT versus round-robin and 17.6% versus a tuned cache+load-aware scheduler.
  • Why it matters architecturally: Long-context serving turns KV movement into a fabric-level latency budget item. If routing ignores topology and congestion, “cache locality” can accidentally choose a path that is slower than recomputation or a less-local decode target.
  • Useful diagram: Figure 1 is useful: it visualizes NetKV’s topology/congestion sweep and shows mean-TTFT reduction over the cache+load-aware baseline across oversubscription and background-traffic regimes. Table I is also useful because it positions NetKV against DistServe, Mooncake, llm-d/Dynamo, and topology-aware systems.
New arXiv CS submission · disaggregated inference / network-aware KV transfer / scheduler oracle

NetKV: Network-Aware Decode Instance Selection for Disaggregated LLM Inference

Authors & affiliations: Mubarak Adetunji Ojewale. Affiliations: not clearly listed in the arXiv metadata/PDF.

Tags: Disaggregated LLM serving, Network-aware scheduling, KV-cache transfer

Problem: Disaggregated LLM inference forces KV-cache transfer over the datacenter fabric before decoding starts. Existing routing policies usually consider GPU load and prefix locality, but not fabric tier, bandwidth, latency, or congestion between the prefill and decode instances.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Models decode-instance selection as an online optimization over compute load, cache locality, and network transfer cost, and proves that ignoring the network term can make cache-aware-only scheduling arbitrarily suboptimal as context length grows.
  • Introduces a thin network cost oracle and an O(|D|) per-request greedy scheduler whose tier rankings are robust to stale telemetry.
  • Evaluates on a 64-GPU four-tier fat-tree simulator with Mooncake traces, showing up to 21.2% mean-TTFT reduction over round-robin, 17.6% over cache+load-aware scheduling, and up to 20.1 percentage-point SLO improvement.

Why it matters: This directly connects LLM serving placement with fabric behavior. It says the scheduler should ask “how expensive is this KV movement on today’s path?” rather than assuming a decode GPU with the right prefix is automatically the right target.

Key insights:

  • KV transfer time enters TTFT directly; for long-context requests it can dominate the user-visible latency budget.
  • Prefix locality and network locality are separable signals. A cache hit across a bad tier can lose to a less-local option on a better path.
  • The paper’s oracle abstraction is intentionally modest: it does not require transport or hardware changes, only a scheduler-visible network cost signal.
  • Stale telemetry is less fatal than it sounds when candidate paths are grouped by stable topology tiers; this is a practical point for production control planes.

Actionable lessons: Treat KV placement/routing as a networked systems problem. A serving stack should expose topology tier, current path congestion, candidate decode load, cache-hit size, and expected KV bytes to the same decision point.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 shows NetKV’s performance over topology oversubscription and background-traffic sweeps; it is the clearest visual of when the network-aware term matters most.

New arXiv CS submission · RDMA / programmable switches / in-network scheduling

Rain: RDMA-assisted In-Network Scheduling for Microsecond-scale Workloads

Authors & affiliations: Zhihuang Ma, Xingming Cui, Xiaoliang Chen, and Zuqing Zhu (University of Science and Technology of China, Hefei, China).

Tags: RDMA, Programmable switches, Datacenter scheduling

Problem: Microsecond-scale datacenter services need both high throughput and tight tail latency. Software schedulers add overhead, while prior in-network schedulers often lack global coordination, rely heavily on packet recirculation, or cannot handle large tasks cleanly.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes Rain, an RDMA-assisted scheduler built on programmable switches with centralized switch-side queues and bounded worker-local queues.
  • Uses bidirectional on-switch queuing to match tasks with worker-issued tokens, reducing worker-side polling and approximating join-bounded-shortest-queue behavior without global aggregation.
  • Adds a switch-driven RDMA engine that pre-writes large tasks to worker memory with one-sided RDMA WRITE multicast, keeping compact metadata on the switch; on RocksDB, reports 1.75× higher throughput than the best SOTA under the same tail-latency requirement.

Why it matters: This is not an AI-cluster paper per se, but the mechanism is highly relevant to SmartNIC/DPU/switch-offload thinking: put just enough queueing and task metadata in the network to remove host polling and reduce tail latency, while using RDMA for payload movement.

Key insights:

  • The switch stores control metadata and matching state, not arbitrary large tasks; RDMA handles the large-payload path.
  • Bidirectional queues let workers advertise capacity with tokens while tasks wait in the switch, which bounds local queues without requiring a global software coordinator.
  • The paper finds a useful real-system caveat: shallower worker queues do not always improve tail latency, so Rain adapts worker queue depths and slice mappings at runtime.
  • Slice-aware scheduling reduces dispersion-induced head-of-line blocking by grouping more homogeneous task classes.

Actionable lessons: For AI data planes, separate metadata scheduling from payload movement. Programmable-switch or DPU designs should avoid storing bulk tensors in-network, but can still coordinate capacity tokens, placement decisions, and RDMA writes.

New arXiv CS submission · tensor parallelism / LLM inference runtime / cross-GPU communication

Scaling LLM Inference Beyond Amdahl`s Limits via Eliminating Non-Scalable Overheads

Authors & affiliations: Alan Zhao and Cyril Y. He (scitix); Wei Xu (Tsinghua University).

Tags: LLM serving, Tensor parallelism, GPU runtime overheads

Problem: Tensor parallelism is needed to fit large models and KV caches, but increasing TP degree introduces cross-GPU communication and non-scalable runtime work. That creates an empirical sweet spot where higher TP helps memory pressure but hurts per-token scaling.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Profiles LLM inference scalability and identifies non-scalable overheads in scheduling, I/O, and sampling in addition to cross-GPU communication.
  • Builds Albireo, an inference engine that overlaps scheduling and I/O with compute and uses sequence-parallel sampling to shrink the serial portion.
  • Reports up to 1.9× higher throughput, 48% lower latency, and 28% higher GPU utilization than vLLM across models/benchmarks, plus up to 2× production throughput improvement.

Why it matters: This paper is useful because it separates “TP is slow because communication” from the broader runtime serial fraction. For cluster architects, it is a reminder that adding bandwidth is not enough if the serving engine still serializes scheduling, I/O, or sampling around every decode step.

Key insights:

  • The optimal TP degree depends on both communication overhead and memory pressure from weights plus KV cache; the best point can move with model size and workload.
  • Non-scalable host/runtime work can cap cluster-wide throughput even when GPU compute and interconnect are healthy.
  • Sequence-parallel sampling is interesting because it attacks a post-compute serial stage that is easy to ignore in network-centric analysis.
  • The architecture emphasizes pipeline overlap rather than changing the model, which makes the idea more deployable.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating TP scale-up designs, profile the whole decode loop: network collectives, scheduler path, input/output processing, sampling, and GPU utilization. A fabric upgrade only pays off if the runtime can keep the serial fraction small.

New arXiv CS submission · heterogeneous devices / model-parallel replicas / edge-fog LLM serving

E2LLM: Towards Efficient LLM Serving in Heterogeneous Edge/Fog Environments

Authors & affiliations: Truong-Thanh Le, Amir Taherkordi, Hoang-Loc La, Frank Eliassen, Phuong Hoai Ha, and Peiyuan Guan. Affiliations: not clearly listed in the arXiv metadata/PDF.

Tags: Heterogeneous infrastructure, LLM serving, Model parallelism

Problem: Edge/fog deployments often cannot place a whole LLM on one device. Naively splitting a model across all available devices ignores heterogeneous compute, memory, and inter-device bandwidth, and can create bottleneck stages.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Replicates the full model across multiple device groups, then applies model parallelism within each replica instead of building one monolithic partition.
  • Assigns replicas specialized PREFILL or DECODER roles based on their efficiency for input-token versus output-token work.
  • Uses a genetic algorithm for cluster formation and dynamic programming for per-cluster pipeline partitioning; reports over 50% lower average waiting time than Splitwise under high demand.

Why it matters: The paper is edge/fog-oriented, but the design issue generalizes to heterogeneous AI infrastructure: replica formation must account for compute speed, memory capacity, and bandwidth together, not just count accelerators.

Key insights:

  • Prefill and decode are different workloads, so heterogeneous replicas can be role-specialized rather than treated as fungible serving workers.
  • The DP partitioner explicitly takes profiled layer latency, memory constraints, and inter-device bandwidth as inputs, which is the right shape for heterogeneous pods.
  • Cluster formation is a combinatorial placement problem; a greedy “fastest devices together” rule can miss better bottleneck-balanced groupings.
  • The evaluation uses very mixed hardware, including RTX-class GPUs, Apple M-series devices, and Jetson AGX Orin, making the heterogeneity concrete.

Actionable lessons: For heterogeneous serving, maintain device profiles that include compute, memory, and link bandwidth, then form prefill/decode-specialized replicas rather than assuming uniform workers. This is especially relevant when NPUs, GPUs, CPUs, and memory-rich devices coexist.

New arXiv CS submission · KV-cache management / GPU attention kernels / agent serving

Multi-Segment Attention: Enabling Efficient KV-Cache Management for Faster Large Language Model Serving

Authors & affiliations: Chunan Shi, Yilei Chen, Yilin Chen, Xupeng Miao, and Bin Cui (Peking University, Beijing, China).

Tags: KV-cache systems, LLM serving, GPU kernels

Problem: Lossless KV-cache systems can evict blocks from GPU memory and reconstruct them on demand, but existing policies often optimize for hit rate or position heuristics rather than the actual attention-kernel latency caused by non-contiguous context.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces AsymCache, a computation-latency-aware KV-cache management system that aligns cache residency with GPU attention-kernel performance.
  • Designs Multi-Segment Attention for efficient processing of non-contiguous KV context, plus an eviction policy that jointly optimizes cache hit rate and position-aware recomputation cost.
  • Adds an adaptive chunking scheduler and reports 1.90–2.03× TTFT reduction, 1.62–1.71× TPOT reduction over recent baselines, and up to 18.1% lower average job latency when integrated into Continuum-style agent serving.

Why it matters: This is less network-facing than NetKV, but it changes the amount and shape of KV state that serving systems must hold, reconstruct, or move. For disaggregated serving, better cache semantics upstream can reduce pressure on downstream network placement.

Key insights:

  • KV-cache “usefulness” is not just future access probability; the position of a hit changes the cost of serving it through attention kernels.
  • Non-contiguous context is becoming a first-class serving pattern as agentic and long-context workloads reuse scattered spans.
  • Cache eviction and kernel design need to co-design: a clever cache policy can backfire if it produces fragments that the GPU executes inefficiently.
  • Agent serving benefits because repeated program/context segments can be kept exact without always retaining every block in HBM.

Actionable lessons: Design KV-cache managers with kernel-latency models, not just byte capacity and hit rate. For networked serving, expose cache fragmentation and recomputation cost to routing/placement decisions so fabric movement and GPU reconstruction are compared honestly.

Useful diagram: Figure 4 gives the AsymCache overview, and Figure 5 illustrates the Multi-Segment Attention kernel for non-contiguous KV context.

Fast take

The common thread is that serving systems are becoming fabric- and memory-hierarchy-aware. KV cache is no longer just a local HBM object: it is routed across topology, reconstructed by kernels, partitioned across heterogeneous devices, and sometimes controlled by in-network scheduling primitives.
