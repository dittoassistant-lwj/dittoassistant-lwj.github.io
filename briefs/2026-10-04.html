AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s strongest signal is fabric-aware resource assembly: using optical stitching to recover fragmented TPU capacity, plus scale-up MoE kernels and heterogeneity-aware planning for GPU clusters.

Most important paper of the day

RingStitch: Demand-Aware Optical Stitching for Fragmented TPU Clusters

Authors & affiliations: Huiru Ao, Fan Yang, Binglei Wang, Bo Liu, and Jialong Li — Faculty of Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence, Shenzhen University of Advanced Technology.

Tags: Optical Circuit Switching, TPU Clusters, Cluster Scheduling

Why this is the top pick: This is the most network-facing paper today. It treats the optical fabric as a scheduler-visible resource for recovering stranded accelerator fragments, while still pricing topology/ring cost instead of pretending that arbitrary cross-rack assembly is free.

Deeper explanation: RingStitch is useful because it frames OCS as more than bandwidth provisioning. The scheduler gets a new placement primitive: assemble a training slice from partially free racks only when local placement fails, then choose nearby racks and a low-cost logical ring so the recovered capacity does not become a communication tax. That is a clean mental model for superpod operators: defragmentation and topology control have to be solved together.

  • Problem: OCS-enabled TPU-style clusters can have enough idle accelerators in aggregate while no single rack, and no set of fully free racks, can satisfy a new job. The paper targets this rack-level residual fragmentation without letting cross-rack stitching explode communication cost.
  • Key contributions:
  • Formulates the gap between aggregate free capacity and policy-feasible capacity in OCS-enabled TPU clusters.
  • Designs RingStitch: local-first placement, bounded cross-rack candidate generation, fragment-first allocation, and topology-aware ring ordering under a lexicographic objective.
  • Evaluates on a TPU 8t-like SuperPod simulator and reports up to 5.2 percentage-point utilization improvement, up to 7.8 percentage-point lower demand-weighted blocking, and large waiting-time reductions versus full-rack stitching on fragmentation-heavy workloads.
  • Why it matters architecturally: For accelerator fabrics with OCS or other reconfigurable links, expose “fragment capacity” as a schedulable object, but attach a communication-cost model to every stitched placement. A practical allocator should try local placement first, stitch only when aggregate capacity exists, and optimize rack count, path/ring cost, and post-placement fragmentation together.

RingStitch: Demand-Aware Optical Stitching for Fragmented TPU Clusters

Authors & affiliations: Huiru Ao, Fan Yang, Binglei Wang, Bo Liu, and Jialong Li — Faculty of Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence, Shenzhen University of Advanced Technology.

Tags: Optical Circuit Switching, TPU Clusters, Cluster Scheduling

Problem: OCS-enabled TPU-style clusters can have enough idle accelerators in aggregate while no single rack, and no set of fully free racks, can satisfy a new job. The paper targets this rack-level residual fragmentation without letting cross-rack stitching explode communication cost.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Formulates the gap between aggregate free capacity and policy-feasible capacity in OCS-enabled TPU clusters.
  • Designs RingStitch: local-first placement, bounded cross-rack candidate generation, fragment-first allocation, and topology-aware ring ordering under a lexicographic objective.
  • Evaluates on a TPU 8t-like SuperPod simulator and reports up to 5.2 percentage-point utilization improvement, up to 7.8 percentage-point lower demand-weighted blocking, and large waiting-time reductions versus full-rack stitching on fragmentation-heavy workloads.

Why it matters: This is the most network-facing paper today. It treats the optical fabric as a scheduler-visible resource for recovering stranded accelerator fragments, while still pricing topology/ring cost instead of pretending that arbitrary cross-rack assembly is free.

Key insights:

  • Fragmentation is not just an allocator issue: a cluster can be globally feasible but infeasible under rack-local or full-rack-only policies.
  • The useful scheduling primitive is selective stitching of residual rack capacity, not all-or-nothing full-rack circuit construction.
  • Topology-aware ordering matters because OCS can make a job schedulable while still creating a poor communication layout.
  • Fragment-first allocation preserves larger local residual blocks for future jobs, improving follow-on placement quality.

Actionable lessons: For accelerator fabrics with OCS or other reconfigurable links, expose “fragment capacity” as a schedulable object, but attach a communication-cost model to every stitched placement. A practical allocator should try local placement first, stitch only when aggregate capacity exists, and optimize rack count, path/ring cost, and post-placement fragmentation together.

Mixture-of-Kittens: MoE Megakernel for NVL72s

Authors & affiliations: Stuart H. Sul, Nash Brown, Henry Wildermuth, William Lin, Federico Cassano, and Christopher Ré — Department of Computer Science, Stanford University; Cursor Research.

Tags: Scale-up Networking, MoE Training, NVL72

Problem: MoE training systems designed around conventional scale-out networks do not transfer cleanly to NVL72-style single-hop scale-up domains; even PyTorch+NCCL can beat specialized MoE systems in many configurations.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds MoK, a deterministic persistent megakernel for NVIDIA NVL72 that fuses token dispatch, expert FFNs, gated activation, and token combine.
  • Shows three scale-up-specific design choices: per-operator push versus pull communication, tunable computation/communication overlap granularity, and fully device-side ring-buffer management to remove CPU-GPU synchronization.
  • Reports up to 2.37× throughput over the strongest public baseline and 1.41× end-to-end production training throughput improvement on 512 GPUs across multiple GB300 NVL72 racks.

Why it matters: MoK is directly in Ethan territory: it argues that large scale-up fabrics should be treated less like a slow network and more like a high-bandwidth shared-memory-ish execution substrate. That changes where communication scheduling belongs — inside the kernel, not only in NCCL-level collectives.

Key insights:

  • Scale-up fabrics invert some assumptions from InfiniBand/RoCE-era MoE systems; communication granularity can be much finer, but too fine starves tensor cores.
  • Push/pull is not a global choice: dispatch and combine can want different directions depending on scheduling overhead and data movement pattern.
  • CPU-GPU synchronization becomes visible overhead on NVL72 systems, so buffer management has to move into persistent device-side execution.
  • The paper’s ring buffer design is a communication-control structure as much as a memory-saving trick.

Actionable lessons: When targeting NVL72-like fabrics, avoid porting scale-out MoE kernels unchanged. Benchmark communication direction, overlap granularity, and host synchronization separately. Treat SM partitioning, buffer lifetimes, and NCCL/RDMA overlap as architectural knobs rather than implementation details.

Heddle: Learning Structural Templates for Parallelism Planning on Heterogeneous GPU Clusters

Authors & affiliations: Taeyoon Kim, Yonguk Song, Seoyeong Choy, Hexiao Duan, Dong Li, Seo Jin Park, and Myeongjae Jeon — UNIST; POSTECH; UC Merced; USC.

Tags: Heterogeneous GPU Clusters, Parallelism Planning, MoE Training

Problem: Shared AI clusters accumulate GPU heterogeneity and variable availability, making distributed-training plan search too slow if every tensor/pipeline/expert-parallel decision is optimized from scratch after each resource change.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Decomposes planning into structural templates — pipeline depth, per-stage GPU type, and tensor-parallel degree — plus profile-based completion of the remaining parameters.
  • Uses an RL policy trained offline to construct reusable templates across GPU compositions, node sizes, and cluster sizes.
  • Reports throughput matching or exceeding five planners, with gains up to 84.5% on dense models and 4.6× on MoE models in evaluated heterogeneous settings.

Why it matters: This is not a transport paper, but it is very relevant to heterogeneous AI infrastructure. The planner explicitly reasons about GPU type, node size, memory, and interconnect bandwidth — exactly the kind of cross-layer signal that becomes important when superpods are no longer homogeneous islands.

Key insights:

  • The key reusable object is the structure of a plan, not every low-level parallelism parameter.
  • Tensor-parallel degree is a network-sensitive choice because it determines frequent collective traffic over local or cross-node interconnects.
  • MoE planning needs bounded expert-layout evaluation; otherwise expert parallelism adds another combinatorial axis on top of GPU heterogeneity.
  • Fast planning matters operationally because placement opportunities can disappear while a planner is still searching.

Actionable lessons: For mixed GPU/NPU generations, separate slow offline learning of structural templates from fast runtime completion using measured primitive profiles. Require the planner to expose interconnect assumptions and rerun sensitivity tests under degraded links, missing GPU types, and non-uniform node sizes.

verified arXiv paper · AI-RAN, Distributed Training, Heterogeneous Edge Infrastructure

Weaver: A System for AI-RAN Compute Sharing with Foundation Model Training

Authors & affiliations: Leyang Xue, Tianxin Wang, Xin Zhe Khooi, Jiaxun Yang, Dheeraj Mahendiran, Yufeng Xia, Mun Choon Chan, Myungjin Lee, and Mahesh K. Marina — The University of Edinburgh; National University of Singapore; Cisco Research.

Tags: AI-RAN, Distributed Training, Heterogeneous Edge Infrastructure

Problem: GPU-accelerated RAN sites may have substantial spare capacity, but RAN workloads are latency-critical and bursty, while foundation-model training wants sustained, coordinated compute across many heterogeneous sites.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Characterizes spare GPU capacity in AI-RAN systems at micro-scale within a cell site and macro-scale across sites, finding 40–85% unused capacity in the studied setting.
  • Introduces a RAN-first spare-compute controller inside the MAC scheduler to smooth RAN GPU demand while preserving per-slot deadlines.
  • Adds a two-level elastic FM training framework that adapts computation within and across sites, reporting 2.1–3.7× higher training throughput than baselines on an eight-site distributed GPU testbed.

Why it matters: This is adjacent rather than superpod-core, but it is a nice heterogeneous-infrastructure signal: spare accelerator capacity is only useful if the primary workload can expose safe windows and the training stack can adapt to bursty, spatially distributed compute.

Key insights:

  • The paper treats the RAN scheduler as the authority for safe spare compute, not an external predictor guessing when GPUs are idle.
  • Temporal burstiness at one site can be partially offset by spatial diversity across sites, which is a scheduling/federation opportunity.
  • Fine-grained GEMM tiling is used as the intra-site adaptation mechanism, while model-layout reconfiguration is reserved for slower inter-site shifts.
  • The design is fundamentally a control-plane problem between a latency-critical network workload and opportunistic AI training.

Actionable lessons: When sharing accelerators with a primary real-time workload, put the admission/control logic near the primary scheduler and export only deadline-safe spare capacity. For distributed training over bursty resources, separate fast local elasticity from slower global layout changes, otherwise reconfiguration overhead will dominate.
