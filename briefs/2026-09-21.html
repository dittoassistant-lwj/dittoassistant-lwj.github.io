Most important paper of the day

DLB: Distributed Load Balancing at Scale for Generative AI Inference

Authors & affiliations: Santiago R. Balseiro, Bartek Wydrowski, Sameer Agarwal, David Applegate, Aaron Archer, Soheil Hassas Yeganeh, Alex Iriza, Bobby Kleinberg, Balasubramanian Sivan, Pranav Vaish, Oscar Zegarra, Wenxin Zhang, Vahab Mirrokni, and Amin Vahdat — Google Research; Columbia University; Cornell University.

Tags: AI Serving Infrastructure, Distributed Load Balancing, Fleet Telemetry

Why this is the top pick: This is the freshest strong signal today. It is not a transport paper, but it is very relevant to AI-cluster operations: once inference is spread across accelerator cells, routing must become capacity-aware, model-aware, and telemetry-driven rather than just “pick a backend.”

Deeper explanation: DLB is valuable because it sits at the boundary between networking, serving, and accelerator economics. The paper’s main move is to replace coarse backend selection with distributed routing that continuously senses serving capacity and reasons about latency curves. For large inference fleets, that is the difference between routing to a nominally healthy cell and routing into a hidden queue behind expensive accelerators.

  • Problem: Large generative-AI fleets serve heterogeneous models on scarce GPU/TPU capacity. Conventional load balancers lack timely visibility into per-cell serving capacity and often rely on overprovisioning when service times and pipeline stages vary.
  • Key contributions:
  • Designs DLB, a production distributed load balancer with root/leaf routing and peer-to-peer probing so routers maintain live capacity estimates across large, geographically distributed infrastructure.
  • Uses learned latency models to estimate the end-to-end latency impact of routing decisions for heterogeneous hardware, models, and serving pipelines.
  • Provides stability/performance analysis for the routing algorithms and reports production migration results with 17% lower median latency and 13% lower p95 latency versus the legacy baseline.
  • Why it matters architecturally: The design points toward AI serving fabrics where load balancers, telemetry, and accelerator schedulers form one control loop instead of three loosely coupled systems.

DLB: Distributed Load Balancing at Scale for Generative AI Inference

Authors & affiliations: Santiago R. Balseiro, Bartek Wydrowski, Sameer Agarwal, David Applegate, Aaron Archer, Soheil Hassas Yeganeh, Alex Iriza, Bobby Kleinberg, Balasubramanian Sivan, Pranav Vaish, Oscar Zegarra, Wenxin Zhang, Vahab Mirrokni, and Amin Vahdat — Google Research; Columbia University; Cornell University.

Tags: AI Serving Infrastructure, Distributed Load Balancing, Fleet Telemetry

Problem: Large generative-AI fleets serve heterogeneous models on scarce GPU/TPU capacity. Conventional load balancers lack timely visibility into per-cell serving capacity and often rely on overprovisioning when service times and pipeline stages vary.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Designs DLB, a production distributed load balancer with root/leaf routing and peer-to-peer probing so routers maintain live capacity estimates across large, geographically distributed infrastructure.
  • Uses learned latency models to estimate the end-to-end latency impact of routing decisions for heterogeneous hardware, models, and serving pipelines.
  • Provides stability/performance analysis for the routing algorithms and reports production migration results with 17% lower median latency and 13% lower p95 latency versus the legacy baseline.

Why it matters: This is the freshest strong signal today. It is not a transport paper, but it is very relevant to AI-cluster operations: once inference is spread across accelerator cells, routing must become capacity-aware, model-aware, and telemetry-driven rather than just “pick a backend.”

Key insights:

  • The important mechanism is router-local decision-making backed by continuous peer probing; global freshness is approximated through distributed telemetry rather than centralized queue polling.
  • The paper treats serving-cell internals as opaque but measurable, which is a practical abstraction for mixed GPU/TPU fleets where model architecture and serving stack evolve faster than the load balancer.
  • Latency modeling is used as a routing primitive, not just an offline dashboard; this makes admission and placement react to the slope of the latency curve under load.
  • The production evidence matters: the claimed wins come from migrations across a large fleet of endpoints, not only from a simulator microbenchmark.

Actionable lessons: For large AI-serving fabrics, expose near-real-time capacity signals at the routing boundary. Keep the routing algorithm distributed, but make it sensitive to accelerator saturation curves and model-stage heterogeneity; otherwise the network edge will route into hidden GPU queues.

Beluga: A CXL-Based Memory Architecture for Scalable and Efficient LLM KVCache Management

Authors & affiliations: Xinjun Yang, Qingda Hu, Junru Li, Feifei Li, Yicong Zhu, Yuqi Zhou, Qiuru Lin, Jian Dai, Yang Kong, Jiayu Zhang, Guoqiang Xu, and Qiang Liu — Alibaba Cloud Computing, Sunnyvale, Hangzhou, Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen.

Tags: CXL Memory, LLM Serving, KV Cache

Problem: LLM serving needs KV-cache capacity beyond GPU HBM and often beyond practical CPU DRAM limits. RDMA memory pools add latency, protocol complexity, and synchronization overhead, while local host memory is capacity-limited.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes Beluga, a CXL-switch-based shared memory architecture where GPUs and CPUs access a large memory pool with load/store-style semantics.
  • Contrasts RDMA-based memory pools with CXL pools for KV-cache management, emphasizing latency, software complexity, and memory-pool scalability.
  • Builds and evaluates a deployed Beluga prototype, including data access interfaces and KV-cache management mechanisms for GPU-accelerated serving.

Why it matters: This is a clean infrastructure paper for the “memory is part of the serving fabric” trend. For long-context inference, the question is no longer only how many GPUs are in a pod; it is how KV state moves across HBM, host memory, CXL, and possibly remote pools.

Key insights:

  • The paper’s key bet is that CXL can make external memory look closer to a shared load/store tier, while RDMA pools still look like explicit networked data movement.
  • KV-cache management stresses both capacity and latency: a big pool is not useful if every access pays remote-memory software overhead.
  • CXL switching changes the resource-allocation problem from per-server DRAM sizing to fabric-level memory provisioning.
  • The design is most compelling where context length or tenant multiplexing pushes KV state beyond HBM but the serving path cannot tolerate full RDMA-style transfer semantics.

Actionable lessons: When designing disaggregated serving nodes, model KV cache as a fabric resource with latency classes. If CXL is present, design page placement and eviction around direct access semantics rather than blindly copying the RDMA memory-pool playbook.

verified arXiv paper · Disaggregated LLM Serving, RoCE/RDMA, KV Transfer

P/D-Serve: Serving Disaggregated Large Language Model at Scale

Authors & affiliations: Yibo Jin, Tao Wang, Huimin Lin, Mingyang Song, Peiyang Li, Yipeng Ma, Yicheng Shan, Zhengfan Yuan, Cailong Li, Yajing Sun, Tiandeng Wu, Xing Chu, Ruizhi Huan, Li Ma, Xiao You, Wenting Zhou, Yunpeng Ye, Wen Liu, Xiangkun Xu, Yongsheng Zhang, Tiantian Dong, Jiawei Zhu, Zhe Wang, Xijian Ju, Jianxun Song, Haoliang Cheng, Xiaojing Li, Jiandong Ding, Hefei Guo, and Zhengyong Zhang — Huawei Technologies Co., Ltd.

Tags: Disaggregated LLM Serving, RoCE/RDMA, KV Transfer

Problem: At tens of thousands of GPUs/NPUs, disaggregating prefill and decode creates a KV-cache transfer and orchestration problem: fixed P/D pools, stale queue reports, and block-fixed D2D transfer over RoCE waste capacity and trigger TTFT misses.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces P/D-Serve, an end-to-end disaggregated serving system deployed over tens of thousands of xPU devices.
  • Uses fine-grained P/D organization and dynamic P/D-ratio adjustment so scenarios with similar prefixes and request patterns can be grouped more effectively.
  • Adds on-demand forwarding and optimized D2D KV-cache transfer over cluster-level RoCE, reporting 60% E2E throughput, 42% TTFT SLO, and 46% D2D transfer-time improvements in commercial deployment.

Why it matters: This is older than the freshness window, but it is unusually relevant because it ties serving architecture directly to RDMA/RoCE data movement. It is exactly the kind of “LLM serving is now a network system” paper worth keeping in the archive.

Key insights:

  • The paper shows why global scheduler queue polling is too weak: prefill time depends on cached-prefix length, memory capacity, and local batch conditions, not just pending-token counts.
  • KV-cache transfer is not a background detail; D2D transfer efficiency directly affects both TTFT and decode throughput.
  • Scenario grouping is a serving/network co-design trick: prefix locality improves cache behavior, but it also changes where traffic must flow.
  • Dynamic RoCE configuration for P/D adjustment is the most network-facing piece, because logical serving roles move while the fabric must keep D2D transfer efficient.

Actionable lessons: For disaggregated inference, treat KV transfer as a first-class collective-ish data path. Measure D2D utilization, not just model throughput, and make P/D placement aware of prefix locality, queue uncertainty, and fabric cost.

Thallus: An RDMA-based Columnar Data Transport Protocol

Authors & affiliations: Jayjeet Chakraborty, Matthieu Dorier, Philip Carns, Robert Ross, Carlos Maltzahn, and Heiner Litz — UC Santa Cruz; Argonne National Laboratory.

Tags: RDMA, Data Transport, Zero-Copy Systems

Problem: Distributed analytics systems frequently serialize columnar data into contiguous TCP-friendly buffers, adding costly memory copies during shuffle/exchange phases in data-center-scale processing.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Studies serialization overhead for Apache Arrow columnar batches in distributed data transport.
  • Designs Thallus, an RDMA-based Apache Arrow transport over InfiniBand using the Mochi/Thallium ecosystem.
  • Shows up to 5.5× faster data transport than a Thallium RPC baseline in evaluated column-selectivity experiments.

Why it matters: This is not AI-specific, but the mechanism is relevant to AI data paths: once tensors, embeddings, KV pages, or training data are already in structured memory layouts, forcing contiguous serialization before hitting the NIC is a self-inflicted tax.

Key insights:

  • The reusable idea is schema-aware zero-copy transport: move the existing buffer layout rather than packing it into a transport-shaped blob.
  • RDMA helps most when the application format already exposes discontiguous buffers that can be registered and transferred directly.
  • The paper is a reminder that “network bandwidth” measurements can hide CPU memory-copy bottlenecks before bytes ever reach the wire.
  • For AI infrastructure, similar thinking applies to tensor shards, Arrow/Parquet feature pipelines, and remote KV/cache stores.

Actionable lessons: Audit every network-facing AI data path for hidden serialization. If the producer already has structured buffers, prefer transport APIs that preserve layout and avoid staging through contiguous host memory.

verified arXiv paper · Datacenter Ethernet, RDMA/RoCE, Hyperscale Networks

Datacenter Ethernet and RDMA: Issues at Hyperscale

Authors & affiliations: Torsten Hoefler, Duncan Roweth, Keith Underwood, Bob Alverson, Mark Griswold, Vahid Tabatabaee, Mohan Kalkunte, Surendra Anubolu, Siyuan Shen, Abdul Kabbani, Moray McLaren, and Steve Scott — ETH Zürich; Microsoft; Hewlett Packard Enterprise; Broadcom; Google.

Tags: Datacenter Ethernet, RDMA/RoCE, Hyperscale Networks

Problem: RoCE brought RDMA semantics onto Ethernet, but hyperscale AI/HPC/storage networks now operate at bandwidths and message rates where old assumptions about headroom, load balancing, congestion control, retransmission, and error handling break down.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Revisits RoCE design choices against hyperscale AI, HPC, and storage requirements.
  • Identifies practical gaps in Ethernet/RDMA deployments, including buffering/headroom, congestion control, tail latency, FEC, packet spraying, and failure/retry behavior.
  • Argues that datacenter and HPC networking requirements are converging toward modernized Ethernet-based high-performance networking rather than today’s TCP/RoCE split.

Why it matters: This is an older fallback, but it is foundational for Ethan’s world: it frames why “Ethernet for AI clusters” is not just a standards checkbox. The hard parts are control loops, loss/reorder semantics, failure modes, and host/NIC/switch co-design.

Key insights:

  • The paper’s core point is that the 100× bandwidth and 10× message-rate shift invalidates comfortable buffer and timeout assumptions.
  • FEC, retransmission, and congestion control are coupled; optimizing one in isolation can push latency or headroom cost elsewhere.
  • SmartNICs and programmable switches make the architecture more flexible but also blur where transport responsibility lives.
  • The convergence argument is still useful: AI clusters want HPC-like performance and cloud-like manageability at hyperscale economics.

Actionable lessons: For Ethernet-based AI fabrics, evaluate the full reliability/congestion stack under incast, packet spraying, link faults, and small-message collectives. Do not accept “RDMA-capable” as equivalent to “scale-up/scale-out training ready.”
