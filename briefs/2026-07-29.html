AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s strongest theme is disaggregation with teeth: moving collectives, storage, and placement decisions out of the GPU critical path, while treating heterogeneous fabrics and memory/storage tiers as first-class architecture objects.

Most important paper of the day

DisDP: Disaggregating Compute, Network, and Storage for Model-Sharded Data-Parallel Training

Authors & affiliations: Mo Sun, Zihan Yang, Changyue Liao, Yingtao Li, Jie Zhang, Kaiqi Chen, Fei Wu, and Zeke Wang. Affiliations: Zhejiang University, China.

Tags: SmartNICs/SmartSwitch, LLM Training, Collective Offload

Why this is the top pick: This is the strongest Ethan-signal today because it attacks the exact AI-superpod pain point: collective communication is not just bytes on the wire; it competes with GEMM launch, CPU orchestration, optimizer-state I/O, and fault domains. The paper’s answer is a clean “move network/storage work out of the GPU critical path” design using SmartNICs and SmartSwitches.

Deeper explanation: DisDP is valuable because it frames model-sharded data parallelism as a three-way resource-coupling problem: GPU compute, network collectives, and optimizer-state storage are logically separate but operationally serialized in common stacks. Its SmartNIC/SmartSwitch design is not just “offload for offload’s sake”; it creates isolation between GEMM and communication launch paths, then moves parameter-server fan-in/fan-out into a network-aware substrate. For AI superpods, that is a useful mental model: if the iteration tail is caused by orchestration interference, adding bandwidth alone may not fix it.

  • Problem: Model-sharded data parallel training such as ZeRO reduces GPU memory pressure, but the extra AllGather/ReduceScatter path aggregates compute, NCCL communication, and optimizer-state I/O on the same CPU/GPU workers, causing GPU kernel interference and low MFU.
  • Key contributions: Proposes DisDP, a fully disaggregated MSDP architecture that separates compute, network collectives, and optimizer-state storage rather than letting GPUs orchestrate all three.; Offloads collectives to SmartNICs and a SmartSwitch, including worker-side collective processing and switch-assisted many-to-one reliable transport for the parameter-server path.; Reports that on 8 distributed GPUs, DisDP outperforms state-of-the-art training systems by 3.98× on a 175B-model training setup, with broader ASTRA-sim scaling analysis.
  • Why it matters architecturally: For model-sharded training, profile the launch/interference path before blaming the fabric. If collectives and optimizer-state movement are serialized through GPU/CPU workers, consider whether NIC/switch offload or a split control/data plane can remove orchestration from the iteration critical path.

DisDP: Disaggregating Compute, Network, and Storage for Model-Sharded Data-Parallel Training

Authors & affiliations: Mo Sun, Zihan Yang, Changyue Liao, Yingtao Li, Jie Zhang, Kaiqi Chen, Fei Wu, and Zeke Wang. Affiliations: Zhejiang University, China.

Tags: SmartNICs/SmartSwitch, LLM Training, Collective Offload

Problem: Model-sharded data parallel training such as ZeRO reduces GPU memory pressure, but the extra AllGather/ReduceScatter path aggregates compute, NCCL communication, and optimizer-state I/O on the same CPU/GPU workers, causing GPU kernel interference and low MFU.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes DisDP, a fully disaggregated MSDP architecture that separates compute, network collectives, and optimizer-state storage rather than letting GPUs orchestrate all three.
  • Offloads collectives to SmartNICs and a SmartSwitch, including worker-side collective processing and switch-assisted many-to-one reliable transport for the parameter-server path.
  • Reports that on 8 distributed GPUs, DisDP outperforms state-of-the-art training systems by 3.98× on a 175B-model training setup, with broader ASTRA-sim scaling analysis.

Why it matters: This is the strongest Ethan-signal today because it attacks the exact AI-superpod pain point: collective communication is not just bytes on the wire; it competes with GEMM launch, CPU orchestration, optimizer-state I/O, and fault domains. The paper’s answer is a clean “move network/storage work out of the GPU critical path” design using SmartNICs and SmartSwitches.

Key insights:

  • The paper’s key observation is interference, not raw link bandwidth: even collectives that consume fewer than 10% of GPU SMs can delay training when launched serially around GEMM and storage work.
  • SmartNIC/SmartSwitch offload is used as a scheduling isolation mechanism, not merely as packet acceleration. That is important for clusters where GPU kernels are non-preemptive and communication kernels miss their launch window.
  • The parameter-server side is redesigned for many workers with a SmartSwitch-assisted reliable protocol, so “storage” becomes part of the data plane rather than a slow host-side afterthought.
  • The multi-rack discussion makes the design relevant beyond a toy single-switch testbed, though the strongest real implementation evidence is the 8-GPU setup.

Actionable lessons: For model-sharded training, profile the launch/interference path before blaming the fabric. If collectives and optimizer-state movement are serialized through GPU/CPU workers, consider whether NIC/switch offload or a split control/data plane can remove orchestration from the iteration critical path.

Application-Driven Architecture Exploration for Cross-Layer Heterogeneous Systems

Authors & affiliations: Yuchen Fan, Minghong Sun, Jikui Ma, Yunpeng Xu, Shunyu Mao, Liu He, Shunan Dong, Jiahao Yang, Yu Zhu, Xinhao Yang, Tianyan Zhong, Haoran Sun, Daoqi Liu, Zongle Huang, Xinyuan Lin, Huazhong Yang, Maokun Li, Yongpan Liu, Yu Wang, Zhenhua Zhu, Hongyang Jia, and Shuwen Deng. Affiliations: Tsinghua University.

Tags: AI Cluster Architecture, Scale-up/Scale-out Fabrics, Design-Space Exploration

Problem: AI/HPC platforms now mix dense tensor compute, sparse kernels, large memory footprints, and communication-heavy collectives, but node-scale design tools and fixed-platform simulators cannot jointly reason about accelerators, memory tiers, scale-up fabrics, switch radix, cabling, rack power, and workload mapping.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces CHASE, a workload-driven framework that represents cross-layer heterogeneous systems as hierarchical typed graphs and filters physically infeasible deployments.
  • Uses a two-level loop: topology-aware workload mapping and calibrated event-driven simulation inside, telemetry-guided hardware-graph optimization outside.
  • Evaluates sparse-computing and LLM workloads, reporting mapper results within 6.06% of exhaustive optima and case-study speedups of 6.20× for sparse workloads and 2.12× for LLM inference versus baselines.

Why it matters: This is useful because it treats AI cluster design as a coupled graph problem: workload DAGs meet scale-up islands, memory tiers, cluster networks, cost, and deployment constraints. That is much closer to real superpod architecture work than a single-device roofline model.

Key insights:

  • The paper explicitly models hierarchy from accelerator/card/node layers up to cluster-layer networks, which is the right abstraction for GPU/NPU pods with both scale-up and scale-out links.
  • The LLM case study favors scale-up islands, matching the intuition that inference performance often wants high-bandwidth local communication before spilling across the cluster.
  • Physical feasibility checks—switch radix, cabling, power, cost—are not cosmetic; they prune designs that a pure simulator might incorrectly treat as viable.
  • The event-driven simulator is calibrated against real hardware, but the value is in trend-faithful architecture exploration rather than cycle-accurate prediction.

Actionable lessons: When exploring heterogeneous AI platforms, keep topology, mapping, and deployment constraints in one loop. A design that looks optimal under abstract bandwidth can become illegal or expensive once rack-level fabric and cabling constraints are included.

Beyond Prefill-Decode Disaggregation: Dissecting LLM Inference for Heterogeneous Platforms via Dynamic Operator Scheduling

Authors & affiliations: Jiaqi Yang, Jiayi Li, Yihan Fu, Hongxiao Zhao, Zhan Chen, Qiuping Wu, Yuchao Yang, Bonan Yan. Affiliations: Peking University, Beijing, China.

Tags: LLM Serving, Heterogeneous NPU/PIM, Dynamic Scheduling

Problem: Prefill/decode disaggregation and static roofline placement are too coarse for heterogeneous NPU–PIM serving systems because latency depends on workload shape, runtime contention, operator DAG structure, and persistent weight layout.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents DOPS, a closed-loop framework that builds a stage-aware DAG and jointly optimizes operator placement plus blockwise weight layout.
  • Introduces Bifocal for dynamic operator-to-device placement and WLA for hardware-efficient weight-layout selection under memory constraints.
  • Reports Bifocal geometric-mean speedups of 1.20×–2.23× over prefill/decode disaggregation baselines, with WLA adding another 1.28×–1.33× over Bifocal/Linear.

Why it matters: This is a good heterogeneous-infrastructure paper because it moves beyond “put prefill here, decode there.” In real accelerator pods, the useful scheduling unit may be an operator or block of weights, and the data movement contract is shaped by memory layout as much as by compute capability.

Key insights:

  • The paper’s main systems point is that no single static NPU/PIM mapping wins across prompt length, batch size, model size, and memory pressure.
  • Weight layout is part of scheduling: if the layout forces expensive reformatting or duplication, the theoretical placement win can disappear.
  • The stage-aware DAG makes prefill/decode a special case rather than the core abstraction, which is healthier for future heterogeneous serving stacks.
  • Trace-driven BurstGPT results suggest the approach is intended for bursty serving behavior, not just fixed microbenchmarks.

Actionable lessons: For heterogeneous serving, treat placement and memory layout as a joint runtime decision. A practical scheduler should observe workload phase and device pressure, then move at operator/block granularity when coarse prefill/decode splits are leaving bandwidth or capacity idle.

Decoding the Skew: Distribution-Aware MoE Inference with Adaptive Kernel Dispatch

Authors & affiliations: En-Ming Huang, An-Cheng Chang, Bai-Cheng Jeng, Shih-Hao Hung, and H. T. Kung. Affiliations: National Taiwan University, Taipei, Taiwan; NVIDIA Corporation, Taipei, Taiwan; Harvard University, Cambridge, USA.

Tags: MoE Inference, GPU Runtime, Expert Parallelism

Problem: MoE inference routes uneven token counts to experts, but serving systems often choose fused-MoE kernels from static token-count buckets, ignoring the live per-expert distribution that determines padding, memory reuse, and kernel efficiency.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Defines a distribution-aware benchmarking framework using an Effective Experts metric and Dirichlet-based reverse modeling to generate controllable routing skew.
  • Shows that the best fused-MoE kernel changes with routing skew and token count, so static kernel selection leaves performance on the floor.
  • Builds DA-MoE, a GPU-resident dispatch runtime that maps live routing histograms to offline-tuned distributions without CPU–GPU synchronization, improving geomean fused-MoE latency by 1.16× on DeepSeek-V3 and 1.29× on Kimi K2 traces.

Why it matters: This is less network-facing than DisDP, but it matters for expert-parallel serving: routing skew is the root of both kernel inefficiency and all-to-all imbalance. Better local dispatch does not solve fabric pressure, but it removes one source of noisy per-layer tail latency.

Key insights:

  • The paper separates effective throughput from physical throughput, which helps distinguish useful expert work from padded or wasted tile work.
  • Routing distributions are workload-state, not just model metadata; runtime histograms carry scheduling information that static buckets throw away.
  • The GPU-resident dispatch path avoids CPU synchronization, which is critical if the mechanism is meant to sit in the per-token or per-layer serving loop.
  • For MoE systems, kernel choice, expert placement, and network all-to-all should ideally share a common view of routing skew.

Actionable lessons: Expose routing-distribution telemetry as a first-class runtime signal. If network load balancers or expert-placement policies only see average token counts, they miss the same skew that already changes optimal GPU kernel selection.

Hardware-Transparent I/O Governance in Disaggregated Heterogeneous Storage

Authors & affiliations: Rajarshi Chowdhury, Akshay Shah, and Sue K. Lee. Affiliations: Oracle America Inc..

Tags: Disaggregated Storage, I/O Scheduling, Cluster SLOs

Problem: Disaggregated storage clusters serving databases and block-volume workloads need tenant-wide I/O governance across heterogeneous hardware generations and skewed access patterns, but neither compute-side nor storage-side local throttling sees the whole tenant budget.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents IORM, a production multi-stage distributed scheduler in Oracle Exadata Exascale.
  • Combines hardware-normalized I/O accounting, quantum-based rate limiting with bounded carry-forward credits, and a distributed adaptive feedback controller for cluster-wide entitlement redistribution.
  • Shows convergence within 5% of provisioned limits under extreme sequential skew, scalable isolation for up to 100 tenant volumes, and full throughput recovery within 15 seconds after a storage-node failure.

Why it matters: This is adjacent rather than AI-specific, but it is relevant to heterogeneous AI infrastructure because large training and serving platforms increasingly depend on disaggregated storage/memory tiers with global SLOs. The paper is a concrete production example of governing a distributed data path when no single node has full visibility.

Key insights:

  • The main difficulty is topological skew: the tenant’s logical limit is global, while hot data may concentrate pressure on a subset of storage nodes.
  • Hardware-transparent accounting matters when a cluster spans generations; otherwise newer nodes distort fairness and limits.
  • The controller design accepts micro-bursts while enforcing long-term SLOs, which is the right shape for bursty checkpoint, cache, or training data paths.
  • Failure recovery is part of the scheduling contract: after a storage-node failure, entitlement redistribution must converge quickly enough to avoid prolonged tenant impact.

Actionable lessons: For AI storage, KV, or remote-memory paths, avoid purely local throttles. Design quota and SLO enforcement around the logical tenant/job, with distributed feedback that can compensate for skew, heterogeneous devices, and failed nodes.
