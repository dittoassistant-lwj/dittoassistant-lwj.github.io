AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s batch has a clean systems theme: rack-scale MoE load balancing, GPU-native remote storage, and one-sided network APIs are all about making the data/control path match the fabric instead of hiding it behind CPU-centric or stale scheduling abstractions.

Most important paper of the day

UltraEP: Unleash MoE Training and Inference on Rack-Scale Nodes with Near-Optimal Load Balancing

Authors & affiliations: Xinming Wei, Chao Jin, Tuo Dai, Yinmin Zhong, Shan Yu, Chengxu Yang, Bingyang Wu, Zili Zhang, Jing Mai, Qianchao Zhu, Zhouyang Li, Yuliang Liu, and Guojie Luo. Affiliations: Peking University; RedNote; Shanghai AI Laboratory; Independent Researcher.

Tags: Scale-up Networking, MoE Systems, AI Clusters

Why this is the top pick: This is the strongest signal today because it treats rack-scale scale-up connectivity as an algorithmic resource, not just a faster pipe. The paper’s core point is that MoE imbalance is no longer just a scheduler problem: fixing it on the hot path requires co-designing the load-balancing plan with the fabric’s ability to move expert state quickly.

Deeper explanation: UltraEP is interesting because it collapses a normally slow control loop into the MoE critical path. The authors observe that expert popularity shifts across microbatches, layers, and data domains, so periodic historical balancing can be wrong exactly when the hot path needs precision. Their answer is to use rack-scale-node connectivity to rearrange expert state after gating, with quota-driven planning and RSN-native transfer mechanisms keeping the exposed overhead small enough to be practical.

  • Problem: Large expert-parallel MoE deployments suffer from per-microbatch expert-load skew: hot experts create compute stragglers, token all-to-all bottlenecks, and activation-memory spikes. Historical, periodic balancing is too stale for production-like non-stationary routing patterns.
  • Key contributions: Characterizes non-stationary expert-load imbalance in MoE training and serving prefill at scale. Designs UltraEP, an exact-load real-time balancer for large-EP MoE deployment on rack-scale nodes. Combines quota-based planning with RSN-native expert-state communication, including persistent tile streaming and relay-based fan-out mitigation. Validates near-ideal balancing quality and reports 94.3% of force-balanced ideal throughput, 1.49× improvement over non-balancing, and production-scale validation on 2560 GPUs.
  • Why it matters architecturally: For NVL72-like or CloudMatrix-like scale-up domains, this is a concrete example of using fabric semantics to change the distributed MoE execution plan, not merely accelerate an unchanged all-to-all.
  • Useful diagram: Figures 1–3 are worth inspecting: Figure 1 contrasts stale periodic balancing with UltraEP’s hot-path exact-load balancing, Figure 2 explains the rack-scale node assumption, and Figure 3 visualizes the MoE imbalance that drives the design.
New arXiv CS submission · rack-scale nodes / MoE expert parallelism / scale-up fabric

UltraEP: Unleash MoE Training and Inference on Rack-Scale Nodes with Near-Optimal Load Balancing

Authors & affiliations: Xinming Wei, Chao Jin, Tuo Dai, Yinmin Zhong, Shan Yu, Chengxu Yang, Bingyang Wu, Zili Zhang, Jing Mai, Qianchao Zhu, Zhouyang Li, Yuliang Liu, and Guojie Luo. Affiliations: Peking University; RedNote; Shanghai AI Laboratory; Independent Researcher.

Tags: Scale-up Networking, MoE Systems, AI Clusters

Problem: Large expert-parallel MoE deployments suffer from per-microbatch expert-load skew: hot experts create compute stragglers, token all-to-all bottlenecks, and activation-memory spikes. Historical, periodic balancing is too stale for production-like non-stationary routing patterns.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Characterizes non-stationary expert-load imbalance in MoE training and serving prefill at scale.
  • Designs UltraEP, an exact-load real-time balancer for large-EP MoE deployment on rack-scale nodes.
  • Combines quota-based planning with RSN-native expert-state communication, including persistent tile streaming and relay-based fan-out mitigation.
  • Validates near-ideal balancing quality and reports 94.3% of force-balanced ideal throughput, 1.49× improvement over non-balancing, and production-scale validation on 2560 GPUs.

Why it matters: This is the strongest signal today because it treats rack-scale scale-up connectivity as an algorithmic resource, not just a faster pipe. The paper’s core point is that MoE imbalance is no longer just a scheduler problem: fixing it on the hot path requires co-designing the load-balancing plan with the fabric’s ability to move expert state quickly.

Key insights:

  • RSN-style scale-up domains change the feasible control loop: moving expert state every microbatch/layer is unrealistic on ordinary scale-out links, but can be viable on rack-wide high-bandwidth fabrics.
  • Expert imbalance hits three places at once: GEMM stragglers, token all-to-all imbalance, and activation-memory pressure. A balancer that only optimizes one axis can leave the real bottleneck alive.
  • The authors separate planning cost from expert-state movement cost; both have to fit into an exposed overhead budget of roughly hundreds of microseconds.
  • Relay-based fan-out mitigation is a useful pattern for rack fabrics: avoid naive many-to-many state copies when hot experts are replicated.
  • For production MoE, “load balancing” needs to react to post-gating reality rather than historical popularity estimates.

Actionable lessons: For MoE system design, expose post-gating token counts, expert placement, scale-up link locality, and state-transfer cost to the same control loop. If the fabric is NVLink/NVL72/RSN-like, consider whether expert-state rearrangement can be a hot-path primitive instead of an offline rebalance.

New arXiv CS submission · GPU-native storage / NVMe over RDMA / CPU-bypass data path

GNStor: Design of GPU-Native High-Performance Remote All-Flash Array

Authors & affiliations: Shushu Yi, Wenbo Wu, Guoci Chen, Junrong Zhu, Shengwen Liang, Mao Bo, Chenying Huan, Chen Tian, and Jie Zhang. Affiliations: Peking University; Nanjing University; Institute of Computing Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences; Xiamen University.

Tags: GPU Storage, RDMA, Heterogeneous Infrastructure

Problem: GPU-heavy applications increasingly depend on remote all-flash arrays for datasets, checkpoints, and shared state, but existing GPU-AFA systems still put the CPU in the I/O orchestration path. That creates CPU-GPU interaction overhead and traffic amplification even when GPUDirect is available.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds GNStor, an end-to-end GPU-AFA design that removes the CPU from the remote I/O path.
  • Introduces GNoR, a GPU-centric NVMe-over-RDMA software stack using atomic-operation-based synchronization and a GPU-friendly memory allocator.
  • Designs deEngine, which decentralizes access control and metadata maintenance into SSD firmware to preserve AFA functionality without a centralized CPU engine.
  • Reports 3.2× higher I/O throughput and 31.1% lower application execution time versus state-of-the-art GPU-AFA systems.

Why it matters: This is a very relevant storage/network path paper for AI infrastructure: it makes the GPU the initiator of remote NVMe-over-RDMA I/O and pushes array services closer to SSD firmware. For training and inference systems, that is exactly the CPU-bypass direction you care about when checkpointing, data loading, or remote KV/state access starts competing with compute.

Key insights:

  • GPUDirect data movement alone is not enough if the CPU still rings the doorbells, manages queues, and runs the AFA engine.
  • Remote storage for AI is not just bulk throughput: LLM checkpoints, optimizer states, input corpora, and KV-like shared data have different access granularities and latency/throughput sensitivities.
  • Moving AFA metadata and access-control functions into SSD firmware is the paper’s big architectural bet; it trades centralized control-plane simplicity for a lower-latency data path.
  • A GPU-native NoR path has to respect SIMT execution; the synchronization and allocator design matter as much as the RDMA transport.

Actionable lessons: When designing GPU-centric storage or disaggregated memory paths, audit the entire control path, not only the data DMA path. If the CPU still serializes submission/completion, the NIC/SSD/GPU datapath can look fast in isolation while the end-to-end application remains bottlenecked.

New arXiv CS submission · Slingshot / one-sided communication / RDMA APIs

RAMC: Remote Access Memory Channels over HPE Slingshot

Authors & affiliations: Whit Schonbein, Matthew G. F. Dosanjh, and Scott Levy. Affiliations: Sandia National Laboratories, Albuquerque, New Mexico, USA.

Tags: Transport/Protocols, RDMA, HPC Interconnects

Problem: MPI RMA, OpenSHMEM, and PGAS-style abstractions can be awkward for persistent one-sided communication: they often assume symmetric memory regions, collective setup, or window/epoch semantics that do not match reusable remote-access patterns on modern interconnects.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Develops RAMC, a one-sided communication library built around persistent unidirectional memory channels.
  • Uses HPE Cray Slingshot memory-region counters for lightweight completion notification and synchronization.
  • Evaluates correctness, scalability, and performance, scaling a heat diffusion code to 19.6K processes across 250 nodes.
  • Reports small-message bandwidth improvements over Cray MPI in the tested libfabric versions, while also identifying small-message latency as an area for further work.

Why it matters: This is not an AI paper, but it is relevant to Ethan’s transport/fabric world: it asks what the right user-level abstraction is for repeated remote memory movement on a modern HPC network. AI collectives, checkpointing, and DPU-assisted data movers all face the same “persistent setup, repeated movement, lightweight completion” design problem.

Key insights:

  • The channel abstraction is deliberately narrower than general RMA, which may make it easier to optimize and reason about.
  • The paper emphasizes persistence: amortize registration/setup once, then invoke repeated data movement with low per-operation overhead.
  • Slingshot’s memory-region counters are used as a hardware completion primitive; that is a useful reminder that transport APIs should expose fabric-specific strengths without forcing the application into monolithic shared-memory semantics.
  • The evaluation shows bandwidth gains but also exposes latency caveats, so this is more promising API direction than finished universal replacement.

Actionable lessons: For AI communication runtimes, consider whether some paths need explicit persistent channels rather than generic message passing or heavyweight RMA windows. The abstraction should match stable peer/region reuse in collectives, halo-like exchanges, checkpoint movers, or SmartNIC pull paths.

New arXiv CS submission · edge LLM serving / bandwidth allocation / speculative inference

Multi-SPIN: Multi-Access Speculative Inference for Cooperative Token Generation at the Edge

Authors & affiliations: Haotian Zheng, Zhanwei Wang, Mingyao Cui, Chang Cai, Hongyang Du, and Kaibin Huang. Affiliations: Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, The University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong SAR, China.

Tags: LLM Serving, Edge Networks, Communication-Aware Scheduling

Problem: Speculative inference can offload draft generation to small local models, but in a multi-user edge setting the system becomes constrained by both device compute and uplink bandwidth. Draft length and bandwidth allocation jointly determine token goodput and server-side batching latency.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes Multi-SPIN, a distributed speculative-inference framework where devices generate draft tokens and an edge-hosted LLM verifies batched drafts.
  • Formulates multi-access draft control as joint draft-length and bandwidth allocation under frequency-division multiple access.
  • Derives tractable closed-form/decomposed solutions for homogeneous and heterogeneous draft-length regimes.
  • Evaluates with Llama-2 and Qwen model pairs, reporting up to 88% goodput improvement over heterogeneity-agnostic baselines.

Why it matters: This is lower priority than the cluster-fabric papers, but it is a clean example of communication-aware inference control: the “scheduler” is jointly choosing how much work to do at devices and how much wireless bandwidth to assign before the LLM verifies. The same shape appears in heterogeneous serving and disaggregated inference, just with different links.

Key insights:

  • Uniform draft lengths make server-side batching simpler, but force bandwidth compensation for weak devices to avoid synchronization drag.
  • Heterogeneous draft lengths relax that batching constraint with zero padding and can reward devices with higher acceptance rates.
  • The useful metric is token goodput, not raw tokens generated or raw link throughput; accepted tokens and communication latency are coupled.
  • The paper makes compute/communication heterogeneity explicit through C2 capability, which is the right mental model for mixed accelerator and network environments.

Actionable lessons: In heterogeneous inference systems, choose batching and partitioning parameters jointly with link allocation. Treat draft size, KV/state movement, and bandwidth as one control problem rather than independent serving/runtime knobs.

Useful diagram: The system-model figure around Section II is useful: it shows distributed devices producing SLM drafts, uplinking them over a multi-access channel, and an edge LLM performing batched verification.

Fast take

The useful thread is CPU-bypass and fabric-aware control. UltraEP uses rack-scale scale-up links to move expert state on the hot path; GNStor removes CPU orchestration from GPU-to-remote-flash I/O; RAMC asks for a lighter persistent channel abstraction over Slingshot; Multi-SPIN is a smaller edge-serving example of joint compute/communication control.
