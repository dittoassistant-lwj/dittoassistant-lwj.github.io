AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s batch is mostly infrastructure-adjacent rather than pure fabric: MoE RL post-training needs expert movement and routing foresight, GPU serving needs aging-aware observability, capacity planning needs offered-load curves, and chiplet GPUs remind us that scale-up networking starts inside the package.

Most important paper of the day

Harnessing Routing Foresight for Micro-step-level MoE load balancing in RL Post-training

Authors & affiliations: Yuming Zhou, Haoyang Li, Sheng Lin, Yanfeng Zhao, Tong Zhao, Xupeng Miao, Jie Jiang, Fangcheng Fu, and Bin Cui. Affiliations: Peking University; Shanghai Jiao Tong University; Tencent.

Tags: MoE Training, Heterogeneous AI Infrastructure, Communication/Placement

Why this is the top pick: ForeMoE is the strongest signal because it connects MoE routing, runtime placement, and expert-transfer data paths. It is not just a better balancing heuristic; it treats RL post-training as a staged pipeline where future routing decisions can be observed early and used to schedule expert movement before the imbalance hurts throughput.

Deeper explanation: The key systems idea is that RL post-training breaks the assumptions behind many pretraining-oriented MoE balancers. Step-level statistics look calm, but micro-steps are tiny and bursty, so the useful control loop has to operate at micro-step granularity. ForeMoE exploits the rollout/recompute/policy-update structure: routing choices exposed during rollout become foresight for later stages. That lets the runtime plan expert relocation/replication and move expert state through complementary CPU-assisted and GPU-direct paths while overlapping with computation. For Ethan’s wheelhouse, the paper is useful because it makes expert placement a network-facing runtime decision, not merely a model-level load-loss knob.

  • Problem: MoE RL post-training creates high-frequency expert-load imbalance at the micro-step level: step-level load can look stable, while tiny micro-batches swing enough to underutilize experts and stall the pipeline.
  • Key contributions: Introduces ForeMoE, a micro-step-level load-balancing system that uses routing information from the rollout stage to guide later recompute and policy-update stages. Uses a hierarchical planner to break the NP-hard expert placement/load-balancing problem into tractable subproblems suitable for frequent reconfiguration. Builds an expert transfer engine that combines CPU-assisted and GPU-direct paths and overlaps expert movement with computation. Evaluates on 64 GPUs and reports up to 1.45× speedup over state-of-the-art RL post-training systems.
  • Why it matters architecturally: It points toward MoE runtimes where routing telemetry, placement state, and transfer bandwidth are co-optimized at the same control-loop granularity.
  • Useful diagram: Figure 1 shows the MoE expert-parallel layer and how token routing creates load imbalance. Later figures lay out the RL post-training stages and ForeMoE’s system pipeline, which are useful for understanding where routing foresight enters the runtime.
Fresh arXiv CS paper · MoE RL post-training / expert movement / GPU-direct transfer

Harnessing Routing Foresight for Micro-step-level MoE load balancing in RL Post-training

Authors & affiliations: Yuming Zhou, Haoyang Li, Sheng Lin, Yanfeng Zhao, Tong Zhao, Xupeng Miao, Jie Jiang, Fangcheng Fu, and Bin Cui. Affiliations: Peking University; Shanghai Jiao Tong University; Tencent.

Tags: MoE Training, Heterogeneous AI Infrastructure, Communication/Placement

Problem: MoE RL post-training creates high-frequency expert-load imbalance at the micro-step level: step-level load can look stable, while tiny micro-batches swing enough to underutilize experts and stall the pipeline.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces ForeMoE, a micro-step-level load-balancing system that uses routing information from the rollout stage to guide later recompute and policy-update stages.
  • Uses a hierarchical planner to break the NP-hard expert placement/load-balancing problem into tractable subproblems suitable for frequent reconfiguration.
  • Builds an expert transfer engine that combines CPU-assisted and GPU-direct paths and overlaps expert movement with computation.
  • Evaluates on 64 GPUs and reports up to 1.45× speedup over state-of-the-art RL post-training systems.

Why it matters: This is the most directly useful paper today because it treats MoE load balance as a dynamic systems problem, not just a router-loss problem. The network-facing bit is the expert transfer path: frequent reconfiguration only works if the data movement path is explicit, overlapped, and cheap enough.

Key insights:

  • RL post-training has a different load-balance shape than pretraining: concentrated task domains and small micro-batches make per-micro-step imbalance the real pain point.
  • Future routing information is available in staged RL pipelines; using it converts load balancing from reactive history matching into proactive placement.
  • Expert movement is a first-class data path. The paper’s CPU-assisted plus GPU-direct transfer engine is essentially a placement/communication co-design layer.
  • The design suggests that MoE systems need telemetry at the micro-step and expert-transfer level, not only aggregate per-step token counts.

Actionable lessons: If you are designing MoE training infrastructure, expose expert-placement state, transfer bandwidth, and routing forecasts to the runtime. Treat expert migration like a scheduled communication primitive with overlap windows, rather than an occasional background maintenance operation.

Fresh arXiv CS paper · LLM serving reliability / GPU software stacks / long-run observability

Characterizing Software Aging in GPU-Based LLM Serving Systems

Authors & affiliations: Domenico Cotroneo and Bojan Cukic. Affiliation: College of Computing and Informatics, University of North Carolina at Charlotte.

Tags: LLM Serving, Reliability/Observability, GPU Infrastructure

Problem: LLM serving stacks are long-running GPU-plus-host systems, but software aging studies mostly come from CPU-centric services with steadier workloads. The paper asks how memory growth, latency drift, and stack choices behave over multi-day serving runs.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Runs a 216-hour empirical campaign across GPU-based LLM serving configurations.
  • Compares standalone and Triton-wrapped serving paths, plus different vLLM engine variants, using a shared model/GPU path and monitoring pipeline.
  • Reports process-private memory growth and latency/throughput behavior to separate host-stack effects from GPU execution effects.

Why it matters: This is not a transport paper, but it is operationally relevant for AI clusters: fabric debugging gets messy when the serving stack itself is slowly changing resource state. Aging signals can masquerade as congestion, load imbalance, or GPU variance if they are not measured separately.

Key insights:

  • Different orchestration stacks can have very different private-memory growth even when sharing the same model and GPU.
  • Long-run slopes matter more than short warm-up behavior; an initially noisy service may stabilize while another keeps drifting.
  • For production inference, reliability telemetry should include host process memory, CUDA/GPU state, request mix, and tail latency in the same timeline.
  • The study is a good reminder that “GPU serving performance” is a composite of Python/runtime orchestration, CUDA execution, batching, and wrapper layers.

Actionable lessons: For cluster observability, add aging-specific dashboards: per-process USS/RSS slope, allocator behavior, engine version, wrapper layer, p99 latency drift, and restart impact. Otherwise the network team may get paged for symptoms caused by runtime decay.

Fresh arXiv CS paper · LLM infrastructure economics / concurrency / serving utilization

Beyond Per-Token Pricing: A Concurrency-Aware Methodology for LLM Infrastructure Cost Estimation

Authors & affiliations: Chitral Patil. Affiliation: Independent Researcher; the paper notes the work was conducted independently and does not represent the author’s employer.

Tags: LLM Serving, Infrastructure Modeling, Capacity Planning

Problem: Most LLM cost calculators reduce serving economics to token price or assumed GPU utilization. That hides the real systems variable: offered load and concurrency determine whether expensive accelerators are saturated or stranded.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Defines an effective cost methodology that ties cost per output token to offered load, measured throughput, and latency/SLA feasibility.
  • Evaluates six H100 serving configurations across dense and MoE models, including FP16/FP8 comparisons.
  • Shows that underutilization can dominate economics: effective cost spans roughly $0.21 to $15.25 per million output tokens on identical H100 hardware in the paper’s setup.

Why it matters: For Ethan’s world, the useful part is the capacity-planning lens: fabric, batching, and serving topology choices should be evaluated at realistic arrival rates, not at imaginary 100% utilization. A network design that improves saturation at SLA can be worth more than one that only improves peak tokens/sec.

Key insights:

  • The cost curve drops sharply as offered load rises, then flattens near saturation; using one fixed utilization number loses the important regime transition.
  • SLA-feasible cost can differ from unconstrained saturation cost because tail latency caps the usable load.
  • FP8 appears especially valuable for the evaluated MoE configurations, not just dense models, because it increases effective throughput under serving load.
  • Good serving economics requires measuring concurrency, arrival rate, output throughput, TTFT, and TPOT together.

Actionable lessons: When comparing serving fabrics or disaggregated deployments, plot cost and p99 latency versus offered load. Do not use peak throughput alone; the design target is the cheapest SLA-feasible operating point.

Useful diagram: The most useful figures are cost-curve plots rather than an architecture block diagram: Figure 1 plots effective cost versus offered load, and later figures show underutilization penalty plus SLA-feasible cost floors.

Fresh arXiv CS paper · chiplet GPUs / scale-up locality / HBM placement

Making Locality-aware GEMM Compatible with Page-Granularity Placement on Chiplet GPUs

Authors & affiliations: Euijun Chung, Jae Hyung Ju, and Hyesoon Kim. Affiliations: not clearly listed in the arXiv metadata/HTML/PDF text available from arXiv.

Tags: Scale-up Architecture, GPU Memory Systems, Chiplet Interconnects

Problem: Multi-chiplet GPUs expose non-uniform HBM locality: remote HBM access across the interposer burns latency, power, and shared bandwidth. Locality-aware GEMM wants data near the chiplets that consume it, but conventional page-granularity placement can break that mapping.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Analyzes how GEMM shape, operand layout, CTA scheduling, and memory placement interact on multi-chiplet GPUs.
  • Identifies cases where page-granularity placement cannot realize the locality pattern implied by the GEMM computation.
  • Proposes a chiplet-contiguous layout approach to make locality-aware GEMM compatible with page-level placement constraints.

Why it matters: This is scale-up fabric signal. Even before traffic leaves the node, locality policy on chiplet GPUs changes pressure on the interposer and HBM fabric. For NVL72-like or future chiplet accelerators, “networking” increasingly starts inside the package.

Key insights:

  • For LLM GEMMs, the right placement strategy depends on operand layout and whether the operation is forward, activation-gradient, or weight-gradient.
  • Remote HBM traffic is not just a memory-system issue; it consumes shared inter-chiplet bandwidth and can interfere with other traffic classes.
  • Page size and placement granularity are architectural constraints that can erase algorithmic locality unless the layout is co-designed with the kernel.
  • Locality-aware scheduling should be evaluated at the GEMM-shape level, not only at model-level averages.

Actionable lessons: For scale-up architecture studies, track remote-HBM bytes per GEMM and expose page-placement constraints in the simulator. A chiplet topology that looks balanced at bandwidth aggregate may still underperform if placement granularity fights the kernel’s access pattern.

Fast take

ForeMoE is the paper I’d read first: it is the most systems-facing and has the clearest runtime/data-movement hook. The software-aging paper is useful for production observability, especially when network symptoms may actually be runtime drift. The cost-estimation paper gives a practical capacity-planning lens, and the chiplet-GPU locality paper is a good scale-up reminder that interconnect pressure begins before traffic reaches NICs or NVLink-scale fabrics.
