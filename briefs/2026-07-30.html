AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s batch is unusually close to Ethan-core: scale-up supernode communication, MoE traffic shaping at the NIC, photonic/CXL memory for KV cache, and cross-layer fabric policy. The common thread is that AI infrastructure is moving from host-orchestrated networking toward fabric-native control.

Most important paper of the day

StrataCL: Fabric-Native Communication Library for Production Supernodes

Authors & affiliations: Tiancheng Hu, Jin Qin, Yuzheng Wang, Ke Liu, TangShengsheng Li, Sheng Wang, Zhongzhe Hu, Tianlun Hu, Wei Wang, Lijun Li, Jingbin Zhou, Xiaoming Bao, Hongwei Sun, Jieru Zhao, Huimin Cui, Tao Xie, and Chenxi Wang. Affiliations: Peking University; SKLP, Institute of Computing Technology, CAS; University of Chinese Academy of Sciences; Shanghai Jiao Tong University; Huawei Technologies Company Ltd.

Tags: Scale-up Networking, Collective Communication, AI Supernodes

Why this is the top pick: StrataCL is the strongest signal because it is not a generic “make collectives faster” paper; it is a production-supernode communication-library design for CloudMatrix384-class systems. It directly targets the pain point in NVL72/CloudMatrix-like fabrics: once remote HBM access is fast enough, redundant staging copies, registration latency, NPU-core synchronization, and compute-communication interference become first-order bottlenecks.

Deeper explanation: The mental model is that scale-up fabrics change the communication-library contract. In a traditional RDMA scale-out cluster, user-buffer registration is expensive and internal communication buffers are a pragmatic compromise. In a supernode with a unified physical address space and near-HBM remote access, those extra copies become architectural tax. StrataCL’s answer is registration-on-allocation plus fabric-native operators that use full-mesh programming, workload-balanced NPU-core partitioning, and NPU-driven SDMA offload. The important design point is not just bandwidth; it is keeping communication from stealing the accelerator cores and launch windows needed by overlapped compute.

  • Problem: Modern AI jobs on hundreds of accelerators are communication-bound, but existing libraries often separate user buffers from communication buffers, creating redundant HBM copies or costly registration paths that are especially painful under dynamic MoE and serving allocation patterns.
  • Key contributions: Proposes StrataCL, a zero-redundancy, fabric-native communication library for production supernodes.; Introduces registration-on-allocation to make user-buffer direct communication transparent and off the critical path.; Designs supernode-specific operators using full-mesh communication, balanced NPU-core partitioning, and NPU-driven SDMA offload; reports up to 1.6× collective bus-bandwidth improvement, 1.4× MoE dispatch/combine bandwidth improvement, 1.9× LLM inference throughput, and 2.2× lower P99 TTFT.
  • Why it matters architecturally: In scale-up AI pods, treat remote-memory semantics and buffer-registration policy as part of the collective design, not as allocator plumbing. Once the fabric is fast, software data movement and core contention can dominate.

StrataCL: Fabric-Native Communication Library for Production Supernodes

Authors & affiliations: Tiancheng Hu, Jin Qin, Yuzheng Wang, Ke Liu, TangShengsheng Li, Sheng Wang, Zhongzhe Hu, Tianlun Hu, Wei Wang, Lijun Li, Jingbin Zhou, Xiaoming Bao, Hongwei Sun, Jieru Zhao, Huimin Cui, Tao Xie, and Chenxi Wang. Affiliations: Peking University; SKLP, Institute of Computing Technology, CAS; University of Chinese Academy of Sciences; Shanghai Jiao Tong University; Huawei Technologies Company Ltd.

Tags: Scale-up Networking, Collective Communication, AI Supernodes

Problem: Modern AI jobs on hundreds of accelerators are communication-bound, but existing libraries often separate user buffers from communication buffers, creating redundant HBM copies or costly registration paths that are especially painful under dynamic MoE and serving allocation patterns.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes StrataCL, a zero-redundancy, fabric-native communication library for production supernodes.
  • Introduces registration-on-allocation to make user-buffer direct communication transparent and off the critical path.
  • Designs supernode-specific operators using full-mesh communication, balanced NPU-core partitioning, and NPU-driven SDMA offload; reports up to 1.6× collective bus-bandwidth improvement, 1.4× MoE dispatch/combine bandwidth improvement, 1.9× LLM inference throughput, and 2.2× lower P99 TTFT.

Why it matters: StrataCL is the strongest signal because it is not a generic “make collectives faster” paper; it is a production-supernode communication-library design for CloudMatrix384-class systems. It directly targets the pain point in NVL72/CloudMatrix-like fabrics: once remote HBM access is fast enough, redundant staging copies, registration latency, NPU-core synchronization, and compute-communication interference become first-order bottlenecks.

Key insights:

  • Scale-up fabrics change the bottleneck order: after UB/NVLink-like links make remote accelerator memory cheap, internal-buffer copies can become a larger fraction of latency than the wire itself.
  • Registration-on-allocation is a clean systems trick: push registration work into the allocator path so dynamic user buffers can communicate directly without a millisecond-scale registration cliff on the critical path.
  • NPU-driven SDMA offload matters because communication kernels otherwise consume accelerator cores and interfere with overlapped compute.
  • The paper evaluates real production applications on CloudMatrix384 with up to 512 NPU dies, making this more grounded than a pure simulator or single-node microbenchmark.

Actionable lessons: For supernode collectives, profile HBM staging traffic and accelerator-core occupancy, not only link utilization. If remote memory is fast enough, the library should make user-buffer direct communication the default path and reserve accelerator cores for actual model work.

Incast-Free MoE Rate-Based Scheduling

Authors & affiliations: Evyatar Cohen, Jose Yallouz, Alexander Shpiner, Mark Silberstein, Sylvia Ratnasamy, and Isaac Keslassy. Affiliations: Technion; NVIDIA; UC Berkeley.

Tags: MoE Communication, Transport/Protocols, NIC Scheduling

Problem: MoE expert traffic is skewed, and decentralized round-robin flow scheduling can drain cold expert queues early, leaving many senders converging on hot receivers near the end of an epoch. The authors show this creates an exponential incast pattern that reactive congestion control can only throttle after the fabric is already oversubscribed.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Identifies and models an MoE-specific exponential incast failure mode under round-robin scheduling.
  • Proposes a proactive fair scheduler that normalizes the demand matrix by worst-case sender/receiver demand, assigning rates that prevent any sender uplink or receiver downlink from exceeding physical line rate.
  • Outlines a NIC implementation path using programmable congestion-control support such as NVIDIA DOCA PCC, and evaluates 64×64 to 512×512 GPU-scale workloads with real Qwen2/Qwen3 and synthetic DeepSeek-V3-style MoE matrices.

Why it matters: This is very Ethan-relevant because it treats MoE all-to-all as a transport problem, not just a model-parallel runtime detail. The key shift is proactive rate allocation from the token-to-expert demand matrix, instead of relying on packet-level congestion signals after queues have already formed.

Key insights:

  • The failure mode is structural: independent round-robin senders can synchronize on hot receivers even without malicious traffic or obvious path imbalance.
  • Reactive congestion control protects buffers, but can over-correct and leave links underutilized, inflating collective completion time.
  • The proposed scheduler effectively converts a dynamic MoE scheduling problem into a static demand-guided rate matrix per epoch.
  • The design deliberately abstracts away intra-node NVLink-style traffic and focuses on inter-server traffic, which is the right cut for NIC/fabric control.

Actionable lessons: For expert-parallel communication, expose the routing matrix to the network scheduler before the all-to-all starts. If the NIC only sees independent flows, it may be too late to distinguish healthy skew from impending incast.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is the useful diagnostic plot: it shows the most-requested receiver’s queue growing exponentially near the end of a round-robin MoE scheduling epoch. I did not see a single architecture diagram; this paper is more mechanism/model plus simulation.

verified arXiv paper · photonic interconnects, cxl memory, llm serving

A Photonic-CXL Memory Appliance for Scalable KV Cache Management in LLM Inference

Authors & affiliations: Jing Ding, Yash Nishant, Chandrish Ambati, Jyothsna Kamati, and Trung Diep. Affiliations: Marvell Technology, Santa Clara, USA.

Tags: Photonic Interconnects, CXL Memory, LLM Serving

Problem: Long-context LLM serving needs both very large KV-cache capacity and high retrieval bandwidth. GPU HBM is fast but capacity-limited, host DRAM helps but supports only limited concurrency, SSD is too slow for many short/mid contexts, and electrical CXL pooling faces switch latency, reach, and scaling limits.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Characterizes the KV-cache memory wall across GPU HBM, host memory, and disk tiers using multi-generation GPU systems and LLaMA models.
  • Proposes the Marvell Photonic Fabric Memory Appliance, a photonic-CXL hybrid that uses a passive fiber shuffle and switch-free full-crossbar topology to provide 32 TB shared memory across 16 hosts.
  • Reports over 50% latency reduction versus electrical CXL pools in hardware emulation and a 6.6× TTFT improvement for multi-turn conversation workloads in serving simulation.

Why it matters: This is a concrete attempt to make KV cache a rack/supernode memory-fabric problem. The interesting part is not just “bigger memory”; it is replacing an electrical switch hierarchy with a passive photonic shuffle so CXL-like memory pooling can scale capacity without adding switch-latency cliffs.

Key insights:

  • The paper frames KV-cache serving as a capacity-bandwidth product problem: tens of TB and hundreds of GB/s are both needed, and today’s tiers usually provide only one side.
  • Host-memory retrieval can be dramatically faster than recomputation, but concurrency breaks the simple “offload KV to CPU DRAM” story.
  • Electrical CXL is not assumed to be free; switch latency, cable reach, and power scaling are treated as deployment blockers.
  • The passive photonic crossbar is attractive because it shifts complexity out of active packet/switch scheduling and into deterministic optical connectivity.

Actionable lessons: For disaggregated KV-cache designs, evaluate the memory tier by capacity, bandwidth, latency, and fan-out reach together. A CXL pool that works for a few hosts may hit a switch-latency or cable-reach wall before it reaches LLM-serving scale.

verified arXiv paper · gpu-cxl fabrics, ebpf, dpu/nic policy

The Fabric Is the Cluster Driver: Cross-Layer eBPF Policies for GPU-CXL Fabrics

Authors & affiliations: Yiwei Yang and Andi Quinn. Affiliations: UC Santa Cruz, USA.

Tags: GPU-CXL Fabrics, eBPF, DPU/NIC Policy

Problem: Modern LLM serving paths span GPU execution, RDMA/DPU/NIC queues, CXL memory placement, KV-cache movement, MoE all-to-all, and checkpoint traffic, but today’s control planes split these signals across layers that cannot see each other’s semantics.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Defines fabric_ext, a cross-device eBPF policy system that compiles semantic movement graphs into hooks across GPUs, drivers/runtimes, DPUs/NICs, and CXL switch or near-memory execution points.
  • Introduces consistency-classed BPF maps and a cross-device effect verifier for policies spanning SIMT GPU execution and event-driven fabric handlers.
  • Implements a prototype in Damer using dputime and evaluates 1,000 generated movement microbenchmarks plus 14 LLM fabric workloads, reporting modeled data-movement speedups when policy decisions move closer to the fabric.

Why it matters: This is more conceptual than StrataCL, but it points at a real gap in AI cluster control: the GPU knows whether a transfer is KV, MoE, checkpoint, or decode-critical; the NIC and CXL fabric know queue pressure and placement state. A useful future stack probably needs a safe contract between those worlds.

Key insights:

  • The paper’s central abstraction is a semantic movement graph: not just bytes from A to B, but bytes with phase, stride, reuse distance, ordering, ownership, and transformations such as reduce, compress, checksum, scatter/gather, replicate, or persist.
  • The “fabric as driver” idea is strongest when the policy decision can be made at the nearest safe hook rather than bouncing through the host CPU.
  • The verifier is important because cross-layer fabric policies can otherwise create GPU-fabric dependency cycles or unsafe waits on completions triggered by the same handler.
  • The evaluation is modeled/prototype-heavy, so I would treat the paper as an architecture direction rather than proof of production wins.

Actionable lessons: Design observability and policy APIs so the fabric can see workload semantics without giving arbitrary code unsafe control of the data path. “QP priority” is too blunt for KV fetches, MoE critical tokens, and checkpoint traffic sharing the same fabric.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is the architecture diagram: one logical eBPF policy is split across GPU hooks, driver/runtime hooks, DPU/NIC hooks, and CXL switch/near-memory handlers while bulk data stays on RDMA, GPUDirect, and CXL paths.

verified arXiv paper · rdma, disaggregated memory, database systems

Two-sided RDMA Striking Back for Disaggregated Memory Databaases

Authors & affiliations: Hokeun Cha, Aditya Akella, and Xiangyao Yu. Affiliations: University of Wisconsin-Madison; University of Texas at Austin.

Tags: RDMA, Disaggregated Memory, Database Systems

Problem: Disaggregated-memory databases often favor one-sided RDMA to avoid remote CPU involvement, but one-sided verbs are weak at expressing system functions like starvation prevention, priority scheduling, preemption, and multi-step indexing without network amplification.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Revisits the one-sided versus two-sided RDMA trade-off specifically for disaggregated-memory databases.
  • Builds Lotus, a two-sided RDMA design that uses limited memory-server CPU resources intentionally, with lightweight caching and intra/inter-transaction batching to reduce remote round trips.
  • Evaluates Lotus against one-sided RDMA indexing and concurrency-control baselines, reporting up to 8.2× higher throughput and 42.9× lower p999 latency on YCSB workloads.

Why it matters: This is not AI-specific, but it is relevant to remote-memory and storage paths in heterogeneous AI infrastructure. The useful lesson is that “avoid remote CPU at all costs” can be the wrong rule when the operation needs richer scheduling semantics than one-sided RDMA exposes.

Key insights:

  • One-sided RDMA is excellent for simple remote memory access, but complex concurrency-control behavior can become awkward or network-amplifying.
  • Two-sided RDMA can win when server CPU work replaces multiple network round trips or enables starvation-free scheduling.
  • Batching is the hidden lever: if a transaction can aggregate requests, the cost of involving the memory-server CPU is amortized.
  • The paper separates non-partitioned and co-partitioned memory-disaggregation models, which is a useful lens for AI remote-memory/KV systems too.

Actionable lessons: For AI remote-memory, KV, or disaggregated storage designs, choose RDMA verbs based on operation semantics, not dogma. One-sided is great for raw get/put; two-sided may be better when fairness, preemption, batching, or compound operations matter.
