Revisiting Bruck: Phase-Efficient All-to-All Collective Communication in Reconfigurable Networks

Authors & affiliations: Anton Juerss and Stefan Schmid. Affiliations: Anton Juerss: Weizenbaum Institute & TU Berlin; Stefan Schmid: TU Berlin & Weizenbaum Institute.

Tags: Collectives, Optical/Reconfigurable Networks, Scale-Up Fabrics

Why this is the top pick: This is the strongest signal today because it is directly about all-to-all on scale-up interconnects, with MoE dispatch explicitly in the motivation. The paper’s useful move is not “use optics”; it is matching the collective’s phase structure to what an optical circuit fabric can actually reconfigure efficiently.

Deeper explanation: ReTri is interesting because it treats collective communication as a joint algorithm/fabric object. In an optical reconfigurable network, the expensive operation is not just sending bytes; it is stopping communication long enough to install a topology. The paper’s core design response is to expose fewer, structured, bidirectional phases so each optical state carries enough all-to-all work to matter. That framing is directly relevant to MoE dispatch/combine in scale-up domains: the right question is whether the collective schedule gives the fabric enough regularity to exploit, not merely whether the fabric has high peak bandwidth.

  • Problem: All-to-all collectives are becoming a scale-up bottleneck for ML/HPC workloads, especially MoE-style dense exchange; optical reconfigurable networks can reshape topology at runtime, but only if the collective exposes sparse phases that amortize reconfiguration delay.
  • Key contributions:
  • Revisits Bruck-style All-to-All for optical reconfigurable networks and argues that collective schedule and topology-reconfiguration strategy must be co-designed.
  • Introduces ReTri, a bidirectional All-to-All schedule based on ternary block propagation, completing in ⌈log3 n⌉ phases with two optical transceivers per endpoint.
  • Reports preliminary simulations where ReTri improves completion time by up to 10× over Pairwise All-to-All and up to 2.1× over reconfigurable Bruck under the paper’s modeled settings.
  • Why it matters architecturally: For superpod collective design, do not evaluate OCS/photonic fabrics only with bulk traffic matrices. Co-design dispatch/combine schedules so each reconfiguration state is reusable, bidirectional, and dense enough to amortize switching delay; otherwise the optical layer becomes a very expensive pause button.

Revisiting Bruck: Phase-Efficient All-to-All Collective Communication in Reconfigurable Networks

Authors & affiliations: Anton Juerss and Stefan Schmid. Affiliations: Anton Juerss: Weizenbaum Institute & TU Berlin; Stefan Schmid: TU Berlin & Weizenbaum Institute.

Tags: Collectives, Optical/Reconfigurable Networks, Scale-Up Fabrics

Problem: All-to-all collectives are becoming a scale-up bottleneck for ML/HPC workloads, especially MoE-style dense exchange; optical reconfigurable networks can reshape topology at runtime, but only if the collective exposes sparse phases that amortize reconfiguration delay.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Revisits Bruck-style All-to-All for optical reconfigurable networks and argues that collective schedule and topology-reconfiguration strategy must be co-designed.
  • Introduces ReTri, a bidirectional All-to-All schedule based on ternary block propagation, completing in ⌈log3 n⌉ phases with two optical transceivers per endpoint.
  • Reports preliminary simulations where ReTri improves completion time by up to 10× over Pairwise All-to-All and up to 2.1× over reconfigurable Bruck under the paper’s modeled settings.

Why it matters: This is the strongest signal today because it is directly about all-to-all on scale-up interconnects, with MoE dispatch explicitly in the motivation. The paper’s useful move is not “use optics”; it is matching the collective’s phase structure to what an optical circuit fabric can actually reconfigure efficiently.

Key insights:

  • The ORN win depends on phase reuse: a topology state has to carry enough useful traffic to pay back the global pause/reconfiguration cost.
  • Pairwise All-to-All is a poor fit for reconfigurable fabrics because it creates many skinny phases with little structure to amortize.
  • Bidirectional use of optical circuits matters; otherwise a nominally high-bandwidth topology can waste half of the available circuit directionality.
  • The paper treats scale-up domains as networks with real topology and port constraints, not as abstract fully connected collectives.
  • For MoE, the implication is that algorithmic phase structure may be as important as raw optical bandwidth.

Actionable lessons: For superpod collective design, do not evaluate OCS/photonic fabrics only with bulk traffic matrices. Co-design dispatch/combine schedules so each reconfiguration state is reusable, bidirectional, and dense enough to amortize switching delay; otherwise the optical layer becomes a very expensive pause button.

Short-circuiting Rings for Low-Latency AllReduce

Authors & affiliations: Sarah-Michelle Hammer, Stefan Schmid, Rachee Singh, and Vamsi Addanki. Affiliations: Sarah-Michelle Hammer and Stefan Schmid: TU Berlin; Rachee Singh: Cornell University; Vamsi Addanki: Purdue University.

Tags: AllReduce, Photonic Interconnects, Collective Cost Models

Problem: The usual Hockney-style intuition says Recursive Doubling should beat Ring AllReduce for small messages, but that ignores physical path length, propagation delay, and congestion on ring-based GPU-to-GPU topologies.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Shows that on physical ring topologies, Ring AllReduce can remain optimal even for short messages once propagation delay and congestion are modeled.
  • Explains why Recursive Doubling’s fewer logical rounds can be offset by longer paths and higher link congestion.
  • Proposes a circuit-switching heuristic that lets Recursive Doubling short-circuit ring paths when photonic reconfiguration delay is low enough, while falling back to static Ring when it is not.

Why it matters: This paper is a good antidote to abstract collective folklore. It says the winner is not determined by the number of rounds alone; the physical fabric can flip the answer through hop count and congestion.

Key insights:

  • Logical step count is a dangerous proxy for latency when the physical topology is not fully connected.
  • Recursive Doubling trades fewer rounds for longer paths, so its propagation term can sum to the same order as Ring while its congestion term gets worse.
  • Reconfigurable photonic shortcuts create a middle path: keep Recursive Doubling’s communication structure, but alter the physical topology at collective-round granularity.
  • The hard parameter is reconfiguration delay; too high, and the safe answer is still Ring.

Actionable lessons: When tuning GPU collectives, include propagation and path-congestion terms in the model, especially for scale-up rings/rails. If a fabric supports circuit switching, expose a policy knob that chooses per-collective between static Ring and short-circuited schedules based on message size and reconfiguration latency.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is useful: it shows Astra-Sim results where Recursive Doubling can be slower than Ring on a 16-GPU 800 Gbps ring, grounding the counterintuitive claim in a concrete network simulation.

Flowcut Switching: High-Performance Adaptive Routing with In-Order Delivery Guarantees

Authors & affiliations: Tommaso Bonato, Daniele De Sensi, Salvatore Di Girolamo, Abdulla Bataineh, David Hewson, Duncan Roweth, and Torsten Hoefler. Affiliations: Tommaso Bonato, Salvatore Di Girolamo, and Torsten Hoefler: ETH Zurich; Daniele De Sensi: Sapienza University of Rome; Abdulla Bataineh, David Hewson, and Duncan Roweth: HPE.

Tags: Adaptive Routing, RDMA/RoCE, Datacenter Transport

Problem: Per-packet adaptive routing improves utilization but can reorder packets; flowlet/flowcell schemes reduce reordering but depend on burst gaps and do not guarantee in-order delivery, which is painful for RoCE, TCP, and QUIC.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces Flowcut switching, which reroutes a flow only after prior in-flight bytes for that flow have drained, guaranteeing in-order delivery under the model.
  • Shows three deployment options: full switch state, ingress-only state, and NIC-only state, giving different memory/performance trade-offs.
  • Reports simulation results with up to 50% lower FCT versus ECMP, up to 40% improvement over conservative flowlet switching, and strong behavior under failures.

Why it matters: This is relevant to AI fabrics because RDMA traffic is often smooth and rate-limited, exactly the regime where flowlets are starved of natural gaps. Flowcut is a clean attempt to get adaptive routing without handing the transport an out-of-order grenade.

Key insights:

  • The paper attacks the classic trilemma: per-packet adaptivity, in-order delivery, and low switch/NIC state.
  • RDMA/RoCE differs from bursty TCP traffic; fewer natural flowlet gaps make timeout-based flowlet switching less effective.
  • A drain-before-reroute rule can preserve ordering while still reacting to congestion and failures, at the cost of explicit in-flight accounting.
  • The NIC-only option is architecturally interesting for AI clusters because it avoids requiring every switch ASIC to keep rich per-flow state.

Actionable lessons: For RoCE AI clusters, treat packet reordering as a first-class transport/fabric interface problem. If you want adaptive multipath, design the reroute granularity around receiver ordering and NIC capabilities, not just average link utilization.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is useful because it shows how the “right” flowlet timeout varies across workloads and failures; it visually motivates why fixed timeout flowlets are brittle.

Distributed Training under Packet Loss

Authors & affiliations: Erez Weintraub, Ron Banner, and Ariel Orda. Affiliations: not clearly listed in the arXiv metadata/PDF.

Tags: Distributed Training, Loss-Tolerant Transport, Gradient Aggregation

Problem: Large model training stacks typically assume reliable links such as InfiniBand or RoCE; relaxing reliability could cut tail latency and ACK/retransmission overhead, but packet loss can corrupt gradients or cause model drift.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes a distributed training framework intended to operate over unreliable connections while preserving unbiased gradient aggregation.
  • Adds bounded-drift parameter broadcasts so workers do not diverge indefinitely even with repeated missing messages.
  • Reports experiments on Llama 2 7B with 64 GPUs where 10% random packet loss changes perplexity by at most 0.8% under the paper’s setup.

Why it matters: This is less polished as a systems artifact, but the question is exactly in Ethan territory: how much reliability does training actually need, and which correctness guarantees belong in the communication layer versus the optimizer/training algorithm?

Key insights:

  • The core idea is to make missing packets statistically safe rather than forcing the fabric/transport to pretend loss never happens.
  • Unbiased aggregation and bounded drift attack two different failure modes: gradient bias and inter-worker model divergence.
  • If the approach holds under more realistic correlated loss and collective patterns, it could loosen the reliability contract for some training traffic classes.
  • The current evidence should be treated as early: random packet loss is easier than congestion-correlated loss or incast-driven drops.

Actionable lessons: Worth testing as a thought experiment against production transport design: identify which tensors/steps can tolerate statistical reconstruction and which require strict reliability. Do not generalize from random loss to RoCE congestion loss without a harsher correlated-loss experiment.

Useful diagram: No obvious architecture diagram was found in the first pages; the useful content is mainly the loss-tolerant aggregation and drift analysis.

verified arXiv paper · disaggregated llm serving, kv cache, provisioning models

Analytical Provisioning for Attention-FFN Disaggregated LLM Serving under Stochastic Workloads

Authors & affiliations: Chendong Song, Meixuan Wang, Hang Zhou, Hong Liang, Yuan Lyu, Zixi Chen, Yuwei Fan, and Zijie Zhou. Affiliations: Chendong Song, Hang Zhou, and Zijie Zhou: HKUST, Hong Kong, China; Meixuan Wang, Hong Liang, Yuan Lyu, Zixi Chen, and Yuwei Fan: Huawei Hong Kong Research Center, China.

Tags: Disaggregated LLM Serving, KV Cache, Provisioning Models

Problem: Attention–FFN disaggregation separates KV-cache-heavy attention from stateless FFN compute, but the Attention/FFN ratio is sensitive to stochastic prompt/decode lengths, synchronization barriers, and per-layer activation communication.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds a renewal-reward workload model for AFD serving under continuous batching and request replenishment.
  • Derives a closed-form mean-field provisioning rule with Attention-, communication-, and FFN-bottleneck regimes plus a Gaussian barrier-aware correction.
  • Validates with a trace-calibrated AFD simulator whose predicted optimal A/F ratio is within 10% of the simulation optimum.

Why it matters: This is adjacent rather than pure networking, but useful because AFD makes per-token inter-device communication part of the decode critical path. The provisioning ratio is therefore a network-facing systems knob, not just a queueing parameter.

Key insights:

  • AFD creates a three-stage decode loop: Attention, activation communication, and FFN; the slowest stage determines useful provisioning.
  • The Attention side is stochastic because KV cache length evolves with each request, while FFN is more batch-size driven.
  • Barrier effects across Attention workers can waste FFN capacity even when mean load looks balanced.
  • The paper explicitly leaves real-system validation to future work, so the result is best used as a sizing model or simulator check.

Actionable lessons: For disaggregated decode, provision by regimes rather than average GPU utilization. Measure activation-transfer cost and barrier skew separately; a nominally compute-rich FFN pool can still idle if Attention workers arrive unevenly or the A↔F path is underprovisioned.
