Budgeted Express-Mesh: Traffic-Aware Link Placement and Deadlock-Free Adaptive Routing

Authors & affiliations: Li Cao and Jingyuan Ma — Institute for Interdisciplinary Information Sciences (IIIS), Tsinghua University, Beijing, China.

Tags: Scale-up/NoC Topology, Adaptive Routing, Traffic-Aware Fabrics

Why this is the top pick: This is the most Ethan-shaped paper today: topology, routing, congestion signals, deadlock freedom, and scarce long-link budget all in one compact NoC design problem. It is not a datacenter fabric, but the same “where do I spend expensive connectivity?” question shows up in GPU scale-up domains.

Deeper explanation: Budgeted Express-Mesh is useful because it makes the scarce-resource version of fabric design explicit. The paper does not simply add links; it asks which long links are worth their wire budget, how to route onto them without creating new hotspots, and how to preserve deadlock freedom while reacting to imperfect congestion signals.

  • Problem: A regular mesh is layout-friendly, but traffic concentrates on a few rows, columns, or cuts; adding shortcuts only helps if placement and routing avoid turning the shortcuts into new hotspots.
  • Key contributions:
  • Defines Budgeted Express-Mesh: add a small set of bidirectional express links under wire-length, degree, and minimum-length constraints.
  • Combines ASPL-guided greedy placement, simulation-guided annealing, and committed top-K routing driven by delayed express-link congestion and reservation signals.
  • Evaluates multiple synthetic traffic patterns and robustness settings, reporting improved high-load throughput over mesh and random placements, with gains surviving quantized/delayed congestion signals, longer express-link latency, multi-flit packets, and a 16×16 heterogeneous workload.
  • Why it matters architecturally: For GPU/XPU scale-up domains, long wires, high-radix ports, and optical/electrical shortcuts are expensive. This paper is a compact reminder that the best topology is workload-shaped and only as good as the adaptive routing/control loop riding on it.
  • Useful diagram: Figure 1 is useful: it visualizes the evaluated express-link topologies as red shortcuts over the mesh. Figures 2–5 then show throughput–latency Pareto curves under different traffic patterns, which makes the topology/routing trade-off concrete.

Budgeted Express-Mesh: Traffic-Aware Link Placement and Deadlock-Free Adaptive Routing

Authors & affiliations: Li Cao and Jingyuan Ma — Institute for Interdisciplinary Information Sciences (IIIS), Tsinghua University, Beijing, China.

Tags: Scale-up/NoC Topology, Adaptive Routing, Traffic-Aware Fabrics

Problem: A regular mesh is layout-friendly, but traffic concentrates on a few rows, columns, or cuts; adding shortcuts only helps if placement and routing avoid turning the shortcuts into new hotspots.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Defines Budgeted Express-Mesh: add a small set of bidirectional express links under wire-length, degree, and minimum-length constraints.
  • Combines ASPL-guided greedy placement, simulation-guided annealing, and committed top-K routing driven by delayed express-link congestion and reservation signals.
  • Evaluates multiple synthetic traffic patterns and robustness settings, reporting improved high-load throughput over mesh and random placements, with gains surviving quantized/delayed congestion signals, longer express-link latency, multi-flit packets, and a 16×16 heterogeneous workload.

Why it matters: This is the most Ethan-shaped paper today: topology, routing, congestion signals, deadlock freedom, and scarce long-link budget all in one compact NoC design problem. It is not a datacenter fabric, but the same “where do I spend expensive connectivity?” question shows up in GPU scale-up domains.

Key insights:

  • The core lesson is that topology and routing are coupled. A good express link can relieve a cut, but only if the runtime route selector does not herd traffic into the new link.
  • The paper separates static graph quality from dynamic routing quality, which is the right evaluation pattern for scale-up fabrics too.
  • The use of delayed and quantized congestion/reservation signals is useful because real fabrics rarely expose perfect, instantaneous state.
  • The reserved deterministic mesh-XY escape VC is a clean reminder: adaptive routing needs an explicit progress path, not just clever path choice.

Actionable lessons: For accelerator fabrics, treat long links or high-radix ports as a budgeted resource tied to known traffic matrices. Evaluate placement and routing together, and always keep a simple escape path when introducing adaptive choices.

Ask the Tool, Don't Guess: Agent Tool Calls Hold Their Progress, and the Serving System Should Read It

Authors & affiliations: Yipeng Liu, Yingqiang Zhang, Feifei Li, and Huanchen Zhang — Tsinghua University; Zhejiang University; Alibaba Cloud Computing.

Tags: LLM Serving, KV Cache Management, Agent Infrastructure

Problem: Agentic requests often wait on tools while their KV cache occupies GPU memory. Current serving systems decide cache retention, eviction, or reload timing by guessing tool duration before the tool actually runs.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Shows why static predictors, declared durations, and historical tool timings fail for long-running tool calls: the relevant information appears during the call, not before it.
  • Builds a harness that exposes tool progress without changing what the agent sees, classifying progress signals across public agent corpora.
  • Feeds progress hints into a serving engine and reports roughly 20.7–20.8% lower p90 TTFT after tool calls versus LRU, close to an oracle policy, with safeguards that treat tool reports as untrusted input.

Why it matters: This paper is very relevant to inference platforms because it connects agent runtime semantics to GPU memory/KV-cache control. It argues that the serving layer should consume live progress signals rather than infer them indirectly from request history.

Key insights:

  • The cache decision point is between model turns, but the best signal is inside the tool call. That is a cross-layer interface problem, not only a scheduling heuristic problem.
  • Long tool calls dominate waiting time, so improving their retention/reload decisions can move tail TTFT even if most calls are short.
  • Progress signals need trust boundaries. The paper’s session-credit idea is useful because clients should not be able to lie their way into unlimited cache priority.
  • The mechanism resembles congestion feedback: weak but timely in-flight state beats polished stale estimates.

Actionable lessons: If building agent serving, expose structured progress from tool sandboxes to the serving plane. Cache policy should know “near completion” or “fraction remaining,” and should couple that signal with quota/credit controls.

verified arXiv paper · Distributed Serving, GPU Scheduling, Cross-GPU Token Placement

PixelFlow: Token-Level Workload Management for Efficient Distributed DiT Serving

Authors & affiliations: Zhexiang Zhang, Minchen Yu, Yifan Sun, Xu Bai, Xingliang Yuan, and Adel N. Toosi — The University of Melbourne; The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen.

Tags: Distributed Serving, GPU Scheduling, Cross-GPU Token Placement

Problem: Distributed Diffusion Transformer serving has mixed-resolution requests and tight latency SLOs. Request-level batching/admission leaves residual GPU capacity unused, while global coordination forces independently progressing GPUs to synchronize.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces token-level workload management for online DiT serving: image tokens become the scheduling and partitioning unit rather than whole requests.
  • Adds variable-sized request partitions, fanout-aware token placement to reduce cross-GPU exchange, and overlap of remaining transfers with denoising computation.
  • Evaluates Stable Diffusion 3 and FLUX.1-dev on H100 GPUs, reporting up to 43% higher SLO attainment and up to 2.8× goodput versus state-of-the-art DiT serving systems.

Why it matters: This is not LLM training, but it is a clean serving-systems example where fine-grained partitioning changes the communication pattern. The useful part is the token placement logic: balance compute without blindly creating dense all-to-all traffic.

Key insights:

  • The paper’s main systems move is replacing request-granular admission with token-granular admission so residual capacity can be filled without violating SLO slack.
  • Fanout-aware placement matters because balanced compute can still be a bad plan if it creates dense K/V exchange.
  • Independent GPU groups reduce global synchronization delay; the serving system only synchronizes wider groups when admission actually needs combined resources.
  • The DiT setting is a good reminder that future “AI serving” fabrics will carry more than autoregressive LLM KV traffic.

Actionable lessons: When partitioning inference work across GPUs, optimize the communication graph, not just the arithmetic load. A balanced schedule that induces all-to-all traffic can lose to a slightly less balanced but locality-aware token layout.

verified arXiv paper · KV Cache, LLM Serving, Correctness/Validation

Shared KV Caching for Replicated 27B Inference: Correctness Failures and Performance Boundaries

Authors & affiliations: Frank Li — UNSW Sydney.

Tags: KV Cache, LLM Serving, Correctness/Validation

Problem: Shared host-memory KV caching can avoid prefill recomputation when a request moves across replicas, but apparent cache hits do not guarantee correct state transfer, and cross-replica reuse must be compared against local GPU cache locality.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Studies two single-GPU 27B vLLM replicas sharing a 256 GiB LMCache pool and isolates a raw-pointer fallback whose missing CUDA-stream dependency can produce incorrect state under delay.
  • Provides a validation path using byte tests, runtime helpers, full-pool allocation checks, and service-level regression before trusting performance numbers.
  • Reports large TTFT reductions for cross-replica reuse at long contexts, while also showing that fixed placement/local reuse changes the value proposition.

Why it matters: This is a practical antidote to hand-wavy KV-cache claims. It says: before you celebrate cross-replica cache reuse, prove state integrity, include local-hit baselines, and measure when movement across the memory/network path actually pays.

Key insights:

  • A cache hit counter is not a correctness proof. For KV state, ordering and stream dependencies are part of correctness.
  • Cross-replica reuse helps most when placement cannot preserve conversation locality; if local reuse is available, the comparison changes.
  • The evaluation separates cold computation, local reuse, and cross-replica reuse, which is the minimum useful decomposition for production serving decisions.
  • Long-context TTFT can improve dramatically, but those gains depend on workload locality and validated transfer paths.

Actionable lessons: For KV-cache tiers, add integrity tests to the serving pipeline before optimizing. Benchmark against sticky placement and local GPU prefix hits, not only cold-prefill baselines.

verified arXiv paper · Container Networking, eBPF, Cloud Infrastructure

Netkit: Specializing Linux Packet Delivery for Container Networks

Authors & affiliations: Daniel Borkmann and Paul Chaignon — Isovalent at Cisco, Switzerland; Isovalent at Cisco, France.

Tags: Container Networking, eBPF, Cloud Infrastructure

Problem: Container network namespaces preserve isolation but add packet-delivery overhead, especially through veth/backlog queue traversals, so colocated container communication can be slower than same-namespace process communication.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents netkit, an eBPF-based datapath that redirects packets between namespaces while preserving compatibility with the normal Linux network stack expected by containers.
  • Adds a small BPF redirect helper and a netkit device integrated with minimal Cilium changes for Kubernetes environments.
  • Reports up to 37% throughput improvement and near parity between container-to-container and process-to-process communication by removing redundant namespace-transition buffering.

Why it matters: This is the “boring but real” infrastructure paper in today’s batch. AI clusters are increasingly Kubernetes-shaped; shaving container datapath overhead matters for control planes, sidecars, inference microservices, and any host-local fast path around accelerators.

Key insights:

  • The performance gap is not caused by physical isolation; it is a software datapath artifact from generic packet delivery across namespace boundaries.
  • eBPF is used here as specialization rather than bypass: keep compatibility, but skip redundant backlog traversal.
  • The evaluation asks the right operational question: how close can container-to-container communication get to process-to-process communication?
  • For AI serving stacks, host-local networking overhead can become visible once GPU execution is short or highly pipelined.

Actionable lessons: Do not ignore “local” traffic in AI platforms. For multi-container inference services, instrument same-host paths separately from ToR paths; kernel datapath overhead can be an avoidable tax.
