Today’s batch is mostly about bandwidth and state management around LLM serving: one directly network-facing split-learning paper, plus several KV-cache papers that keep turning “memory capacity” into a runtime placement and data-movement problem.

Most important paper of the day

Contribution-Aware Bandwidth Allocation for Multimodal Split Learning

Authors & affiliations: Iason Ofeidis and Leandros Tassiulas. Affiliation: Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Yale University, New Haven, CT, USA.

Tags: Split Learning, Bandwidth Allocation, Edge/Cloud AI

Why this is the top pick: This is the most network-facing pick today: it treats the AI edge uplink as a scarce shared resource and asks which activations are worth transmitting, rather than compressing tensors blindly.

Deeper explanation: ModalShare is valuable because it frames communication as a resource-allocation problem at the model boundary. The client still sends smashed activations, but the server estimates each modality’s marginal contribution and uses that signal to decide how the fixed uplink payload should be divided. That makes the compression policy task-aware without adding client-side work or extra uplink probes. For Ethan’s world, the transferable idea is bigger than split learning: in AI clusters, not all bytes competing for a fabric budget have equal value. Expert activations, KV pages, remote weights, and multimodal features should be scheduled by contribution, reuse probability, and critical-path impact—not only by tensor size.

  • Problem: Multimodal split learning lets weak edge clients train with a server, but the uplink must carry smashed activations for every modality. Equal keep-ratio compression spends the same fraction per modality even when modalities contribute very different value to the fused prediction.
  • Key contributions:
  • Formulates inter-modality allocation as an iso-budget problem: every policy transmits the same expected payload, but differs in how that payload is split across modalities.
  • Introduces ModalShare, which sets per-modality keep-ratios from Shapley contribution scores computed at the server over already received activation coalitions.
  • Reports large accuracy gains over equal keep-ratios at matched payload, including 15.4 and 12.4 percentage-point improvements on CREMA-D and MVSA at 5× compression.
  • Why it matters architecturally: Bandwidth allocation needs a utility function. This paper’s Shapley-score mechanism is one concrete way to couple model semantics to transport/resource decisions.
  • Useful diagram: Figure 1 is useful: it contrasts identical transmission budgets with different modality allocations and shows why an audio-heavy versus video-heavy split changes accuracy. Figures 2–4 then walk through the ModalShare pipeline and budget/accuracy behavior.

Contribution-Aware Bandwidth Allocation for Multimodal Split Learning

Authors & affiliations: Iason Ofeidis and Leandros Tassiulas. Affiliation: Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Yale University, New Haven, CT, USA.

Tags: Split Learning, Bandwidth Allocation, Edge/Cloud AI

Problem: Multimodal split learning lets weak edge clients train with a server, but the uplink must carry smashed activations for every modality. Equal keep-ratio compression spends the same fraction per modality even when modalities contribute very different value to the fused prediction.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Formulates inter-modality allocation as an iso-budget problem: every policy transmits the same expected payload, but differs in how that payload is split across modalities.
  • Introduces ModalShare, which sets per-modality keep-ratios from Shapley contribution scores computed at the server over already received activation coalitions.
  • Reports large accuracy gains over equal keep-ratios at matched payload, including 15.4 and 12.4 percentage-point improvements on CREMA-D and MVSA at 5× compression.

Why it matters: This is the most network-facing pick today: it treats the AI edge uplink as a scarce shared resource and asks which activations are worth transmitting, rather than compressing tensors blindly.

Key insights:

  • The natural compression unit is not always the tensor or layer; for multimodal models, the allocation boundary can be the modality because each stream has different marginal value.
  • Server-side contribution scoring is attractive because it avoids extra client compute and extra uplink probes—the control signal is derived from activations already paid for.
  • Equal keep-ratio policies can be actively bad: they allocate bytes in proportion to cut dimension, not task utility.
  • The paper is edge-focused, but the same principle maps to cluster MoE/KV movement: bandwidth allocation should track contribution or reuse value, not raw byte ownership.

Actionable lessons: For bandwidth-constrained split or disaggregated inference, add a utility signal to the transport/admission policy. If two tensor streams compete for the same fabric budget, allocate by expected contribution to output quality or latency objective, not by static tensor size.

GrowPage: On-Demand KV Budgeting for Efficient LLM Reasoning Serving

Authors & affiliations: Qiankun Ma, Yanjiang Zhou, Zinan Xiong, Haofei Wang, Zhen Song, Yang Xiang, Ziyao Zhang, and Hairong Zheng. Affiliations: Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences; Pengcheng Laboratory; University of Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Tags: LLM Serving, KV Cache, PagedAttention

Problem: Reasoning workloads produce long outputs with KV-cache demand that varies both across requests and during a single decode. Fixed per-request KV budgets either waste memory or compress too aggressively when a request’s attention working set grows.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Identifies the demand mismatch in fixed KV budgeting for reasoning serving, covering both inter-request heterogeneity and intra-request temporal variation.
  • Proposes GrowPage, an on-demand KV budgeting framework using dual-timescale query summaries to estimate evolving attention demand.
  • Co-designs the policy with PagedAttention so the runtime can choose between compressing within the current allocation or acquiring another physical page while preserving continuous batching and prefix caching.

Why it matters: KV memory pressure is increasingly a cluster scheduling problem, not just a model compression problem. GrowPage is useful because it turns KV capacity into a runtime resource with page-level decisions.

Key insights:

  • Reasoning traces are non-stationary: the right KV budget at token 500 may be wrong at token 5,000.
  • The capacity decision is binary and operationally clean at boundaries: compress local history or request another physical page.
  • Integrating with PagedAttention matters because production serving already relies on page-level allocation, batching, and prefix-cache mechanics.
  • The design is local-memory focused, but the control loop is exactly what a distributed KV tier would need before spilling to remote memory or RDMA-backed pools.

Actionable lessons: Treat KV cache as elastic state. Serving systems should expose page-level telemetry—attention working-set growth, compression pressure, and page acquisition rate—so placement policies can decide when to keep, compress, migrate, or spill KV state.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 gives the clearest story: fixed KV budgets leave performance/throughput trade-off on the table, while GrowPage adapts per-request capacity. Figure 2 illustrates the on-demand budgeting workflow around PagedAttention pages.

A Universal Context-Reuse Layer for Cross-Model KV Sharing

Authors & affiliations: Yi Li, Dongming Jiang, Yi Zhao, and Bingzhe Li. Affiliation: University of Texas at Dallas.

Tags: Cross-Model KV Sharing, LLM Serving, Context Reuse

Problem: Serving stacks increasingly route the same long context through multiple models—escalation, agents, verification, routing—but ordinary KV reuse assumes producer and consumer are the same model, forcing each model to recompute prefill.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Studies cross-model KV sharing: translating KV states from a source model into representations consumable by a different target model.
  • Evaluates within-family and cross-family handoff settings, including Qwen-to-Qwen, Qwen-to-Gemma, and Llama-to-Qwen cases.
  • Reports target-side prefill cost reductions while maintaining accuracy/perplexity near native baselines in several heterogeneous model paths.

Why it matters: This is a strong infrastructure idea for heterogeneous serving fleets: shared context should become a portable state object, not a private byproduct locked inside one model instance.

Key insights:

  • Cross-model serving breaks the usual prefix-cache abstraction because cached KV is tied to hidden dimensions, layers, tokenizers, and attention layouts.
  • If translation is cheaper than target prefill, context reuse becomes a data-movement and representation-conversion problem instead of a pure compute problem.
  • The biggest wins should appear in long-context agent or RAG workflows where several models repeatedly consume the same evidence and tool state.
  • Correctness risk is the hard part: a translated KV may save latency but silently perturb downstream quality unless guarded by task-aware validation.

Actionable lessons: For multi-model serving, design context stores with explicit producer/consumer metadata: model family, tokenizer, layer mapping, quality guardrails, and transfer cost. KV reuse should be admitted only when translation latency plus quality risk beats native prefill.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is an architecture-style overview of cross-model KV sharing, showing repeated context processing across models and the proposed reuse layer that translates cached state instead of recomputing prefill.

More GPUs or a Smaller Cache? Tensor Parallelism versus KV Compression for Memory-Bound LLM Serving

Authors & affiliations: Srikanta Datta Tumkur, Mehar Simhadri, Anshu Bansal, Jay Iyer, Sai Pavan Kumar, Sai Kapil Kumar, Ramesh Nampelly, and Raj Dandekar. Affiliations: MIT and Vizuara.

Tags: LLM Serving, Tensor Parallelism, KV Compression

Problem: When KV cache becomes the serving bottleneck, operators can add GPUs via tensor parallelism or shrink the cache via quantization/eviction. These options are usually evaluated in separate literatures and on incompatible cost/latency axes.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds a cost-normalized frontier comparing tensor-parallel and KV-compressed configurations under fixed model, quality floor, and latency target.
  • Decomposes why compression can buy memory headroom at much lower cost than tensor parallelism for the evaluated memory-bound regimes.
  • Identifies decision boundaries including when model residency, not KV cache, makes tensor parallelism unavoidable.

Why it matters: This is practical capacity-planning material. It forces a serving architect to price all-reduce overhead, GPU count, latency target, and KV quality loss in the same model instead of arguing from favorite technique territory.

Key insights:

  • Tensor parallelism is not “free memory”; it adds per-layer collective communication and multiplies the hardware bill.
  • KV compression is attractive when the model already fits on one GPU and memory pressure is dominated by context state rather than weights.
  • The relevant metric is not peak throughput alone but cost per million tokens at a latency and quality floor.
  • The crossover point is hardware- and model-dependent, so the conclusion should be recalibrated for HBM size, interconnect bandwidth, and all-reduce implementation.

Actionable lessons: Before adding TP for memory headroom, run a three-way planner: single-GPU compressed KV, TP without compression, and hybrid TP+compression. Include all-reduce latency, HBM capacity, quality floor, and dollars/token; otherwise the cluster can buy GPUs to solve a cache problem compression would have handled.

Minima-KV: Retention-Preserving KV Cache Compression with Mixed-Format Paged Attention

Authors & affiliations: Sergii Kozyrev and Davyd Maiboroda. Affiliation: Minima AI, Inc.

Tags: KV Cache, Paged Attention, Mixed-Precision Memory

Problem: Long-context serving needs to reduce KV footprint and bandwidth, but eviction can delete live-request information and uniform quantization spends equal bits on tokens with unequal future value.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Defines a retention-preserving lifecycle for paged KV memory with Recent and Anchor pages in FP8 and Stale pages in TQ3, without deleting live-request pages.
  • Describes mixed-format paged attention kernels that merge FP8 and TQ3 partial outputs through a stable global softmax without a dense shadow cache.
  • Reports profile-separated Qwen3.6-27B evidence with quality deltas within about one percentage point of dense control across tested LongBench v2 lengths.

Why it matters: The useful angle is not just another KV compression ratio; it is the runtime contract. Live pages stay addressable, and kernels understand mixed physical formats directly.

Key insights:

  • Retention-preserving compression is safer for serving than eviction-heavy schemes when future attention can move back to old context.
  • PagedAttention is becoming the right abstraction layer for cache innovation because it exposes allocation and kernel boundaries simultaneously.
  • Mixed-format kernels avoid paying back compression savings by reconstructing a dense shadow cache.
  • The paper separates quality and throughput profiles, which is honest: serving teams should demand repeated-system measurements before trusting headline speedups.

Actionable lessons: Design KV tiers so compression state is visible to the kernel and scheduler. If the runtime can keep all live pages addressable while varying format, it can reduce HBM pressure without creating correctness cliffs from hard eviction.
