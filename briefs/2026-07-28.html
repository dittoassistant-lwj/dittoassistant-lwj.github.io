Denial of Deadline: Network-Driven Accuracy Collapse in Distributed Inference Pipelines

Authors & affiliations: Jhonatan Tavori, Gur-Eyal Sela, Ion Stoica, and Gil Zussman. Affiliations: Columbia University; UC Berkeley.

Tags: Distributed Inference, Network Reliability, AI Infrastructure Security

Problem: Two-path inference systems increasingly combine a local fast path with a remote higher-accuracy slow path, but network delay, queueing, and autoscaling lag can make the slow-path result arrive after the merger deadline and silently remove its accuracy benefit.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Defines a cross-domain abstraction for assisted inference: fast path, slow path, router, and merger coordination layer.
  • Shows that shaped workload attacks such as Yo-Yo bursts can exploit shared slow-path bottlenecks, including queueing and autoscaling lag, to push benign slow-path predictions past useful deadlines.
  • Evaluates a two-tier edge–cloud multi-object tracking pipeline where roughly 4,000 burst-shaped requests raise benign slow-path p99 latency from 92 ms to 2 s and reduce tracking quality by 7.0 HOTA points on average.

Why it matters: This is the strongest signal because it turns “network latency” from a perf nuisance into a correctness and accuracy failure mode for distributed AI inference. For edge–cloud, routed-model, or disaggregated serving designs, the merger deadline is effectively part of the transport contract.

Key insights:

  • The attack does not need model weights or victim data; it targets the coordination layer by delaying otherwise-valid slow-path responses.
  • Average load is a weak safety metric here. Burst shape and autoscaling lag dominate whether the slow path misses the application deadline.
  • Accuracy loss is temporally and semantically uneven: the paper reports targeted intervals losing 2.0–18.7 HOTA points and rare classes such as stop signs losing nearly half of pre-attack prediction accuracy.
  • The mitigation space is not only bigger clusters; it includes deadline-aware admission control, priority isolation, rate limiting, routing policy, and merger semantics.

Actionable lessons: Treat slow-path inference as a deadline-sensitive network service, not just an optional accuracy add-on. If the serving architecture merges remote results, design admission control, isolation, and telemetry around “returned before useful deadline” rather than raw success rate or average latency.

That diagram makes the new attack surface obvious—the coordination layer can be correct functionally while still discarding late, higher-accuracy results.

DICE: Detailed Inter-Chiplet End-to-End PHY Modeling for Accurate Chiplet Simulation

Authors & affiliations: Rashid Aligholipour, Stefanos Kaxiras, and Yuan Yao. Affiliations: Uppsala University, Sweden.

Tags: Scale-up Interconnects, Chiplet PHY, Architecture Simulation

Problem: Chiplet-system simulators often model inter-chiplet links as fixed-latency pipes, missing runtime PHY behavior such as SNR shifts, crosstalk, clock jitter, FEC convergence, retransmissions, and PHY-level flow control.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces DICE, a gem5 module for in-simulation end-to-end inter-chiplet PHY modeling.
  • Models QC-LDPC encoding/decoding, PAM4 modulation, lossy-channel transmission, LLR demodulation, adaptive packet resend, and flow control between chiplets.
  • Shows that realistic PHY behavior changes packet-latency composition and shifts system IPC by 6.8% on average and up to 27.6% versus fixed-latency inter-chiplet models.

Why it matters: For AI accelerators and NVLink/NVL-style scale-up fabrics, this is a reminder that “on-package” or “within-node” links are still networks with tail behavior. If the PHY is abstracted away, architects may overestimate the value of topology or cache placement choices.

Key insights:

  • The interesting result is not just higher mean latency; long-tail and run-to-run variability from FEC/retry behavior can move application-level IPC.
  • Inter-chiplet communication intensity is tied to LLC miss behavior, so workload memory locality directly changes how much PHY fidelity matters.
  • The paper validates against production chiplet processors and shows fixed-latency HeteroGarnet-style models can produce off-trend conclusions.
  • This maps cleanly to heterogeneous AI nodes: as chiplet/GPU/NPU fabrics push bandwidth density, reliability mechanisms become part of the performance model.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating scale-up designs, model link reliability and retry latency as first-class effects. A topology or placement policy that wins under fixed latency can lose once PHY-induced tail delays, FEC convergence, and retransmission backpressure are included.

SpecBox: Speculative Sandbox Scheduling for Efficient LLM Agent Serving

Authors & affiliations: Yihui Zhang, Tianyu Wo, Jinghao Wang, Xiaoyang Sun, Menghao Zhang, Cangzhou Yuan, Li Li, Chunming Hu, Albert Y. Zomaya, and Renyu Yang. Affiliations: Beihang University; University of Leeds; The University of Sydney.

Tags: LLM Serving, Disaggregated Runtime, Agent Infrastructure

Problem: LLM agents increasingly invoke isolated external sandboxes over MCP-like interfaces; reserving sandboxes wastes memory, while on-demand sandbox startup exposes cold-start latency on the interactive path.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds SpecBox, a predictive runtime that overlaps sandbox prewarming with streaming LLM token generation.
  • Uses keyword matching, streaming semantic embeddings, stochastic prefetching over a sandbox dependency graph, semantic result caching, and an out-of-band shared-memory transport plane.
  • Reports up to 2.9× P99 latency reduction versus on-demand sandboxing and 45.9% lower peak memory usage versus permanently reserved sandbox deployments.

Why it matters: This is adjacent to networked AI infrastructure rather than fabric research, but it has a very real data-plane angle: agent serving is becoming a stream of RPC-like interactions between model workers and disaggregated tool environments. Cold-start, artifact movement, and control-plane sequencing are now serving-path bottlenecks.

Key insights:

  • The key systems move is temporal overlap: infer likely tool demand while the LLM is still generating, then hide sandbox bootstrapping behind token generation.
  • The paper explicitly separates predictive preparation from side-effect execution, which is important for correctness in speculative runtimes.
  • The shared-memory transport optimization is a useful reminder that artifact transfer can dominate once sandbox startup is hidden.
  • The evaluation uses 200 MCPBench-derived multi-turn trajectories and 32 MCP-compatible tool servers, making it more grounded than a toy agent-runtime demo.

Actionable lessons: For agent-serving platforms, design the tool/runtime path as a pipelined distributed system. Prewarm and place environments based on streaming intent, but keep side effects gated until the agent commits; separately optimize artifact movement so the network/serialization path does not become the next boss fight.

KernelScript: Cross-Boundary Typed DSL for eBPF Applications

Authors & affiliations: Cong Wang, Siyuan Sun, and Yusheng Zheng. Affiliations: Multikernel Technologies; Xi’an University of Posts and Telecommunications; UC Santa Cruz and Eunomia Labs.

Tags: eBPF, Programmable Data Plane, Systems Tooling

Problem: eBPF applications span kernel programs, userspace loaders, maps, ring buffers, and attach lifecycle logic, but today those cross-boundary contracts are mostly implicit and can silently diverge.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces KernelScript, a DSL that describes eBPF entry points, userspace control flow, maps, ring buffers, program handles, and execution domains in one typed source.
  • Compiles to standard eBPF C, userspace C, and Makefiles while preserving the libbpf/bpftool/clang toolchain.
  • Evaluates 43 eBPF workloads across XDP, TC, kprobe, tracepoint, and struct_ops, rejecting cross-boundary bugs at compile time and reducing cross-boundary change diffs by about 5×.

Why it matters: Not an AI-cluster paper directly, but relevant to programmable networking and observability. A lot of production fabric telemetry, packet steering, and kernel-bypass glue leans on eBPF-like machinery; type-checking the kernel/userspace boundary is a practical reliability upgrade.

Key insights:

  • The paper’s core observation is that maps and program handles are single concepts duplicated across kernel and userspace; typing them once removes a whole class of mismatch bugs.
  • It keeps the existing verifier/toolchain path, so the proposal is incremental rather than asking operators to trust a new kernel backend.
  • The workload coverage includes XDP and TC, which makes it relevant to packet-path and dataplane experiments, not only tracing.
  • For AI cluster debugging, safer eBPF scaffolding matters because bad telemetry code can corrupt the very signal used to diagnose congestion or gray failures.

Actionable lessons: If you use eBPF for fabric observability or packet-path experiments, treat the userspace/kernel contract as API surface. Typed maps, lifecycle checks, and generated glue are boring in the best way: fewer runtime surprises while poking production-adjacent datapaths.

Useful diagram: No single hardware architecture diagram dominates; the useful artifact is the type-system/model view that unifies maps, program handles, and execution domains across userspace and kernel code.

verified arXiv paper · transport protocols, l4s/ecn, network evaluation

Fake It No More: Evaluating L4S with SCReAM on Video Traffic

Authors & affiliations: Nawel Alioua, Ryan Zanone, Cheng Xi, and Elizabeth Belding. Affiliations: UC Santa Barbara.

Tags: Transport Protocols, L4S/ECN, Network Evaluation

Problem: L4S/DualPI2 evaluations often rely on synthetic RTP-like traffic, but interactive media quality depends on codec behavior, packet loss, content motion, and application-level QoE—not just queue delay.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Extends SCReAM BW with a video codec so experiments can use actual video traffic rather than only synthetic RTP traffic.
  • Evaluates SCReAM over Classic and L4S modes in Mahimahi on a mobile network trace, with random loss and different motion-complexity levels.
  • Finds L4S reduces median per-run p95 queue delay by 35% but drops sender throughput by 42.4%; under 1% random loss, QoE benefits become more pronounced despite narrower network-level gains.

Why it matters: This is less AI-specific, but it is a clean transport-methodology paper. The useful lesson for AI fabrics is measurement discipline: queue delay, throughput, and application-level quality can move in different directions, especially under ECN/AQM control loops.

Key insights:

  • The L4S win is not free: lower p95 queueing delay comes with a substantial sender-throughput reduction in their baseline.
  • Random loss narrows network-level differences but can amplify QoE advantages, so transport conclusions depend on the application metric.
  • Motion complexity changes stability; L4S maintained more stable median inter-frame delay across content classes.
  • For datacenter AI transports, the analogous lesson is to pair packet metrics with job-level metrics such as step time, allreduce tail, TTFT, and accuracy/SLO impact.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating congestion control or AQM, do not stop at queue delay and throughput. Carry the measurement up to workload-visible impact; otherwise a fabric tweak can look better in packet counters while making the application trade-off worse.

Useful diagram: The paper’s useful figures are evaluation plots rather than an architecture diagram, especially the queue-delay/inter-frame-delay comparisons across motion levels.
