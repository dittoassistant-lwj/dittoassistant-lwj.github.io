AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s batch is a mix of one very strong scale-up fabric paper plus fresh serving-infrastructure work: optical high-bandwidth domains for LLM training, expert-locality routing for disaggregated MoE decode, KV-cache compression, and kernel-level LLM serving simulation.

Most important paper of the day

InfiniteHBD: Building Datacenter-Scale High-Bandwidth Domain for LLM with Optical Circuit Switching Transceivers

Authors & affiliations: Chenchen Shou, Guyue Liu, Hao Nie, Huaiyu Meng, Yu Zhou, Yimin Jiang, Wenqing Lv, Yelong Xu, Yuanwei Lu, Zhang Chen, Yanbo Yu, Yichen Shen, Yibo Zhu, and Daxin Jiang. Affiliations: Peking University; StepFun; Lightelligence Pte. Ltd.; Unaffiliated.

Tags: Scale-up Networking, Optical Circuit Switching, LLM Training Fabrics

Why this is the top pick: This is the most Ethan-relevant paper in today’s set even though it is older: it directly attacks the scale-up fabric problem behind NVL72-like domains, using optical switching at the transceiver layer to trade switch-fabric cost for reconfigurable topology and fault isolation.

Deeper explanation: InfiniteHBD is interesting because it treats the LLM scale-up domain as a topology, cost, and failure-containment problem at once. Instead of simply scaling a switch-centric NVL-style fabric, the design moves reconfigurability into optical transceivers and uses k-hop rings to create variable-size high-bandwidth domains. The practical systems question is whether the extra optical/control complexity buys enough flexibility: larger TP/EP groups when healthy, less stranded capacity after failures, and lower spillover onto the DCN. That is exactly the design space where superpod fabrics are headed.

  • Problem: LLM training needs very large high-bandwidth domains for tensor and expert parallelism, but switch-centric designs such as NVL-style fabrics become expensive at scale, while GPU-centric designs can suffer large fault blast radius and poor utilization after node failures.
  • Key contributions: Proposes InfiniteHBD, a transceiver-centric high-bandwidth-domain architecture that embeds optical circuit switching inside transceivers instead of relying only on centralized switch fabrics.; Introduces OCSTrx, reconfigurable k-hop ring topologies, and an HBD-DCN orchestration algorithm for variable-size training groups and fault isolation.; Evaluates cost, fault waste, cross-ToR traffic, and MFU, reporting 31% of NVL-72 cost, near-zero GPU waste under failures, and 3.37× MFU improvement over an NVIDIA DGX-style baseline in the paper’s setup.
  • Why it matters architecturally: It pushes the scale-up fabric discussion beyond raw bandwidth into topology reconfiguration, failure blast radius, and interaction with the datacenter network.

InfiniteHBD: Building Datacenter-Scale High-Bandwidth Domain for LLM with Optical Circuit Switching Transceivers

Authors & affiliations: Chenchen Shou, Guyue Liu, Hao Nie, Huaiyu Meng, Yu Zhou, Yimin Jiang, Wenqing Lv, Yelong Xu, Yuanwei Lu, Zhang Chen, Yanbo Yu, Yichen Shen, Yibo Zhu, and Daxin Jiang. Affiliations: Peking University; StepFun; Lightelligence Pte. Ltd.; Unaffiliated.

Tags: Scale-up Networking, Optical Circuit Switching, LLM Training Fabrics

Problem: LLM training needs very large high-bandwidth domains for tensor and expert parallelism, but switch-centric designs such as NVL-style fabrics become expensive at scale, while GPU-centric designs can suffer large fault blast radius and poor utilization after node failures.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes InfiniteHBD, a transceiver-centric high-bandwidth-domain architecture that embeds optical circuit switching inside transceivers instead of relying only on centralized switch fabrics.
  • Introduces OCSTrx, reconfigurable k-hop ring topologies, and an HBD-DCN orchestration algorithm for variable-size training groups and fault isolation.
  • Evaluates cost, fault waste, cross-ToR traffic, and MFU, reporting 31% of NVL-72 cost, near-zero GPU waste under failures, and 3.37× MFU improvement over an NVIDIA DGX-style baseline in the paper’s setup.

Why it matters: This is the most Ethan-relevant paper in today’s set even though it is older: it directly attacks the scale-up fabric problem behind NVL72-like domains, using optical switching at the transceiver layer to trade switch-fabric cost for reconfigurable topology and fault isolation.

Key insights:

  • The paper separates DCN-scale traffic from HBD-scale traffic: tensor/expert parallelism wants Tbps-class local bandwidth, while DP/PP/CP/SP can tolerate conventional datacenter-network paths.
  • Putting OCS capability in the transceiver changes the scaling unit: topology can be built from many small reconfigurable optical endpoints rather than a few large central switches.
  • The k-hop ring idea is attractive because it can form variable-size bandwidth domains for different TP/EP group sizes without paying full non-blocking-switch cost.
  • Fault isolation is a first-class fabric metric here; the paper argues that smaller node-level blast radius matters as much as bisection bandwidth for large training pools.
  • The HBD-DCN orchestration angle is important: scale-up fabric design cannot be separated from how jobs are placed and how residual traffic spills into the scale-out network.

Actionable lessons: For scale-up fabric studies, evaluate cost, blast radius, stranded GPUs, and cross-ToR spillover together—not just peak bandwidth. When comparing NVLink/NVL-style, GPU-mesh, and optical options, model variable TP/EP group sizes and node failures; a fabric that looks efficient when healthy can waste many GPUs after small failures.

ELDR: Expert-Locality-Aware Decode Routing for PD-Disaggregated MoE Serving

Authors & affiliations: Sangjin Choi, Sukmin Cho, Yifan Xiong, Ziyue Yang, Youngjin Kwon, and Peng Cheng. Affiliations: KAIST; Microsoft Research; Shanghai Xingyunzhili Artificial Intelligence Institute.

Tags: Disaggregated LLM Serving, MoE Communication, Routing / Locality

Problem: In prefill-decode disaggregated MoE serving, decode workers are usually treated as interchangeable once load is balanced. The paper shows that this misses a real bottleneck: MoE decode latency depends on how many distinct experts the batch activates, because those expert weights must be loaded from HBM each step.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds expert signatures from prefill expert activations to predict decode-time expert locality before output tokens are generated.
  • Uses balanced K-means offline plus locality-band routing online to place requests on decode workers with compatible expert activation patterns while controlling load.
  • Reports lower TPOT and TTFT versus load-only routing, with gains tied to reducing the per-worker active-expert union.

Why it matters: This is a good network-facing serving paper because it reframes “routing” as placement of future communication and memory pressure, not just request count. In MoE systems, colocating similar requests can reduce expert-weight movement and indirectly change inter-worker pressure.

Key insights:

  • Equal queue length is not equal work for MoE decode; the active-expert union is closer to the true memory-bandwidth cost.
  • Prefill already exposes useful routing information, so the system can make decode placement decisions before generation begins.
  • The locality/load trade-off resembles rack-aware placement: too much locality risks imbalance, too little locality scatters the working set.
  • The idea becomes more important as MoE serving gets more disaggregated, because expert locality affects both HBM reuse and how often weight/cache movement paths are exercised.

Actionable lessons: For MoE serving design, track active-expert cardinality per decode batch alongside normal load metrics. A scheduler that only balances requests can create pathological HBM and network pressure. If simulating disaggregated MoE, model expert locality and batch composition rather than assuming every decode worker has identical per-token cost.

MosaicKV: Serving Long-Context LLM with Dynamic Two-D KV Cache Compression

Authors & affiliations: Sheng Qiang, Ruiwei Chen, Yinpeng Wu, Jinyu Gu, Zhichao Hua, Yubin Xia, Binyu Zang, and Haibo Chen. Affiliations: Institute of Parallel and Distributed Systems, Shanghai Jiao Tong University.

Tags: KV Cache, Long-Context Serving, Memory / Heterogeneous Infrastructure

Problem: Long-context serving makes KV cache the dominant memory footprint, but many compression schemes operate along only one dimension—sequence or channel—leaving throughput on the table or causing too much accuracy loss when applied naively.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes dynamic two-dimensional KV compression across sequence and channel dimensions, rather than treating them independently.
  • Uses fine-grained sparse KV representations and runtime-aware mechanisms to reduce cache size while preserving model quality.
  • Reports up to 16× attention speedup, 4.8× lower decode latency, 7.3× higher throughput, 3× lower memory use, and 1.76% average accuracy loss on LongBench and RULER.

Why it matters: This is less fabric-native than the top pick, but it matters for disaggregated serving because KV cache volume is the object being moved, stored, paged, and sometimes transferred across workers. Reducing KV volume changes the network/storage pressure envelope.

Key insights:

  • KV compression is not only a GPU memory trick; in disaggregated prefill/decode systems it can reduce P2D transfer volume and cache-store bandwidth.
  • The paper’s two-dimensional framing is useful because sequence-only pruning and channel-only projection fail in different ways on long contexts.
  • Accuracy loss is the real constraint: aggressive channel compression layered onto sequence compression can collapse quality, so compression must be content- and layer-aware.
  • For long-context services, cache footprint, attention compute, and transfer cost are coupled; optimizing one dimension alone can simply move the bottleneck.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating KV disaggregation, include compression ratio, transfer bytes, attention speed, and accuracy loss in one budget. For system architecture, expose compression state to placement and cache-management policies; a “compressed token block” has different network and memory cost than a raw KV page.

Verified arXiv CS paper · llm serving simulation / gpu kernels / performance modeling

KernelSight-LM: A Kernel-Level LLM Inference Simulator

Authors & affiliations: Xiteng Yao, Taeho Kim, Hengzhi Pei, Xinle Liu, Kyle Ulrich, Leonard Lausen, Ashish Khetan, Xiang Song, George Karypis, and Martin Herbordt. Affiliations: Amazon Web Services; Boston University.

Tags: LLM Serving Simulation, GPU Kernels, Performance Modeling

Problem: End-to-end LLM inference performance depends on serving policy, GPU kernel behavior, communication, and host overhead. Pure benchmarking is slow and hardware-specific, while coarse simulators miss kernel-level effects that determine TTFT, TPOT, and throughput.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds a discrete-event LLM inference simulator with a roofline-based kernel model, learned efficiency term, communication model, and host-overhead model.
  • Supports two prediction tiers: cross-generation prediction with no target-GPU measurements, and a target-measured tier using one model-agnostic microbenchmark sweep.
  • Reports kernel-level and end-to-end prediction accuracy across multiple model families, GPU generations, prefix caching, and continuous batching.

Why it matters: This is useful infrastructure tooling: if the simulator’s communication model is extended carefully, it could become a place to test serving/fabric co-design without waiting for every new GPU generation or production deployment.

Key insights:

  • Kernel-level modeling matters because decode is often limited by memory bandwidth and scheduling granularity, not just FLOP/s.
  • The simulator decomposes serving into GPU kernels, communication, and host overhead, which is the right shape for studying cross-layer bottlenecks.
  • Cross-generation prediction is valuable for architecture planning: the paper tries to estimate new-GPU serving behavior before full deployment data exists.
  • For Ethan’s world, the missing or most extensible piece is likely richer network modeling: collectives, KV transfer, multi-rail placement, and contention could make this more fabric-aware.

Actionable lessons: Use this kind of simulator as a scaffold, not an oracle. For cluster-network questions, plug in realistic communication traces and contention models; otherwise the GPU-kernel accuracy may hide fabric effects. Still, the paper is a good reminder to separate TTFT, TPOT, and throughput because they stress different parts of the serving stack.

Useful diagram: Figure 4 gives the architecture overview of KernelSight-LM, including hardware/model/request inputs, kernel prediction, communication modeling, host overhead, and the discrete-event scheduler. Figures 1–3 explain the GPU/roofline model.

Fast take

Read InfiniteHBD first for scale-up fabric design, then ELDR if you care about MoE-serving placement. MosaicKV is the cache-volume knob, and KernelSight-LM is the simulator scaffold that could become more fabric-aware with better communication models.
