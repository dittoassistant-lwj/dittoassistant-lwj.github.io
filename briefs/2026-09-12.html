QPS-ToR: A Parallel Iterative Switching Algorithm for Reconfigurable Optical Datacenter Switching

Authors & affiliations: Dongzhao Song, Qianru Yu, and Jun “Jim” Xu — Georgia Tech, Atlanta, Georgia, USA.

Tags: Optical Datacenter Networks, Load Balancing, Reconfigurable Fabrics

Why this is the top pick: This is the cleanest network-facing signal today: it is not another LLM serving scheduler, but a concrete optical fabric scheduling mechanism with throughput/FCT claims and an obvious connection to bursty AI-cluster traffic.

Deeper explanation: QPS-ToR is interesting because it focuses on the control loop that decides which rack pairs actually receive optical capacity. For AI clusters, that decision is central: collective phases, checkpointing, and disaggregated serving can create bursty many-to-many or skewed rack-pair demand. A fabric can have impressive raw optical bandwidth and still underperform if the matching engine assigns circuits without enough queue awareness or cannot run fast enough. The paper’s mechanism is modest but important: use a sliding-window parallel iterative scheduler to approximate high-throughput crossbar matching while staying compatible with an existing traffic-aware RODCN workflow. That makes it a plausible building block rather than a whole new optical architecture.

  • Problem: Reconfigurable optical data center networks can add high-capacity ToR-to-ToR connectivity, but traffic-oblivious schedules waste capacity and traffic-aware scheduling can bottleneck on the switch-matching algorithm itself. The paper targets the scheduling loop inside NegotiaToR-like RODCN control planes.
  • Key contributions:
  • Replaces NegotiaToR’s single-iteration iSLIP engine with SW-QPS, a sliding-window parallel iterative algorithm adapted from crossbar scheduling.
  • Simplifies the scheduling cycle from three-step Request–Grant–Accept to two-step Request–Grant while preserving compatibility with the existing NegotiaToR workflow.
  • In 128-ToR flow-level simulations under realistic datacenter workloads, reports up to 36% higher throughput and 82% lower flow completion time than NegotiaToR, while outperforming RotorNet on the parallel optical topology.
  • Why it matters architecturally: It reframes optical fabric performance as a scheduling/matching problem under realistic demand, which is exactly where AI-cluster traffic can punish traffic-oblivious designs.
  • Useful diagram: The paper contains architecture/scheduling diagrams: the introduction motivates a flattened optical ToR fabric, and the algorithm section illustrates the Request–Grant scheduling flow and sliding-window state used by QPS-ToR.

QPS-ToR: A Parallel Iterative Switching Algorithm for Reconfigurable Optical Datacenter Switching

Authors & affiliations: Dongzhao Song, Qianru Yu, and Jun “Jim” Xu — Georgia Tech, Atlanta, Georgia, USA.

Tags: Optical Datacenter Networks, Load Balancing, Reconfigurable Fabrics

Problem: Reconfigurable optical data center networks can add high-capacity ToR-to-ToR connectivity, but traffic-oblivious schedules waste capacity and traffic-aware scheduling can bottleneck on the switch-matching algorithm itself. The paper targets the scheduling loop inside NegotiaToR-like RODCN control planes.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Replaces NegotiaToR’s single-iteration iSLIP engine with SW-QPS, a sliding-window parallel iterative algorithm adapted from crossbar scheduling.
  • Simplifies the scheduling cycle from three-step Request–Grant–Accept to two-step Request–Grant while preserving compatibility with the existing NegotiaToR workflow.
  • In 128-ToR flow-level simulations under realistic datacenter workloads, reports up to 36% higher throughput and 82% lower flow completion time than NegotiaToR, while outperforming RotorNet on the parallel optical topology.

Why it matters: This is the cleanest network-facing signal today: it is not another LLM serving scheduler, but a concrete optical fabric scheduling mechanism with throughput/FCT claims and an obvious connection to bursty AI-cluster traffic.

Key insights:

  • The key limitation in a reconfigurable optical fabric is not only how quickly circuits can be set up; it is whether the matching algorithm assigns scarce optical slots to the queues that actually need them.
  • Sliding-window QPS adds queue-length awareness without making every cycle expensive. That matters for AI jobs where elephant transfers and synchronization phases can create strong ToR-pair skew.
  • The paper’s comparison against both traffic-aware NegotiaToR and traffic-oblivious RotorNet usefully separates two effects: demand awareness and matching quality.
  • The result hints that optical fabrics for AI clusters should be evaluated at the scheduler/control-loop level, not just by counting bisection bandwidth or circuit reconfiguration time.

Actionable lessons: If an AI-cluster fabric uses reconfigurable optical paths, make the matching primitive queue-aware and cheap enough to run frequently. For design reviews, ask whether the scheduler can prioritize hot source-destination pairs during collective or checkpoint bursts, and whether its control cycle becomes the real throughput limiter.

Useful diagram: The paper contains architecture/scheduling diagrams: the introduction motivates a flattened optical ToR fabric, and the algorithm section illustrates the Request–Grant scheduling flow and sliding-window state used by QPS-ToR.

Building py-kvcache: A Performance Characterization of External KV Caching for vLLM with NVMe SSDs

Authors & affiliations: Joseph Kanichai, Tiziano De Matteis, and Animesh Trivedi — Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam; IBM Research Zurich.

Tags: LLM Serving, KV Cache, SSD/Memory Hierarchy

Problem: External KV caching can avoid recomputing long prefixes, but loading KV blocks from CPU or NVMe is not automatically faster than recomputation, especially with fast GPUs or short prefixes. The paper characterizes where external KV cache tiers help or hurt in vLLM.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Measures GPU, CPU, and NVMe KV-cache tiers using synthetic workloads, long-context benchmarks, production traces, and transfer-granularity experiments.
  • Builds py-kvcache, a vLLM KV Offload connector using asynchronous direct I/O, bounded shared staging, and scheduler-aware preloading while requests are still queued.
  • Reports 2.0× faster disk loading than LMCache at 80k tokens, with preloading contributing 1.34×; with GPU+CPU+disk caching, it is 1.23× faster than LMCache and within about 4% of native vLLM KV Offload.

Why it matters: This is directly relevant to disaggregated LLM serving because it treats KV movement as an admission and scheduling decision, not just a storage bandwidth problem.

Key insights:

  • The break-even point is setup-specific: on a weaker GPU, Bailian trace replay improves TTFT, while on H100 the average request can fall below the point where external loading beats recomputation.
  • Transfer granularity and intermediate staging memory matter as much as nominal SSD bandwidth; the bottleneck is the whole path into the request schedule.
  • Scheduler-aware preloading is the important mechanism: reading while the request waits converts some storage latency into overlapped latency.
  • External KV caching should be a dynamic policy choice over prefix length, GPU speed, cache residency, and queue state rather than an unconditional serving feature.

Actionable lessons: For KV-cache tiers, measure TTFT against recomputation on the actual GPU/storage/network path. Add an admission rule: only load external KV when expected transfer plus staging overhead beats recompute, and start movement before the request reaches execution.

Useful diagram: The paper includes useful system and workflow diagrams for py-kvcache’s vLLM connector path, cache tiers, staging buffers, and scheduler-aware preloading; those are the diagrams to inspect before copying the design.

PATTON: Enabling Commodity PIM for Production LLM Serving

Authors & affiliations: Hangyeol Kim, Sanghyun Lee, Teokkyu Suh, and Joo-Young Kim — KAIST, Daejeon, Republic of Korea.

Tags: PIM, LLM Serving, KV Cache Runtime

Problem: Processing-in-memory can accelerate memory-bound decode attention, but production serving engines constantly allocate, populate, share, cache, and reclaim logical KV blocks. Commodity PIM needs a runtime that maps this block lifecycle to physical layouts and command generation without wrecking GEMV efficiency or cache capacity.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces PATTON, a runtime that integrates production LLM serving engines with commodity PIM while preserving logical KV-cache block semantics.
  • Uses hierarchical granule allocation: block-sized Key/Value granules fix physical placement and commands, while coarser granules group blocks for efficient GEMV and capacity utilization.
  • Adds a Commit Zone that stages partial Value blocks for efficient single-token writes before committing them to GEMV-optimized locations; reports 1.95× average speedup and 4.83× higher energy efficiency over evaluated baselines.

Why it matters: PATTON is useful because it exposes the hidden systems problem in “PIM for LLM serving”: the accelerator is only viable if KV lifecycle management, address mapping, and command generation fit the serving runtime.

Key insights:

  • The Value cache has a three-way conflict: GEMV-friendly layout, efficient single-token writes, and capacity utilization. Optimizing only one creates expensive movement or fragmentation elsewhere.
  • The runtime boundary is the main contribution. PATTON does not require changing PIM processing units; it adapts placement and commands around commodity hardware constraints.
  • The Commit Zone is a small but important systems trick: absorb incremental decode writes in a write-friendly area, then later place data where GEMV likes it.
  • For heterogeneous AI infrastructure, the paper is a reminder that memory-side compute must preserve the serving engine’s block abstraction, not bypass it.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating PIM/NMP for serving, include block allocation, cache hits, writes, and reclamation in the benchmark. A raw attention speedup is not enough; the memory-compute substrate needs a runtime contract with vLLM-like block managers.

Useful diagram: The paper’s early figures lay out the production KV-cache lifecycle and PATTON runtime path, including hierarchical granules and the Commit Zone. Those diagrams are useful for seeing why naive PIM layouts collide with single-token decode writes.

UNISON: A Co-Designed Near-Memory Scheduler of Session KV Residency for LLM Agents

Authors & affiliations: Fan He, Yan Li, and Xiaoyang Zeng — State Key Laboratory of Integrated Chips and Systems, Fudan University, Shanghai, China.

Tags: LLM Agents, KV Residency, Near-Memory Scheduling

Problem: Agentic LLM workloads keep growing KV prefixes alive across tool waits and many turns. Existing eviction policies based on recency or timeout can evict a session that is logically waiting but likely to return, wasting future prefill work.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes UNISON, an event-driven near-memory scheduler for session-level KV residency across SRAM/HBM-like tiers.
  • Introduces SPEAR for eviction using gap averages and turn-indexed return hazard, and TIDE for spending idle tool-wait windows as DMA budget for tier placement.
  • Evaluates 1,415 sessions and 33,596 turns across coding and general-mission traces; the joint policy is the best non-oracle entry on every trace.

Why it matters: This is more serving-infrastructure than network paper, but it is relevant because agentic serving turns KV cache into a long-lived, tiered residency problem that will eventually pressure GPU, host, SSD, CXL, and possibly remote memory paths.

Key insights:

  • The object being scheduled is a session, not a request. That changes cache value from “was this prefix recently used?” to “will this agent loop return soon?”
  • Tool wait time is not dead time; it can be used as a DMA window to migrate KV state without blocking active decode.
  • The policy separates eviction from tiering but gives both the same live ranking, which is a useful design pattern for multi-tier KV managers.
  • The paper suggests future serving systems need semantic signals from the agent loop, not just token-level telemetry from the engine.

Actionable lessons: Expose session and tool-wait structure to the KV manager. In agent-serving stacks, make eviction/tiering policies aware of expected return gaps so the system moves or keeps the right prefixes before the next turn arrives.

verified arXiv paper · MoE Inference, Near-Memory Processing, NoC/Placement

HDA-MoE: Hybrid Parallelism and Dynamic, Adaptive Scheduling for Mixture-of-Experts with 3D Near-Memory Processing

Authors & affiliations: Haochen Huang, Shuzhang Zhong, Shengxuan Qiu, Zhe Zhang, Shuangchen Li, Cong Li, Dimin Niu, Hongzhong Zheng, Guangyu Sun, Runsheng Wang, and Meng Li — Peking University; Alibaba DAMO Academy; Alibaba Group Beijing Hupan Lab; Beijing Advanced Innovation Center for Integrated Circuits.

Tags: MoE Inference, Near-Memory Processing, NoC/Placement

Problem: MoE inference stresses memory capacity, bandwidth, and inter-bank communication. On 3D near-memory processing systems, pure tensor parallelism increases communication, while pure expert parallelism can leave compute unbalanced under dynamic routing.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Combines offline hybrid parallel mapping with online dynamic adaptive scheduling for MoE execution on 3D NMP architectures.
  • Balances communication overhead and compute utilization across distributed memory/compute banks under dynamic expert-routing behavior.
  • Reports 1.1×–3.4× speedup over tensor parallelism, 1.1×–1.5× over expert parallelism, 1.1×–3.7× over a hybrid compute-balanced baseline, and 1.1×–1.3× over HD-MoE.

Why it matters: This is a good heterogeneous-infrastructure paper: it connects MoE routing, expert placement, communication overhead, and NoC-like near-memory topology rather than treating expert execution as a pure kernel problem.

Key insights:

  • Hybrid parallelism is valuable because it can trade communication against load balance at a finer granularity than TP or EP alone.
  • Dynamic routing makes static placement fragile; an online scheduler is needed when hot experts shift with input distribution.
  • The paper’s Figure 1 is a nice visual mental model: TP communicates heavily, EP can imbalance banks, and hybrid mapping tries to keep both constraints in view.
  • For MoE hardware, the key bottleneck is often movement between memory-compute islands, not just raw expert FLOPs.

Actionable lessons: For MoE accelerators and scale-up fabrics, evaluate placement with routing skew and interconnect topology in the loop. A good design needs both an offline mapping baseline and a runtime path that adapts when experts become hot.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is especially useful: it compares tensor parallelism, expert parallelism, and hybrid parallelism with expert placement, bank utilization, communication, and execution timelines.
