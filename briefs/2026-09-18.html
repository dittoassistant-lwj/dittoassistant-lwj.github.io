MeshKV: A Network-on-Chip KV Cache Fabric for Scalable Transformer Decoding Accelerators

Authors & affiliations: Dong Liu and Yanxuan Yu — University of California, Los Angeles; Columbia University.

Tags: Scale-up/NoC Fabrics, KV Cache, LLM Serving Accelerators

Why this is the top pick: This is today’s strongest systems signal: it treats KV movement as a fabric problem. The design vocabulary—packetized flows, multicast, credit alignment, hotspot avoidance—is very close to scale-up networking, just moved inside the accelerator package.

Deeper explanation: MeshKV is the most architecture-relevant paper today because it puts routing, multicast, credits, and placement directly in the path of transformer decoding. It is not a datacenter network paper, but the control problem is familiar: long-context serving creates nonuniform demand, shared data movement, and back-pressure, and the fabric only helps if the software/kernel pipeline is shaped around it.

  • Problem: Autoregressive transformer decoding is increasingly limited by irregular KV-cache movement. Centralized DRAM or memory-controller paths create hotspots on tiled accelerators, especially for long-context multi-stream serving.
  • Key contributions:
  • Proposes MeshKV, a lightweight packetized NoC fabric that treats KV blocks as flows rather than forcing them through centralized memory paths.
  • Co-designs TaKV affine striping, Mare multicast with duplicate suppression, and PAD pipelining to overlap prefetch, tile multiply, and streaming softmax behind credit-aligned FIFOs.
  • Reports an 8×8 FPGA implementation that reduces interconnect traffic by up to 58%, improves KV bandwidth utilization by 2.1×, and reaches up to 1.9× multi-stream throughput on LLaMA-2-7B and Mistral-7B at 8K–32K context.
  • Why it matters architecturally: KV-cache serving is becoming a data-movement problem across HBM, SRAM, CXL-like tiers, and accelerator fabrics. MeshKV is a concrete example of designing the fabric as part of the attention datapath.

MeshKV: A Network-on-Chip KV Cache Fabric for Scalable Transformer Decoding Accelerators

Authors & affiliations: Dong Liu and Yanxuan Yu — University of California, Los Angeles; Columbia University.

Tags: Scale-up/NoC Fabrics, KV Cache, LLM Serving Accelerators

Problem: Autoregressive transformer decoding is increasingly limited by irregular KV-cache movement. Centralized DRAM or memory-controller paths create hotspots on tiled accelerators, especially for long-context multi-stream serving.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes MeshKV, a lightweight packetized NoC fabric that treats KV blocks as flows rather than forcing them through centralized memory paths.
  • Co-designs TaKV affine striping, Mare multicast with duplicate suppression, and PAD pipelining to overlap prefetch, tile multiply, and streaming softmax behind credit-aligned FIFOs.
  • Reports an 8×8 FPGA implementation that reduces interconnect traffic by up to 58%, improves KV bandwidth utilization by 2.1×, and reaches up to 1.9× multi-stream throughput on LLaMA-2-7B and Mistral-7B at 8K–32K context.

Why it matters: This is today’s strongest systems signal: it treats KV movement as a fabric problem. The design vocabulary—packetized flows, multicast, credit alignment, hotspot avoidance—is very close to scale-up networking, just moved inside the accelerator package.

Key insights:

  • The paper’s key move is to stop viewing KV cache as passive memory and instead expose it as routed traffic with homes, flows, credits, and multicast opportunities.
  • Multicast is not just a datacenter collective trick; for decoding accelerators, shared KV segments across streams can amortize movement inside the tile mesh.
  • Back-pressure is converted into useful work only if compute and fabric scheduling are co-designed. PAD’s prefetch/multiply/reduce pipeline is doing the same kind of latency hiding that good collective runtimes attempt at cluster scale.
  • The result implies that long-context serving accelerators need topology-aware KV placement, not only cache compression or bigger memory bandwidth.

Actionable lessons: For architecture work, model KV traffic at the fabric level early. If the serving path has tile-local SRAM, HBM/CXL/HBF, and multicast-capable interconnects, the placement policy and credit loop are part of the attention kernel—not a later “memory subsystem” detail.

GeoMesh: Workload-Balanced and Sign-Compressed Geo-Distributed LLM Training

Authors & affiliations: Changyong Shin, Jaerim Park, Minchul Kang, Younghun Go, Zhixiong Niu, Yongqiang Xiong, Gyeongsik Yang, and Chuck Yoo — Korea University; Microsoft Research Asia.

Tags: Geo-distributed Training, WAN Communication, Heterogeneous GPU Clusters

Problem: Geo-distributed LLM training has two coupled bottlenecks: heterogeneous GPUs create stragglers, and slow WAN links make synchronous pseudo-gradient exchange dominate runtime.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces GeoMesh, a synchronous geo-distributed training framework that assigns each worker a different batch size and number of inner steps to balance round time.
  • Reduces communication volume by nearly 32× using compressed sign-based pseudo-gradients plus lightweight magnitude and token-count metadata.
  • On heterogeneous GPUs and Azure-derived WAN conditions, reports up to 70.2% lower time-to-target perplexity and up to 8.0× / 5.6× lower straggler- and WAN-induced GPU idle.

Why it matters: This matters because it makes heterogeneity and WAN bandwidth explicit training variables. Instead of relaxing into stale async updates, GeoMesh keeps synchronization but reshapes the workload and gradient payload so the network is less punitive.

Key insights:

  • The important split is Idlehete versus Idlenet: GPU heterogeneity and WAN exchange are different failure modes and need different controls.
  • Fast GPUs should not wait for slow GPUs; they should consume more local work per round while maintaining a synchronous global update structure.
  • Sign-compressed pseudo-gradients are a pragmatic middle ground: much less WAN traffic than dense synchronization, while avoiding the accuracy damage the authors observe in asynchronous baselines.
  • The paper is a reminder that geo-distributed training is not only a routing problem—it is a joint batch-size, local-step, compression, and network-budget problem.

Actionable lessons: If training spans regions or heterogeneous accelerator pools, expose worker speed, memory capacity, and WAN bandwidth to the optimizer/scheduler. Treat “equal batch per worker” as a baseline assumption to kill, not a sacred invariant.

Useful diagram: Figure 2 is the most useful system diagram: it shows adaptive workload balancing followed by compressed sign synchronization. Figure 1 is also useful for separating heterogeneity idle from WAN-induced idle.

Accelerating Sharded Data Parallelism at Scale with Federated Learning

Authors & affiliations: Gianluca Mittone and Marco Aldinucci — University of Turin.

Tags: Distributed Training, FSDP/HSDP, Communication Reduction

Problem: Fully sharded and hybrid sharded data parallel training can hit prohibitive communication overhead at large scale, especially on multi-tier interconnects whose performance is not uniform across groups.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces FL+FSDP and FL+HSDP, hybrid algorithms that interleave sharded data parallelism with FedAvg-style aggregation.
  • Partitions large deployments into smaller federation groups, keeping expensive collectives mostly local and requiring only lightweight inter-group traffic.
  • On Llama3.1-8B pre-training over 512 A100 GPUs, reports up to 8.04× faster data processing and 4.48× lower evaluation perplexity than corresponding baselines under identical hyperparameters.

Why it matters: This is interesting because it attacks distributed-training communication by changing the synchronization hierarchy. Instead of asking the fabric to make one giant sharded job cheap, it restructures the algorithm into groups that better match tiered interconnect reality.

Key insights:

  • The core trick is reducing the radius of frequent communication: keep heavy sharded-DP traffic inside groups and make cross-group exchange lighter.
  • Global batch-size growth is not free; bounding it relative to federation group size can improve both efficiency and convergence behavior.
  • The result points toward topology-aware sharding groups as an algorithmic control plane, not only a placement concern.

Actionable lessons: For superpod training, compare DP/FSDP/HSDP plans by communication scope and synchronization frequency, not just memory footprint. A hierarchy that maps to fabric tiers may beat a theoretically cleaner flat collective.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is useful: it contrasts standard sharded-DP layouts with the proposed FL-augmented grouping and shows how federation groups constrain expensive communication.

PrefixBench-H100: Characterizing Prefix Reuse and Time-to-First-Token in H100 LLM Serving

Authors & affiliations: Omkar Shewale, Deepak Kumar, and Divakar Kumar Yadav — Illinois Institute of Technology; University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee.

Tags: LLM Serving, KV Cache, H100 Benchmarking

Problem: Prefix reuse should reduce TTFT by avoiding repeated prefill work, but the real benefit depends on runtime scheduling, cache granularity, concurrency, output length, and memory pressure.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds PrefixBench-H100, a reproducible benchmark for prefix reuse on a single NVIDIA H100 across synthetic, chat-style, and retrieval-style workloads.
  • Compares vLLM and TensorRT-LLM while varying shared-prefix length, suffix diversity, arrivals, concurrency, output length, and cache configuration.
  • Finds regimes with large TTFT reductions, regimes where cache pressure erodes benefits, and cases where runtime leadership flips because differences arise above the cache in scheduling.

Why it matters: This is useful because it is a measurement paper, not another cache policy. It helps separate “prefix reuse is good” from the more operational question: when does the serving runtime actually turn cache hits into lower user-visible latency?

Key insights:

  • Cache hit rate alone is not enough; TTFT depends heavily on scheduler behavior under bursty versus steady arrivals.
  • The paper reports 5–6.5× TTFT reduction at long prefixes in sequential reuse, but also shows leadership flips between vLLM and TensorRT-LLM as concurrency and traffic shape change.
  • Some knobs that look cache-relevant are not useful in practice: the authors report TensorRT-LLM tokens_per_block settings as nearly indistinguishable on H100 in their tested backend.

Actionable lessons: For serving evaluation, measure prefix reuse with realistic arrival processes and concurrency sweeps. Do not treat cache-hit percentage as a proxy for latency until the scheduler path is instrumented.

Useful diagram: Figures 1, 2, 5, and 7 are the most useful: they show TTFT reduction with prefix length, runtime leadership flips with concurrency, CDF shape under realistic RAG load, and the crossover surface over prefix length and concurrency.

verified arXiv paper · LLM Training, Collective Communication, Traffic Modeling

The Life of a Token: from Words to Bits on the Wire

Authors & affiliations: Davide Avesani, Pengwenlong Gu, Sotiris Skaperas, and Stefano Secci — CEDRIC - ROC; Cnam.

Tags: LLM Training, Collective Communication, Traffic Modeling

Problem: LLM practitioners often discuss tokens, tensors, collectives, and network traffic in separate mental models, making it hard to reason from model configuration to fabric requirements.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents a token-centered tutorial that traces raw text through tokenization, tensors, parallel execution, collective communication, and final network traffic.
  • Uses concrete numerical examples from Dante’s Divine Comedy to connect model architecture and parallelism choices to communication volume, timing, and structure.
  • Builds analytical traffic models for data, pipeline, tensor, and hybrid parallelism, including AllReduce and inter-node communication hierarchy.

Why it matters: This is more tutorial than new system, but it is a clean bridge between ML abstractions and network-facing consequences. Good reference material for explaining why “tokens/sec” eventually becomes bytes, collectives, and congestion.

Key insights:

  • Parallelism choice determines traffic shape: DP creates gradient AllReduce, PP creates stage-boundary activation transfers, and TP creates frequent intra-layer collective traffic.
  • The paper’s useful framing is token → tensor → collective payload → backend communication hierarchy, which makes model-side decisions visible to network designers.
  • The network hierarchy matters: intra-node accelerator links and inter-node datacenter fabric serve different collective phases and should not be collapsed into one bandwidth number.

Actionable lessons: Use this as a communication-model checklist when reviewing training proposals: specify tensor dimensions, parallelism axes, collective type, payload size, and fabric tier before claiming a workload is “network bound.”

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is the roadmap from tokens to network traffic. Figures 5–11 are especially useful for systems discussions because they visualize DP, PP, TP, hybrid parallelism, AllReduce variants, and backend communication hierarchy.
