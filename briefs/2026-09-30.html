Purlin: Separating Orchestration from the Datapath of Collectives

Authors & affiliations: Osayamen Jonathan Aimuyo and Swapnil Gandhi — Stanford University; Christos Kozyrakis — NVIDIA and Stanford University.

Tags: Scale-up Collectives, NVLink/NVL Fabrics, LLM Serving Runtime

Why this is the top pick: This is the strongest signal today because it targets the software boundary that will matter more as scale-up domains grow from single HGX boxes toward rack-scale NVLink domains. The paper treats collective communication as a layered systems problem rather than as a pile of hand-tuned kernels.

Deeper explanation: Purlin is the cleanest architecture paper in today’s set because it attacks collective communication at the abstraction boundary. Current scale-up collective stacks are fast, but many couple collective semantics, rank orchestration, and hardware-specific copy/reduce mechanisms. That coupling becomes fragile when the hardware domain expands from 8-GPU servers toward 72-GPU and larger NVLink-style racks, and when serving systems need workload-specific collectives for prefill, decode, expert routing, and image-generation pipelines. Purlin’s bet is that the semantic contract and orchestration logic are stable enough to sit above a replaceable datapath. If that abstraction holds, new GPU mechanisms can be adopted without rewriting every collective, and application-specific policies can be tuned without forking the whole stack.

  • Problem: GPU collective libraries often entangle the collective semantics, the orchestration schedule, and the hardware datapath. That makes it hard to adopt new scale-up transfer mechanisms or specialize communication for serving workloads without rewriting too much of the stack.
  • Key contributions:
  • Introduces Purlin, a three-layer scale-up collective framework: layouts and policies at the semantic layer, SNAC for orchestration, and Atom copy/reduce primitives for the hardware-specific datapath.
  • Shows how the same orchestration layer can derive and reuse coordination across collectives while letting the datapath evolve across A100, H200, and B200-class GPUs.
  • Evaluates seven collectives and SGLang integration, reporting up to 5.14× latency speedup, up to 4.50× bandwidth improvement, and serving interactivity gains of up to 2.85× under online overload.
  • Why it matters architecturally: For rack-scale scale-up fabrics, design collective software with a stable orchestration interface and a replaceable datapath. Otherwise every new GPU memory-transfer primitive or serving-specific collective becomes a bespoke fork that is painful to validate and maintain.

Purlin: Separating Orchestration from the Datapath of Collectives

Authors & affiliations: Osayamen Jonathan Aimuyo and Swapnil Gandhi — Stanford University; Christos Kozyrakis — NVIDIA and Stanford University.

Tags: Scale-up Collectives, NVLink/NVL Fabrics, LLM Serving Runtime

Problem: GPU collective libraries often entangle the collective semantics, the orchestration schedule, and the hardware datapath. That makes it hard to adopt new scale-up transfer mechanisms or specialize communication for serving workloads without rewriting too much of the stack.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces Purlin, a three-layer scale-up collective framework: layouts and policies at the semantic layer, SNAC for orchestration, and Atom copy/reduce primitives for the hardware-specific datapath.
  • Shows how the same orchestration layer can derive and reuse coordination across collectives while letting the datapath evolve across A100, H200, and B200-class GPUs.
  • Evaluates seven collectives and SGLang integration, reporting up to 5.14× latency speedup, up to 4.50× bandwidth improvement, and serving interactivity gains of up to 2.85× under online overload.

Why it matters: This is the strongest signal today because it targets the software boundary that will matter more as scale-up domains grow from single HGX boxes toward rack-scale NVLink domains. The paper treats collective communication as a layered systems problem rather than as a pile of hand-tuned kernels.

Key insights:

  • The stable part of a collective is its semantic contract; the volatile part is how a given GPU generation moves and reduces bytes. Purlin tries to keep those concerns from infecting each other.
  • SNAC is the interesting middle layer: it derives where/when coordination from layouts, while Atom owns the generation-specific copy/reduce datapath.
  • The design is explicitly aimed at inference regimes where different collectives, payload sizes, and latency/throughput objectives coexist inside systems like SGLang.
  • The Blackwell/Hopper/Ampere portability angle is practical: production fleets do not upgrade in lockstep, so a useful collective stack needs evolvability without abandoning older GPUs.

Actionable lessons: For rack-scale scale-up fabrics, design collective software with a stable orchestration interface and a replaceable datapath. Otherwise every new GPU memory-transfer primitive or serving-specific collective becomes a bespoke fork that is painful to validate and maintain.

HOCCL: Offloading Collective Communication from GPU Cores to Accelerate Distributed Training

Authors & affiliations: Yao Fei, Gongming Zhao, Hongli Xu, Jin Fang, Jiacheng Zhu, and Zhuolong Yu — University of Science and Technology of China; Shuo Xu — China Mobile (Suzhou) Software Technology Co., Ltd.; Kun Huang — Pengcheng Laboratory.

Tags: Collective Communication, Distributed Training, GPU DMA/RDMA

Problem: Overlapped communication in LLM training still consumes GPU SMs for data movement and synchronization. Those SMs compete with compute kernels, especially for background data-parallel and pipeline-parallel traffic that should ideally hide under computation.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Identifies SM occupancy from communication kernels as an underexplored training-efficiency bottleneck, with measurements on 32 H800 GPUs.
  • Designs HOCCL, a zero-SM collective communication framework built around a stream manager, a DMA-based point-to-point executor, and a collective scheduler.
  • Shows near state-of-the-art communication performance while eliminating communication occupancy on nearly 10% of total GPU SMs and improving end-to-end Megatron-LM training throughput by up to 5%.

Why it matters: The useful systems idea is that “network communication” is not free just because it overlaps. If the communication path burns SM capacity, it directly taxes tensor-core work and can turn overlap into resource contention.

Key insights:

  • HOCCL separates foreground traffic that is hard to hide from background traffic that can overlap but should not steal SMs.
  • The practical blocker is not just RDMA capability; it is getting data into RNIC-accessible memory without repeatedly registering dynamic GPU buffers or using SMs as copy engines.
  • DMA offload trades SM pressure for CPU/GPU coordination and DMA startup latency, so the stream manager and pipelining logic are first-order parts of the design.
  • The result is modest but meaningful: a few percent training throughput gain on H800-class clusters is economically large at scale.

Actionable lessons: When profiling distributed training, measure SM occupancy from communication kernels, not just link bandwidth or NCCL time. For traffic meant to overlap with compute, a lower-bandwidth zero-SM path can beat a faster path that steals scarce compute resources.

verified arXiv paper · MoE All-to-All, PCIe GPU Clusters, Inference Communication

VarioPath: Workload-Aware All-to-All Communication for PCIe GPU Clusters

Authors & affiliations: Yao Fei, Jin Fang, Gongming Zhao, Hongli Xu, Jiacheng Zhu, and Zhijing Xin — University of Science and Technology of China; Size Zheng — Tsinghua University.

Tags: MoE All-to-All, PCIe GPU Clusters, Inference Communication

Problem: MoE and sequence-parallel inference rely on AlltoAllv, but PCIe GPU servers lack a dedicated non-blocking scale-up fabric. Intra-node GPU traffic and NIC traffic contend in the PCIe hierarchy, and MoE demand changes every layer or invocation.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents VarioPath, an AlltoAllv scheduler that separates offline topology analysis from online demand-aware scheduling.
  • Defines reusable AlltoAllv channels as contention-free concurrent transfer patterns and organizes them into a compact searchable catalog.
  • Evaluates four PCIe GPU platforms up to 256 GPUs, reporting 5.88× average AlltoAllv speedup over FAST, 1.72× over DeepEP, and up to 27.2% lower Qwen3 inference latency.

Why it matters: This is directly relevant to heterogeneous AI infrastructure because many inference clusters are not perfect NVSwitch islands. PCIe topology becomes the scale-up fabric, and the runtime has to schedule around its bottlenecks.

Key insights:

  • AlltoAllv scheduling on PCIe is both topology-dependent and demand-dependent; doing all planning online is too slow, while static schedules ignore skew.
  • The channel abstraction is a nice split: expensive topology reasoning is amortized offline, then online scheduling maps current demand onto known safe patterns.
  • Extra intra-node transfers that help on NVLink systems can hurt on PCIe systems because they share the same bottleneck links as NIC traffic.
  • The paper reinforces a broader MoE lesson: expert routing skew should be treated as a fabric scheduling input, not just a model-level imbalance.

Actionable lessons: For PCIe-heavy inference fleets, maintain a machine-specific catalog of contention-free transfer patterns. Do not blindly port NVLink/NVSwitch All-to-All assumptions into cheaper PCIe GPU servers.

verified arXiv paper · MoE Training, Topology-Aware Scheduling, Expert Parallelism

TopoEP: Topology-Aware Load Balancing for Expert-Parallel MoE Training

Authors & affiliations: Jiacheng Zhu, Xie Zhao, Gongming Zhao, Hongli Xu, Yao Fei, and Jin Fang — University of Science and Technology of China.

Tags: MoE Training, Topology-Aware Scheduling, Expert Parallelism

Problem: Dynamic MoE routing creates hot experts and rank stragglers. Existing expert-parallel load balancers often ignore the different costs of intra-node scale-up links and inter-node RDMA, or rely on host-side planning that sits on the critical path.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces TopoEP, a GPU-native load-balancing system that turns current MoE routing results into hot-expert replication and token-rerouting decisions at every layer and microbatch.
  • Uses deterministic GPU planning so ranks independently generate bitwise-identical plans without data-dependent host synchronization or plan broadcast.
  • Integrates with Megatron-LM on 32 H800 GPUs, reducing cross-domain token–expert assignments by 96.2%–98.8% and improving training throughput by 6.2%–11.4%.

Why it matters: TopoEP is interesting because it puts MoE load balancing and topology cost in the same loop. It is not enough to smooth expert load if the fix sprays tokens across expensive scale-out links.

Key insights:

  • The core trade-off is between expert-load balance and communication locality; optimizing only one can regress end-to-end training.
  • Making the solver GPU-resident matters because MoE routing decisions arrive on the critical path immediately before dispatch.
  • The two-stage structure—inter-node placement followed by intra-node refinement—matches the hierarchy of modern GPU clusters.
  • The paper’s large reduction in cross-domain token–expert assignments is the strongest architecture signal: locality-aware replication can reduce both stragglers and fabric pressure.

Actionable lessons: MoE training runtimes should expose topology hierarchy to expert-placement and token-routing decisions. A load-balancing plan that ignores RDMA versus NVLink/PCIe cost can look balanced in compute while being expensive on the fabric.

verified arXiv paper · MoE Inference, Congestion Control, GPU Server Topology

Joint Effects of GPU Server Topology, Parallelism, and Congestion Control on MoE Inference: A Controlled Simulation Study

Authors & affiliations: Kaikai Yuan, Rui Xi, and Yu Liu — Inspur Group, Jinan, Shandong, China.

Tags: MoE Inference, Congestion Control, GPU Server Topology

Problem: MoE inference completion time depends on TP/EP partitioning, collective algorithms, GPU/NIC topology, and network congestion control together. Looking at any one layer in isolation can produce misleading conclusions.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds a controlled ASTRA-sim plus NS-3 experiment matrix over 32 GPU ranks, fixed-length prefill-like Chakra traces, six server topologies, four TP/EP partitions, two TP collectives, and four network/congestion-control modes.
  • Reports that exposed communication accounts for 89.9%–95.8% of mean completion time in the feedback-enabled subset.
  • Finds strong interactions: TP16EP2 is 3.68–4.35× slower than TP2EP16, DBT/Direct is slower than Ring/Direct under the evaluated semantics, and RoCE DCQCN is 23.8%–35.7% slower than RoCE HPCC.

Why it matters: This is more of a simulation study than a new system, but it is useful because it quantifies the cross-layer coupling Ethan cares about: server topology, rank mapping, collective choice, and transport feedback are not independent knobs.

Key insights:

  • Adding GPUs or NICs is not monotonic; benefits depend on whether rank mapping actually balances traffic across injection paths.
  • Congestion-control differences matter, but their impact is conditioned by collective choice and topology.
  • The study’s “communication is 90%+ of completion time” result is a reminder that prefill-like MoE inference can be fabric-bound even before request-level serving effects enter.
  • Simulator semantics matter, especially for collective algorithms; the paper is most useful as a controlled sensitivity map, not as a universal performance ranking.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating MoE inference architectures, sweep topology, TP/EP split, collective implementation, rank mapping, and congestion control together. Single-factor benchmarks are likely to overfit to one topology or one traffic regime.

Useful diagram: No obvious architecture figure appeared in the first pages inspected; the paper is mainly a controlled simulation/methodology study with topology and experiment-matrix analysis.
