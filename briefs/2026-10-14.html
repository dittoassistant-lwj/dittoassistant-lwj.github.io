Most important paper of the day

From ASIC to Fleet: Lessons from Building and Operating a Hyperscaler NIC

Authors & affiliations: Prankur Gupta, Alexander Duyck, Jakub Kicinski, Joseph Provine, Neal Peacock, Prabhakaran Ganesan, Rajiv Krishnamurthy, Chen Liu, Akshay Viswakumar, Timothy Vitkin, Jie Meng, Beatriz Padilla Hernandez, Michael Edwards, Andrei Kozlov, Viren Nathan, Tianyi Cui, Joy Chaoyue Xiong, Raul Hormazabal, Mohsin Bashir, Fred Feng, Nathan Walker, Lavin Khandelwal, Matt Maia, Milo Piazza, Mohanraj Thillainayagam, Mahmoud Mehr, Amithash Prasad, and Lee Trager — Meta Platforms, Inc.

Tags: Hyperscaler NICs, SmartNIC/DPU Operations, Fleet Reliability

Why this is the top pick: This is the strongest Ethan-signal because it treats the NIC as a production system, not a box on a PCIe bus. The paper is about the uncomfortable part of custom network hardware: isolation, firmware rollout, cross-layer observability, and blast-radius control at hundreds-of-thousands-of-host scale.

Deeper explanation: The core lesson is that building a custom NIC ASIC only moves the bottleneck. Once the hardware exists, the real design surface becomes firmware/kernel compatibility, hardware-in-the-loop qualification, per-host-slice repair automation, and telemetry that can correlate NIC counters with switch-port counters. For AI superpods, that maps directly onto the problem of operating increasingly custom front-end, back-end, SmartNIC, and DPU paths without letting one bad firmware image or one shared buffer domain become a rack-scale incident.

  • Problem: Vendor multi-host NICs can share firmware, packet buffers, and management paths in ways that let one host’s fault cascade to neighbors; building a physically isolated custom NIC shifts testing, rollout, observability, and repair burden onto the hyperscaler.
  • Key contributions:
  • Presents Meta’s operational infrastructure for fbnic: hardware-in-the-loop CI, unified NIC/switch observability, targeted firmware upgrades, and scoped repair automation.
  • Reports production outcomes over ten months: 12× reduction in unplanned unavailability, 37% lower mean time to repair, and 2.3× fewer hardware swaps than vendor NICs on the same platform.
  • Extracts multi-host isolation failures from seven years of vendor NIC incidents and turns them into CI tests and operational guardrails.
  • Why it matters architecturally: Treat NIC isolation domains, firmware rollout units, and observability joins as first-class architecture. For AI infrastructure, the lesson is to design the fleet-control plane and failure-containment boundary at the same time as the datapath.

From ASIC to Fleet: Lessons from Building and Operating a Hyperscaler NIC

Authors & affiliations: Prankur Gupta, Alexander Duyck, Jakub Kicinski, Joseph Provine, Neal Peacock, Prabhakaran Ganesan, Rajiv Krishnamurthy, Chen Liu, Akshay Viswakumar, Timothy Vitkin, Jie Meng, Beatriz Padilla Hernandez, Michael Edwards, Andrei Kozlov, Viren Nathan, Tianyi Cui, Joy Chaoyue Xiong, Raul Hormazabal, Mohsin Bashir, Fred Feng, Nathan Walker, Lavin Khandelwal, Matt Maia, Milo Piazza, Mohanraj Thillainayagam, Mahmoud Mehr, Amithash Prasad, and Lee Trager — Meta Platforms, Inc.

Tags: Hyperscaler NICs, SmartNIC/DPU Operations, Fleet Reliability

Problem: Vendor multi-host NICs can share firmware, packet buffers, and management paths in ways that let one host’s fault cascade to neighbors; building a physically isolated custom NIC shifts testing, rollout, observability, and repair burden onto the hyperscaler.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents Meta’s operational infrastructure for fbnic: hardware-in-the-loop CI, unified NIC/switch observability, targeted firmware upgrades, and scoped repair automation.
  • Reports production outcomes over ten months: 12× reduction in unplanned unavailability, 37% lower mean time to repair, and 2.3× fewer hardware swaps than vendor NICs on the same platform.
  • Extracts multi-host isolation failures from seven years of vendor NIC incidents and turns them into CI tests and operational guardrails.

Why it matters: This is the strongest Ethan-signal because it treats the NIC as a production system, not a box on a PCIe bus. The paper is about the uncomfortable part of custom network hardware: isolation, firmware rollout, cross-layer observability, and blast-radius control at hundreds-of-thousands-of-host scale.

Key insights:

  • Physical isolation is not just a hardware nicety; it changes the repair and rollout unit from “whole sled” to “host slice,” which directly reduces blast radius.
  • Observability needs a joined view of host, NIC, and switch counters. A NIC counter alone is often not enough to distinguish device fault, link issue, switch behavior, or host-side driver pathology.
  • Driver-first design with a very small firmware message surface is a reliability pattern: keep policy and diagnosability in code paths operators can inspect and update safely.
  • Hardware-in-the-loop CI should encode real production incident modes, not only synthetic packet I/O success cases.

Actionable lessons: For AI cluster NIC/DPU programs, define the isolation boundary, telemetry join keys, firmware rollout granularity, and rollback procedure before fleet deployment. In design reviews, ask what one bad host, one wedged firmware thread, or one congested queue can damage.

Taking Tokenization off the Host for Agentic LLM Serving

Authors & affiliations: Mohammad Siavashi, Pascal Schwinte, Ali Banaei, Gerald Q. Maguire Jr., Marco Chiesa, and Dejan Kostić — KTH Royal Institute of Technology, Stockholm, Sweden.

Tags: LLM Serving, GPU Frontends, Prefix Caching

Problem: Agentic workloads resend very long mostly-stable contexts. Prefix caching removes most prefill, but the frontend still tokenizes the full request before cache lookup, making CPU tokenization a TTFT and fleet-feeding bottleneck.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds GraphTok, a GPU tokenizer that runs pre-tokenization, BPE merging, and token-ID assembly as a replayable CUDA graph.
  • Preserves exact reference token IDs across GPT-2, Llama 3, Qwen 2.5, DeepSeek-V3, and Gemma 3, while handing short inputs to CPU when GPU launch/transfer overhead dominates.
  • Integrates with unmodified vLLM and NVIDIA Dynamo; with cached prefixes it lowers mean TTFT by 2.1–2.4× and lets one Dynamo frontend sustain 518 requests/s of 121k-token traffic within a 2 s P99 target.

Why it matters: This is an infrastructure bottleneck hiding in plain sight: once prefill is cached, a “small” CPU preprocessing stage can become the limiter for an entire GPU serving fleet.

Key insights:

  • The important capacity metric is not tokenization throughput in isolation; it is how many expensive GPUs one frontend can keep fed under long-context agentic traffic.
  • Cache hits change the bottleneck hierarchy. Optimizing prefill can expose tokenization, cache lookup, serialization, or frontend networking as the new critical path.
  • Exact tokenizer compatibility matters operationally. A faster tokenizer that changes token IDs is not a drop-in serving optimization.
  • The CPU/GPU split should be workload-adaptive: short prompts stay on CPU, while long cached-prefix requests benefit from GPU execution.

Actionable lessons: In serving capacity plans, profile the full request path after prefix-cache hits, not only model kernels. Size frontends by P99 TTFT under long-context reuse, and treat tokenization/cache-lookup placement as part of the serving data plane.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is useful: it shows tokenization rising from 1–2% of cold-cache TTFT to 49–67% after prefix-cache hits in unmodified vLLM, which explains why the frontend suddenly matters.

verified arXiv paper · Hierarchical KV Cache, LLM Serving, HBM/Host Memory

Adaptive DHiSparse: Speculative Decoding over a Load-Adaptive KV Hierarchy

Authors & affiliations: Naji Alazhar and Wassel Alazhar — Umans AI.

Tags: Hierarchical KV Cache, LLM Serving, HBM/Host Memory

Problem: Long-context serving can run out of KV memory before decode GPUs reach useful throughput. Existing hierarchical sparse KV systems reduce HBM residency, but fixed cache budgets leave capacity unused at low load and cannot directly support draft-model speculative decoding.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Extends hierarchical sparse KV serving to DSpark and DFlash2-style speculative decoding while preserving their proposal and acceptance rules.
  • Introduces adaptive residency: protect the required working set, keep extra history in GPU memory when spare capacity exists, and release it under load pressure.
  • Reports 793 output tokens/s per decode GPU for GLM-5.3 at 100K context and concurrency 128 with DFlash2, 1.45× HiSparse and 1.27× non-speculative adaptive serving.

Why it matters: This is relevant to disaggregated and heterogeneous serving because it treats KV state as a tiered memory/data-movement problem rather than a static HBM allocation.

Key insights:

  • Serving capacity is governed by both KV footprint and retrieval traffic; shrinking HBM use can backfire if it turns every selected row into host-memory traffic.
  • Speculative decoding changes KV hierarchy requirements because verification batches multiple queries and introduces tentative draft state that may be rejected.
  • Load-adaptive residency is the right control knob: under low load, spend HBM to avoid host fetches; under high load, reclaim memory to admit more sessions.
  • The design points toward a memory-tier scheduler for serving, where HBM, host memory, and retrieval bandwidth are co-managed.

Actionable lessons: For long-context serving, track HBM residency, selected-row host fetch rate, and admission capacity together. If KV is tiered across host/device or remote memory, make the cache budget load-aware rather than a fixed deployment constant.

Characterizing Parallelism Strategies in LLM Inference: Fundamental Compute-Communication Trade-offs

Authors & affiliations: Javad Mirzaei and Jeebak Mitra — Office of CTO, Dell Technologies Inc., Canada.

Tags: Distributed LLM Inference, Collective Communication, Parallelism Strategy

Problem: LLM serving deployments must choose tensor parallelism, pipeline parallelism, or hybrid layouts, but the best choice shifts with prefill/decode phase, sequence length, batch size, model shape, and interconnect behavior.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Develops an analytical framework decomposing inference latency into computation, inter-GPU communication, and pipeline bubble overhead.
  • Explicitly models tensor-parallel collective communication, pipeline-parallel point-to-point communication, and pipeline utilization across prefill and decode.
  • Validates the model on multi-GPU platforms and uses it to explain when PP-dominant, TP-dominant, or hybrid configurations are preferable.

Why it matters: This is a useful modeling paper for architecture decisions because it turns “try TP=8 and see” into a more principled compute/communication trade-off.

Key insights:

  • Prefill and decode are different systems problems. Prefill has enough arithmetic intensity to hide or amortize some communication; decode repeatedly pays synchronization and memory/KV costs per token.
  • TP reduces per-GPU compute but introduces frequent collectives, so topology and collective latency matter more as decode granularity shrinks.
  • PP lowers communication volume but can waste capacity through bubbles when the workload cannot keep pipeline stages full.
  • Hybrid parallelism is not a compromise by default; it is only useful when the model/workload/hardware point balances collective overhead against bubble overhead.

Actionable lessons: Capacity planning should model prefill and decode separately, include interconnect topology/collective costs, and report pipeline bubble time alongside GPU utilization. For heterogeneous clusters, avoid a fixed parallelism recipe unless the workload mix is stable.
