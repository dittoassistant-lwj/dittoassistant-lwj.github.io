AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s strongest signal is fabric-aware execution: optical circuits that stop wasting simplex lanes, fused kernels that pull RDMA into the tile pipeline, and serving/training systems that make communication cost explicit instead of treating it as a background tax.

Most important paper of the day

Breaking the Duplex Barrier: Lane-Granularity OCS Scheduling for LLM Training

Authors & affiliations: Bangbo Liang, Yupeng Chen, Sicheng Zhao, Peihao Huang, and Guo Chen — Hunan University; Di Yang, Bohua Xu, and Bin Yang — China Unicom Software Research Institute / China Unicom Research Institute; Shizhen Zhao — Shanghai Jiao Tong University.

Tags: Optical Circuit Switching, LLM Training Fabrics, Collective Scheduling

Why this is the top pick: LACE goes straight at a fabric-design inefficiency that matters for LLM training: OCS links are physically simplex, but many schedulers allocate them as symmetric duplex pairs. LLM training traffic is often directionally skewed across pipeline/tensor/data-parallel phases, so symmetric allocation can strand capacity exactly when the collective schedule is predictable enough to exploit.

Deeper explanation: The paper rebuilds the OCS schedule at lane granularity. Instead of assigning equal TX/RX capacity to a node pair, LACE reconstructs directed node-level demand from the training communication schedule, groups consecutive operations that can share a circuit configuration, then binds physical TX/RX lanes and optical paths under per-node lane inventory and multi-OCS constraints. The nice systems point is that it does this without changing collective algorithms, operation order, or rank placement; it is a fabric compiler sitting below the training plan. On a three-server testbed, asymmetric connectivity gives 1.80× faster communication replay and 1.27× faster GPT-2 training than a symmetric-topology baseline, while larger simulations show gains for LLaMA-3.1 70B/405B schedules.

  • Problem: Demand-aware OCS scheduling still wastes capacity when it forces physically simplex optical paths into duplex-port pairs, especially under asymmetric LLM training traffic.
  • Key contributions:
  • Introduces LACE, an offline OCS schedule compiler that independently allocates TX and RX lanes for directed training demand.
  • Maps logical directional allocations into physical lane bindings and optical paths while handling lane inventory, multiple OCS devices, acknowledgments, and configuration recovery.
  • Evaluates the design with a fixed-topology testbed plus larger LLaMA schedule simulations, showing asymmetric lane allocation can reduce communication completion time without modifying the model’s chosen parallelism strategy.
  • Why it matters architecturally: This is a clean example of exploiting workload determinism below the collective layer. If the training DAG already tells you when traffic is directional, a fabric scheduler should not pay a duplex tax just because the abstraction is convenient.
  • Useful diagram: Figure 1 is the key mental model: it contrasts duplex and simplex scheduling using the same TX/RX lane inventory. Figure 4 shows the LACE pipeline from demand profiling to physical lane connections.
verified arXiv paper · Optical Circuit Switching, LLM Training Fabrics, Collective Scheduling

Breaking the Duplex Barrier: Lane-Granularity OCS Scheduling for LLM Training

Authors & affiliations: Bangbo Liang, Yupeng Chen, Sicheng Zhao, Peihao Huang, and Guo Chen — Hunan University; Di Yang, Bohua Xu, and Bin Yang — China Unicom Software Research Institute / China Unicom Research Institute; Shizhen Zhao — Shanghai Jiao Tong University.

Tags: Optical Circuit Switching, LLM Training Fabrics, Collective Scheduling

Problem: OCS fabrics can match predictable LLM communication schedules, but existing demand-aware schedulers often allocate capacity in symmetric duplex pairs even though each optical light path is simplex, wasting capacity under directional traffic skew.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds LACE, an offline compiler that reconstructs directed node-pair demand and allocates TX/RX lanes independently.
  • Solves the practical mapping problem from directional logical capacity to physical lane bindings and optical paths under per-node and multi-OCS constraints.
  • Shows 1.80× faster communication replay and 1.27× faster GPT-2 training on a three-server testbed, with larger-scale simulations on LLaMA-3.1 70B/405B schedules.

Why it matters: This paper is squarely in AI-cluster fabric territory. It treats the collective schedule as compile-time information and uses it to recover optical capacity that a symmetric abstraction would strand.

Key insights:

  • Duplex allocation is an abstraction leak: the physical OCS resource is simplex, while many training phases are not directionally balanced.
  • The fabric can improve communication time without changing rank placement, collective algorithms, or the training operation order.
  • Software acknowledgments and configuration verification are part of the system, not side details; asymmetric paths need a credible return/control path.
  • The gain is schedule dependent, so the right unit of optimization is the directed traffic induced by the full parallelism plan, not average pairwise demand.

Actionable lessons: For OCS-backed AI fabrics, expose TX/RX lane inventory and directed collective demand to the scheduler. If a fabric compiler only sees undirected bandwidth between node pairs, it may leave a large fraction of usable optical capacity idle.

verified arXiv paper · RDMA, GPU Kernels, Computation-Communication Overlap

mKernel: Fast Multi-GPU, Multi-Node Fused Kernels

Authors & affiliations: Ziming Mao, Scott Shenker, and Ion Stoica — UC Berkeley; Yihan Zhang, Shuang Ma, and Yang Zhou — UC Davis; Shawn Wei Chew — UCLA; Costin Raiciu — University Politehnica of Bucharest.

Tags: RDMA, GPU Kernels, Computation-Communication Overlap

Problem: Existing fused kernels overlap compute and local communication well inside an NVLink domain, but distributed LLM training/inference still pays a separate inter-node communication cost at chunk or collective boundaries.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Implements multi-GPU, multi-node fused kernels that overlap computation, intra-node NVLink transfers, and inter-node RDMA at tile granularity.
  • Partitions SMs into compute and communication roles, then uses an on-GPU controller to adapt the partition at runtime for each kernel/input shape.
  • Drives network operations from the GPU through a lightweight command queue and host proxy over RDMA verbs; reports up to 1.72× speedup on GEMM+AllReduce and 1.88× on Ring Attention across two 16-GPU H200 clusters.

Why it matters: This is a useful bridge between CUDA-kernel design and network transport design. The paper is not just “overlap communication”; it asks where the NIC command path, NVLink hierarchy, SM budgeting, and RDMA backend should sit in the kernel execution pipeline.

Key insights:

  • Tile-level readiness is a better synchronization boundary than whole-kernel or whole-chunk completion when inter-node bandwidth is much scarcer than intra-node bandwidth.
  • Hierarchical data movement matters: mKernel minimizes bytes that cross the inter-node network instead of treating all GPU-to-GPU paths uniformly.
  • The best SM split between compute and communication varies by kernel and input shape, so static partitioning can leave performance on the table.
  • The authors observe limited extra benefit from GPUDirect Async versus host-assisted GPU-initiated communication in their setup, which is a useful caution against assuming the newest NIC path is automatically the bottleneck fix.

Actionable lessons: When designing distributed kernels, measure at the tile and NIC-command granularity. Treat SM reservation for communication, RDMA queue depth, NVLink staging, and inter-node byte minimization as one control problem rather than separate CUDA and networking problems.

verified arXiv paper · Rack-Scale Emulation, RDMA/RoCE/InfiniBand, AI/HPC Infrastructure

Cnuas: A Software-Defined AI/HPC Rack-scale Emulation Platform and Hyperscale Data Center Facility Twin

Authors & affiliations: Weqaar Janjua — University of Limerick and Packet Five Networks Ltd.; Eoin O’Connell and Mihai Penica — University of Limerick.

Tags: Rack-Scale Emulation, RDMA/RoCE/InfiniBand, AI/HPC Infrastructure

Problem: AI/HPC rack software depends on scarce hardware and complex device interfaces, while ordinary software abstractions hide the physical placement of accelerators, NICs, switches, management controllers, and rack infrastructure.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents an open-source rack-scale emulation platform based on OCP Open Rack v3 that targets functional experimentation rather than performance-equivalent simulation.
  • Implements CnuasNIC and CnuasSwitch, exposing a guest-visible RDMA adapter and a host-resident hybrid switch supporting RoCEv2 and native InfiniBand behavior.
  • Adds a web UI and rack/facility model to help developers connect software behavior to device placement, interconnects, and management-plane context.

Why it matters: This is not a performance paper, but it is valuable infrastructure scaffolding. For AI clusters, a lot of software breaks at the boundary between application, provider library, kernel driver, virtual device, NIC, switch, and management controller; a modifiable rack-scale twin gives those boundaries somewhere to be tested.

Key insights:

  • Cnuas emphasizes guest-visible device boundaries — for example, libibverbs through a kernel driver bound to an enumerated PCI device — rather than abstract event-only network simulation.
  • Functional emulation is positioned as complementary to physical testbeds and SR-IOV; it trades timing realism for modifiability and access.
  • Including OpenBMC/rack management context is important because AI/HPC failures and orchestration issues often cut across data, control, and facility planes.
  • The visual rack model is not fluff: it helps developers reason about locality, shared resources, and communication paths that normal cloud APIs hide.

Actionable lessons: For cluster software development, maintain a split between performance simulators and interface-faithful emulators. Use the latter to test verbs paths, driver assumptions, management flows, and topology-sensitive orchestration before hardware access becomes the gating factor.

verified arXiv paper · Low-Communication Training, Decentralized Optimization, Pipeline Parallelism

NoLoCo: No-all-reduce Low Communication Training Method for Large Models

Authors & affiliations: Jari Kolehmainen, Nikolay Blagoev, Semih Kara, John Donaghy, Christopher Nies, and Oğuzhan Ersoy — Gensyn.

Tags: Low-Communication Training, Decentralized Optimization, Pipeline Parallelism

Problem: Low-communication LLM training methods reduce frequent synchronization, but many still require a global all-reduce or all-to-all style synchronization step that scales poorly with worker count and network latency variance.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes NoLoCo, an optimizer/training method that avoids explicit global parameter synchronization and does not require collective communication.
  • Combines dynamic pipeline routing with pairwise/subgroup averaging and a modified Nesterov momentum rule to limit weight drift.
  • Reports up to 4% better final perplexity and up to 4× faster wall-clock convergence than DiLoCo across worker counts, model sizes, and bandwidth settings.

Why it matters: This is algorithmic, but relevant to infrastructure strategy: if the optimizer can tolerate weaker, subgroup communication, the cluster no longer has to be designed entirely around frequent global collectives.

Key insights:

  • The expensive part of “low communication” methods can be the remaining global outer step, especially in high-latency or weakly connected environments.
  • Random pipeline routing doubles as an implicit alignment mechanism across pipeline replicas, reducing the need for explicit synchronization.
  • The method moves the systems trade-off from collective bandwidth to convergence/staleness control, so network savings must be evaluated with final quality, not step time alone.
  • NoLoCo is especially relevant when training spans less dedicated or geographically looser accelerator pools.

Actionable lessons: When planning training over constrained networks, do not assume all optimizers need the same fabric. Add communication-structure choices — global collective, subgroup averaging, gossip, or no-collective variants — to the architecture search alongside topology and parallelism.

verified arXiv paper · Disaggregated LLM Serving, KV Transfer, Request Routing

Calibrate, Then Route: A Measured Study of Learned Request Routing for Disaggregated LLM Serving

Authors & affiliations: Srikanta Datta Tumkur, Jay Iyer, Mehar Simhadri, Sai Pavan Kumar, Sai Kapil Kumar, and Ramesh Nampelly — Vizuara.

Tags: Disaggregated LLM Serving, KV Transfer, Request Routing

Problem: Prefill/decode disaggregation makes each request choose both a prefill and decode instance, but simple routing policies are often blind to prompt length, predicted output length, KV-cache pressure, and SLO class.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds a marginal-cost learned router that scores candidate prefill/decode instances from admission-time features.
  • Validates on an eight-GPU disaggregated vLLM cluster with KV caches moved between pools by NIXL, rather than only in simulation.
  • Finds the learned router reaches the best mean goodput in mixed bursty traces, but only after hardware calibration; using simulator-derived constants loses 4.5 goodput points and much of the tail-latency advantage.

Why it matters: This is a serving-routing paper, but it stays relevant because the routing decision is tied to KV movement and decode-pool pressure. It is a reminder that disaggregation mechanisms do not automatically make placement good.

Key insights:

  • Output-length prediction is the load-bearing feature; prompt length alone is not enough to place decode work.
  • Calibration to the actual cluster matters because a mispriced model degenerates toward queue counting.
  • The advantage grows with decode-pool width and traffic heterogeneity, but can vanish in narrow pools or invert under extreme scarcity.
  • Goodput gains are modest but practically meaningful: the learned router reaches with six GPUs what round-robin reaches with seven in the authors’ setup.

Actionable lessons: For disaggregated serving, treat routing as a calibrated systems component. Feed it real KV pressure, length predictions, SLO class, and measured service constants; then test failure regions where greedy cost minimization over-concentrates load.
