Most important paper of the day

PCM: Programmable Congestion Management for Terabit RDMA NICs

Authors & affiliations: Mikhail Khalilov, Timo Schneider, Tommaso Bonato, Siyuan Shen, Marcin Chrapek, and Yehor Kozyr — ETH Zürich; Alberto Merino — Universidad de Castilla-La Mancha; Rip Sohan, Surendra Anubolu, and Mohan Kalkunte — Broadcom Inc.; Ahmad Ghalayini, Abdul Kabbani, and Torsten Hoefler — Microsoft Corporation; Torsten Hoefler also listed with ETH Zürich.

Tags: RDMA, Congestion Control, Programmable NICs

Why this is the top pick: This is the top pick because it attacks a real deployment bottleneck Ethan cares about: how to iterate on AI-fabric congestion behavior without waiting for the next ASIC or rewriting each policy for every NIC API.

Deeper explanation: PCM matters because the AI-fabric control loop is getting too workload-specific for fixed NIC behavior, but too latency-sensitive for a slow host control plane. The paper’s split between portable policy handlers and vendor-optimized backends is the interesting architecture move: it gives operators a way to test CC/LB logic in simulation, carry the same source into software transports, and eventually execute it on programmable RDMA NICs. For superpod-scale networks, that is exactly the kind of interface boundary that can turn congestion management from silicon-dependent folklore into an evolvable systems layer.

  • Problem: Datacenter CC and load-balancing policies evolve faster than RDMA NIC silicon and vendor-specific programmable engines. Operators need portable policy logic that can still run efficiently on simulator, software transport, and real NIC datapaths.
  • Key contributions:
  • Defines PCM, an event-driven programming model that separates portable congestion-management policy from vendor/platform integration.
  • Implements eleven well-known datacenter CC/LB algorithms across four datapaths: Ultra Ethernet simulator, CPU-based UCCL, NVIDIA ConnectX-8, and Microsoft 1P NIC offload.
  • Shows hardware-relevant performance: PCM-Swift on CX-8 reaches one reaction per three 4 KiB packets at 800 Gbit/s using about one-fifth of the NIC hardware threads; in UCCL it matches native Swift at 1.56 Tbit/s goodput with 64 KiB chunk control.
  • Why it matters architecturally: For AI cluster transports, design CC/LB as a portable policy layer with measured signal semantics. Keep policy experiments reproducible in simulation, then require a narrow backend contract for NIC execution. When evaluating new NIC features, ask whether the policy survives across vendors or becomes another one-off control plane.

PCM: Programmable Congestion Management for Terabit RDMA NICs

Authors & affiliations: Mikhail Khalilov, Timo Schneider, Tommaso Bonato, Siyuan Shen, Marcin Chrapek, and Yehor Kozyr — ETH Zürich; Alberto Merino — Universidad de Castilla-La Mancha; Rip Sohan, Surendra Anubolu, and Mohan Kalkunte — Broadcom Inc.; Ahmad Ghalayini, Abdul Kabbani, and Torsten Hoefler — Microsoft Corporation; Torsten Hoefler also listed with ETH Zürich.

Tags: RDMA, Congestion Control, Programmable NICs

Problem: Datacenter CC and load-balancing policies evolve faster than RDMA NIC silicon and vendor-specific programmable engines. Operators need portable policy logic that can still run efficiently on simulator, software transport, and real NIC datapaths.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Defines PCM, an event-driven programming model that separates portable congestion-management policy from vendor/platform integration.
  • Implements eleven well-known datacenter CC/LB algorithms across four datapaths: Ultra Ethernet simulator, CPU-based UCCL, NVIDIA ConnectX-8, and Microsoft 1P NIC offload.
  • Shows hardware-relevant performance: PCM-Swift on CX-8 reaches one reaction per three 4 KiB packets at 800 Gbit/s using about one-fifth of the NIC hardware threads; in UCCL it matches native Swift at 1.56 Tbit/s goodput with 64 KiB chunk control.

Why it matters: This is the top pick because it attacks a real deployment bottleneck Ethan cares about: how to iterate on AI-fabric congestion behavior without waiting for the next ASIC or rewriting each policy for every NIC API.

Key insights:

  • The paper treats CC/LB policy as portable code with explicit telemetry signals and output controls, which is closer to a “P4 for congestion management” mental model than fixed RoCE knobs.
  • The backend split is the key systems move: vendors optimize signal extraction and actuation, while operators keep policy source stable across hardware generations.
  • Per-event reactions and coarser accumulated-feedback triggers let the model cover both packet-scale path decisions and slower congestion-control responses.
  • The Ultra Ethernet connection matters: PCM is not just an academic interface; the authors say it informed ongoing UET programmable congestion-management work.

Actionable lessons: For AI cluster transports, design CC/LB as a portable policy layer with measured signal semantics. Keep policy experiments reproducible in simulation, then require a narrow backend contract for NIC execution. When evaluating new NIC features, ask whether the policy survives across vendors or becomes another one-off control plane.

verified arXiv paper · RDMA/RoCE, Production AI Infrastructure, Inference & Training

Deploying RDMA for Large-Scale Inference and Training Workloads

Authors & affiliations: Yibo Huang, Yukai Huang, Yang Xu, and Jie Wu — Fudan University and ByteDance Inc.; Yi Cui, Huaping Zhou, Jiancong Tong, Zhaoyu Wu, Gen Miao, Haoran Wei, Boyan Ding, Zhuo Jiang, Jianxi Ye, Yibo Zhu, and Chuanxiong Guo — ByteDance Inc.

Tags: RDMA/RoCE, Production AI Infrastructure, Inference & Training

Problem: RDMA reduces communication overhead, but production inference/training has variable-size messages, many logical connections, incast bursts, buffer-lifetime complexity, and service failures that hardware transport reliability alone cannot solve.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Describes ByteExpress, a software communication layer over RDMA NICs integrated under ByteDance’s Archon RPC framework for parameter-server inference and training.
  • Combines an RDMA-aware allocator, zero-copy transfer path, QP multiplexing, receive-credit flow control, software send windows, and RPC-level recovery/fallback.
  • Reports production gains: mean and P99 model-parameter fetch latency down 35.7% and 43.7%, total CPU usage down 10.69%, and training P99 parameter-push latency down up to 74.0% versus TCP, with no PFC pauses in the reported interval.

Why it matters: This is highly practical because it is not “RDMA is faster” in the abstract; it is a deployment report about the software machinery needed to make RoCE useful for real ML services.

Key insights:

  • Zero-copy is mostly an ownership and allocator problem. Without application/transport agreement on buffer lifetimes, RDMA’s data path advantage leaks into copies and registration overhead.
  • QP state, receive capacity, outstanding bytes, RNIC CC, and fabric flow control are separate resources; one knob cannot protect all of them.
  • Transport reliability is below service availability. Timeouts, retries, replica selection, TCP fallback, and connection monitoring still need to live at RPC/service level.
  • The reported no-PFC-pause interval is interesting because the design bounds incast pressure above the RNIC rather than assuming lossless Ethernet magically behaves.

Actionable lessons: If you deploy RDMA for AI services, build the socket-like interface and memory allocator first, not last. Track posted receives, QP pressure, outstanding bytes, and service-level retries separately. For production reviews, ask whether the fallback/recovery path is as carefully designed as the fast path.

verified arXiv paper · AI Training Networks, Load Balancing, Collective Completion Time

Ofan: Optimal Load Balancing for AI Training

Authors & affiliations: Sarah McClure, Scott Shenker, Sylvia Ratnasamy, and Isaac Keslassy — UC Berkeley; Evyatar Cohen, Jakob Krebs, Mark Silberstein, and Isaac Keslassy — Technion; Alexander Shpiner and Mark Silberstein — NVIDIA; Scott Shenker — ICSI.

Tags: AI Training Networks, Load Balancing, Collective Completion Time

Problem: AI training collectives push hosts toward full line rate and care about collective completion time, but destination-oblivious spraying in fat trees can overload specific downward paths after the upward choice is made.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Shows that existing randomized spraying can suffer queue growth with message size, while destination-based rotation can achieve O(1) queue bounds in the modeled setting.
  • Introduces OFAN, a switch-based destination-aware load-balancing scheme, plus POFAN for pipe-based switch architectures with a P4 implementation on Intel Tofino.
  • Evaluates FSDP2 training with Llama-3 405B and recent spraying-aware CCAs, reporting 16–39× lower CCT inflation versus AR/REPS in the highlighted cases.

Why it matters: This one is directly in Ethan’s lane: it links packet-level load balancing, fat-tree structure, congestion control, and collective completion time for frontier-model training.

Key insights:

  • The structural problem is not just randomness; in a fat tree, once a packet chooses an upward path, the destination fixes the downward path, so destination-oblivious spraying can create unavoidable skew.
  • Collective completion time makes the tail path decisive. A few overloaded queues can dominate training-step time even when average utilization looks acceptable.
  • OFAN’s destination-aware rotation is appealing because it is switch-local and targets queue bounds rather than merely improving hash entropy.
  • The paper explicitly tests interactions with network size, message size, packet size, collective type, failures, topology, incremental deployment, and congestion control, which is the right evaluation surface for AI fabrics.

Actionable lessons: For collective-heavy fabrics, evaluate load balancing against CCT and max queue growth, not just mean FCT or aggregate link utilization. Expose destination structure to the spraying algorithm where possible. In simulation, separate LB effects from congestion-control reactions before combining them.

verified arXiv paper · Collective Communication, GPU Runtime, Compute-Communication Overlap

T-CCL: Resource Efficient and Performant Collective Communication using Tensor Memory Accelerator

Authors & affiliations: Keyvan Dadashzadeh, Yuehong Zhou, and Miquel Pericàs — Chalmers University of Technology and University of Gothenburg; Minyu Cui — Linnaeus University.

Tags: Collective Communication, GPU Runtime, Compute-Communication Overlap

Problem: Collective libraries can consume many SM resources to reach bandwidth/latency targets, which competes with overlapped compute and can reduce the real benefit of communication/computation overlap.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds T-CCL, an intra-node collective library that offloads inter-GPU data movement and reduction to NVIDIA Tensor Memory Accelerator operations.
  • Supports AllReduce, ReduceScatter, and AllGather, and reports up to 2.4× over NCCL with unrestricted communication resources and up to 3.42× under restricted resource budgets.
  • Integrates T-CCL into vLLM as a tensor-parallel communication backend and reports up to 1.31× end-to-end inference throughput improvement over vLLM automatic backend dispatch.

Why it matters: The paper is useful because it reframes collective performance as bandwidth per SM consumed, not just bandwidth. That matters for decode-heavy inference and any fused/overlapped kernel schedule where the communication backend steals execution resources from model compute.

Key insights:

  • For intra-node collectives, “fast” is incomplete unless we account for SM footprint. A lower-bandwidth primitive can win if it leaves enough SMs for overlapped GEMM.
  • TMA is not just a memory-copy convenience; using asynchronous bulk movement/reduction changes the scheduling resource balance between communication and compute.
  • Decode-heavy workloads amplify collective overhead because collectives are invoked frequently with smaller per-token work, so lower-SM communication can show up at serving level.
  • The vLLM integration is important because it checks whether microbenchmark gains survive inside an actual serving runtime.

Actionable lessons: When comparing NCCL alternatives, report SM occupancy/resource reservation alongside bus bandwidth and latency. For inference backends, test decode-heavy and overlap-heavy cases separately. Treat TMA/offload paths as a way to reduce interference, not only as a raw-copy accelerator.
