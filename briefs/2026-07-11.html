AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s batch is less about classic transport and more about memory movement: optical broadcast for immutable LLM weights, KV-cache placement/routing, and serving systems that treat NVLink, global KV stores, and prefill/decode partitioning as first-class architecture constraints.

Most important paper of the day

Who Needs DRAM? We Have Fiber

Authors & affiliations: Hannah Atmer, Thiemo Voigt, Yuan Yao, and Stefanos Kaxiras. Affiliations: Uppsala University.

Tags: Optical Interconnects, Memory Disaggregation, LLM Inference Infrastructure

Why this is the top pick: It is speculative, but unusually relevant to AI superpod architecture: it asks whether datacenter fiber can become an active, recirculating delay-line memory for immutable LLM weights, replacing thousands of redundant HBM/DRAM weight copies with optical broadcast and tap-and-amplify receivers.

Deeper explanation: The paper’s core move is to treat model weights differently from normal mutable memory. For inference, weights are immutable and widely replicated across accelerators serving the same model. Fiber Memory turns that into a broadcast problem: stream weights once into long multi-core fiber loops, let nodes tap the circulating optical stream, and use co-packaged optics plus local buffering/regeneration to feed accelerators. The useful systems question is not “can fiber replace DRAM generally?”—it cannot—but whether a narrow immutable-weight path can reduce the energy and capacity tax of repeatedly fetching identical weights from HBM/DDR at cluster scale.

  • Problem: LLM inference clusters replicate the same immutable weights across many accelerators, consuming scarce HBM/DRAM capacity and repeatedly paying local memory-fetch energy for data that is globally identical.
  • Key contributions: Proposes Fiber Memory, a recirculating optical delay-line memory architecture for immutable model weights; combines multi-core fibers, passive optical tap-and-amplify interfaces, co-packaged optics, and regional optical regeneration; reports a case-study claim of eliminating redundant weight storage across 10,000 AI accelerators and reducing weight-delivery energy by over 70% versus HBM3e-style delivery.
  • Why it matters architecturally: It reframes a large slice of inference memory traffic as a fabric/broadcast design problem, not only a local HBM capacity problem.
  • Useful diagram: Figure 1 shows the ring-and-pod Fiber Memory architecture. Figures 2 and 3 show the asymmetric tap-and-amplify receiver and CPO accelerator integration; Figure 4 shows the streamed weight packet structure.

Who Needs DRAM? We Have Fiber

Authors & affiliations: Hannah Atmer, Thiemo Voigt, Yuan Yao, and Stefanos Kaxiras. Affiliations: Uppsala University.

Tags: Optical Interconnects, Memory Disaggregation, LLM Inference Infrastructure

Problem: LLM inference clusters replicate immutable model weights across many accelerators and repeatedly fetch those weights from local HBM/DRAM, burning memory capacity and energy on identical data movement.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces Fiber Memory, which uses hyperscale datacenter fiber as an active recirculating delay-line memory for immutable data such as LLM weights.
  • Builds a concrete architecture around multi-core fiber loops, optical broadcast, passive tap-and-amplify receiver interfaces, co-packaged optics, and regional optical regeneration.
  • Evaluates a case study claiming redundant weight storage can be removed across 10,000 AI accelerators, with over 70% lower weight-delivery energy versus HBM3e-oriented delivery.

Why it matters: This is a fabric-level thought experiment with teeth: if weights are immutable and massively shared, the right abstraction may be broadcast memory, not a larger pile of per-GPU HBM.

Key insights:

  • The proposal is narrowly scoped: it targets immutable model weights, not arbitrary read/write memory. That makes the optical delay-line idea less crazy than it first sounds.
  • The architecture depends on synchronization between compute demand and circulating weight packets, so packet layout, padding, and interleaving become part of the memory-system design.
  • Co-packaged optics matter because the electrical/optical boundary is where a lot of power and buffering overhead would otherwise sneak back in.
  • Failure handling and reconfiguration are open questions: a shared optical stream is efficient, but faults or skew can affect many consumers at once.
  • The paper is useful as a design probe for “can the network become the weight store?”—especially for inference fleets serving a small number of hot models.

Actionable lessons: For future superpod studies, separate immutable-weight traffic from KV/cache/activation traffic. Immutable data may justify broadcast/optical primitives that would be inappropriate for mutable memory or latency-critical reductions.

SMetric: Rethink LLM Scheduling for Serving Agents with Balanced Session-centric Scheduling

Authors & affiliations: Jiahao Wang, Kaizhan Lin, Kaixi Zhang, Jinbo Han, Xingda Wei, Sijie Shen, Chenguang Fang, Wenyuan Yu, Rong Chen, and Haibo Chen. Affiliations: Institute of Parallel and Distributed Systems, Shanghai Jiao Tong University; Alibaba Group.

Tags: LLM Serving, KV Cache Locality, Cluster Scheduling

Problem: Agentic workloads have high intra-session KV reuse, but routing too aggressively toward local KV-cache hits can overload a few instances while other serving instances sit idle.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Characterizes two real-world agentic-serving traces and shows KV reuse above 80% of request tokens, higher than the reported chat workloads.
  • Identifies the locality/load-balancing trap: cache-aware routing preserves reuse but can cap cluster TPS by concentrating sessions on a subset of instances.
  • Introduces balanced session-centric scheduling: route each session’s first request for load balance, then route follow-up requests cache-aware; reports 10–16% higher cluster TPS for colocated prefill/decode and 2–34% higher prefill TPS under disaggregation.

Why it matters: It is not a transport paper, but it is very relevant to network-facing serving architecture: global KV stores and cache-aware routing change the traffic pattern between prefill/decode workers and the cache tier.

Key insights:

  • The first request in a session acts like a placement decision; get that right and later KV-local requests do not have to fight the load balancer as much.
  • Global KV storage relaxes the need to chase every local cache hit, but it is not free—overusing the global tier can move the bottleneck into cross-instance/cache traffic.
  • Agentic serving optimizes cluster TPS more directly than interactive token latency, but the paper still tracks per-token latency as a guardrail.
  • The metric is intentionally simple: session-turn information is derived from user inputs, avoiding a heavy stateful scheduler.

Actionable lessons: In disaggregated serving designs, evaluate routing with both cache-hit locality and instance-level load balance. A high KV-reuse ratio can be a trap if it creates hot shards or hot decode instances.

Towards Efficient Large Language Model Serving: A Survey on System-Aware KV Cache Optimization

Authors & affiliations: Jiantong Jiang, Peiyu Yang, Rui Zhang, and Feng Liu. Affiliations: School of Computing and Information Systems, The University of Melbourne; School of Computer Science and Technology, Huazhong University of Science and Technology.

Tags: KV Cache Systems, LLM Serving, Memory Hierarchy

Problem: KV-cache work is exploding across scheduling, placement, migration, eviction, quantization, and retention, but the field lacks a clean systems taxonomy that connects mechanisms to serving objectives.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Defines system-aware KV infrastructure for serving LLMs, focusing on serving-time mechanisms where KV cache is a primary optimization target.
  • Organizes prior work into temporal behavior (execution and scheduling), spatial behavior (placement and migration), and structural behavior (representation and retention).
  • Builds cross-behavior co-design and behavior-objective maps to show which KV optimizations interact with throughput, latency, memory footprint, and other system metrics.

Why it matters: For Ethan’s infra lens, this is a good map of where KV data movement becomes a network/storage/memory-scheduling problem rather than a pure model-compression trick.

Key insights:

  • The temporal/spatial/structural split is a useful mental model: scheduling decides when KV is needed, placement decides where it lives, and representation decides how much traffic/memory it costs.
  • KV cache is now an infrastructure object spanning GPU HBM, CPU DRAM, SSD, remote memory, and sometimes peer GPUs.
  • Co-design matters: a quantized or retained cache policy can change placement and scheduling decisions, not just memory footprint.
  • The survey is broad, so it is best used as an index for mechanisms and open problems rather than as a single-system result.

Actionable lessons: When reviewing KV-cache systems, classify the mechanism by temporal, spatial, and structural behavior, then ask what cross-layer assumption it makes about the serving fabric and memory hierarchy.

Sangam: Efficiently Serving Diffusion LLMs with the AR Stack

Authors & affiliations: Nitin Kedia, Saurabh Agarwal, Myungjin Lee, and Aditya Akella. Affiliations: The University of Texas at Austin; Cisco Research.

Tags: Diffusion LLM Serving, Prefill/Decode Scheduling, GPU Serving Systems

Problem: Diffusion LLMs do not fit autoregressive serving machinery cleanly: bidirectional attention invalidates exact AR-style KV reuse, while approximate caching creates repeated prefill/decode phases with new interference and partitioning problems.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Shows that cached diffusion LLM serving has a repeated prefill/decode structure, making AR serving concepts relevant but not directly reusable.
  • Introduces Sangam’s deficit token-budget scheduler, which admits in-flight decodes first and admits whole indivisible prefills only when enough token budget accumulates.
  • Explores colocated, disaggregated, and hybrid serving; reports colocated serving works best for decode-heavy LLaDA-8B ShareGPT workloads, while hybrid execution cuts mean latency on prefill-heavy Dream-7B arXiv workloads.

Why it matters: It is a reminder that serving architecture is model-semantics dependent. If dLLMs become relevant, the prefill/decode resource split and scheduling rules need to be redesigned, not copied from AR systems.

Key insights:

  • Bidirectional attention changes cache correctness: committing one token can change other positions’ KV activations.
  • Approximate KV reuse turns dLLM execution into alternating refresh and decode phases, which creates familiar but shifted scheduling pressure.
  • Prefill is indivisible in their setting, so token-budget scheduling has to handle chunky work without starving decodes.
  • Hybrid execution is a useful design point: overflow prefills onto decode workers, but protect decode latency with the same budget discipline.

Actionable lessons: For new model families, derive the serving state machine before choosing the system architecture. Prefill/decode separation, cache reuse, and worker partitioning are not universal constants.

SwiftCache: Efficient LLM Serving for Multi-turn Conversations with Heterogeneous KV Cache Sharing

Authors & affiliations: Jianmin Hu, Minxian Xu, Sa Wang, Chong Ma, Min Shen, Kejiang Ye, Lin Qu, and Chengzhong Xu. Affiliations: Southern University of Science and Technology; Shenzhen Institutes of Advanced Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences; Institute of Computing Technology, Chinese Academy of Sciences; Alibaba Group; University of Macau.

Tags: KV Cache Sharing, NVLink, Heterogeneous LLM Serving

Problem: Multi-turn conversations accumulate large historical KV caches; when local GPU HBM fills, systems offload to CPU memory or SSD, causing expensive reloads and limiting context length.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes SwiftCache, a collaborative inference system where models with lower KV demand donate idle GPU memory to store prefix cache for higher-demand models.
  • Uses cross-model KV-cache sharing over NVLink to avoid slower PCIe transfers, and keeps only the currently active layer’s KV cache in local GPU memory.
  • Reports up to 69% lower P99 TTFT and up to 3.98× longer maximum context length versus vLLM/LMCache and SGLang/HiCache baselines on real workloads.

Why it matters: SwiftCache treats NVLink bandwidth and peer GPU HBM as a shared memory tier for serving, which is exactly the kind of intra-node fabric-aware design that becomes important before going out to CPU/SSD or remote cache tiers.

Key insights:

  • The paper exploits heterogeneity in co-located model memory demand: not every model needs its allocated HBM at the same time.
  • Layer-major cache management reduces the local residency requirement by only keeping the active layer’s KV locally.
  • NVLink is used as a memory-sharing path, not just a collective path, which changes how to think about serving placement inside a node.
  • The main trade-off is interference: donor models must not lose too much performance while lending HBM and NVLink bandwidth.

Actionable lessons: In multi-model serving, measure idle HBM and peer-link headroom as allocatable resources. A good cache hierarchy should prefer peer HBM over CPU/SSD when NVLink bandwidth and interference budgets permit.

Fast take

Read Fiber Memory first if you want the most architecture-provocative idea. Then use SMetric and SwiftCache for concrete KV locality/placement lessons, Sangam for model-dependent serving state machines, and the KV-cache survey as the map of the whole design space.
