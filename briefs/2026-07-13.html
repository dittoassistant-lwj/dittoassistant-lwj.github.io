SiFAR: Synchronization-Free All-Reduce for Low-Latency LLM Inference

Authors & affiliations: Hritvik Taneja, Anish Saxena, Abhishek Revinipati, Jae Hyung Ju, Neal C. Crago, and Moinuddin Qureshi. Affiliations: Georgia Institute of Technology and NVIDIA.

Tags: Collectives, LLM Inference, In-switch Reduction

Problem: Low-batch LLM inference is increasingly bandwidth-bound: tensor parallelism adds memory bandwidth, but every decode step pays All-Reduce synchronization costs that grow with GPU count and are hard to hide when there is little compute overlap.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Identifies the top and bottom barriers in common one-shot/two-shot inference All-Reduce paths as first-order latency costs for small payloads.
  • Eliminates the one-shot bottom barrier with dual buffering, then uses redundant pull with in-switch reduction to keep one-shot-style synchronization benefits while improving scalability.
  • Adds speculative reduction to start transfer before the top barrier, with lightweight validation, and reports up to 52% lower All-Reduce latency plus 18.6% Llama-3.1-8B throughput gain at TP=8.

Why it matters: This is today’s strongest signal because it attacks the latency part of inference collectives directly, not by hoping batch size or kernel fusion hides the network. It is very relevant to agentic/reasoning workloads where every intermediate token pays communication overhead.

Key insights:

  • For low-latency inference, All-Reduce should be treated as a synchronization problem as much as a bandwidth problem; barriers can dominate small payloads.
  • The dual-buffering idea is a clean dependency fix: remove the write-after-write hazard and the bottom barrier becomes unnecessary.
  • The in-switch reduction piece is interesting because it moves beyond using switch reduction only for reduce-scatter; each GPU can pull a full reduced payload while preserving one-shot semantics.
  • Speculative reduction relies on decode-step regularity: after the first All-Reduce, GPUs tend to remain tightly synchronized, so pre-barrier transfer can often be correct and profitable.

Actionable lessons: For superpod inference collectives, measure barrier time separately from byte-transfer time. If low-batch decode is the target, evaluate one-shot/two-shot variants under realistic TP degree, payload size, and switch offload capability rather than only dense-training all-reduce curves.

Trivance: Latency-Optimal AllReduce by Shortcutting Multiport Networks

Authors & affiliations: Anton Juerss, Vamsi Addanki, and Stefan Schmid. Affiliations: Weizenbaum Institute & TU Berlin; Purdue University; TU Berlin & Weizenbaum Institute.

Tags: AllReduce, Topology Algorithms, Torus Networks

Problem: Direct-connect accelerator topologies such as TPU-like tori have limited bisection bandwidth and non-trivial communication distance. Existing AllReduce algorithms often trade off too many communication steps, congestion, or bandwidth inefficiency.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces Trivance, an AllReduce algorithm that completes in log3(n) steps by exploiting both transmission ports of a bidirectional ring.
  • Uses joint reductions to triple communication distance per step while reducing congestion versus Bruck-style approaches by a factor of three and preserving bandwidth optimality.
  • Extends the algorithm to multidimensional torus networks and reports 5–30% improvement over state-of-the-art methods in packet-level SST simulation for latency-sensitive AllReduce sizes.

Why it matters: This is a good theory-meets-systems paper for scale-up/scale-out fabrics: it asks what the collective algorithm can extract from multiport direct-connect topology before you change the hardware.

Key insights:

  • The key move is not just “use both directions”; it combines mirrored collectives so each step advances reductions farther without tripling congestion.
  • Latency-optimal step count matters most for small-to-medium AllReduce payloads, exactly where startup and synchronization costs dominate.
  • The torus extension makes it more relevant to accelerator pods than a pure ring result.
  • The evaluation is simulation-based, so the natural next check is how NIC/GPU injection limits and software launch overheads perturb the clean algorithmic win.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating topology-aware collectives, separate lower-bound step count, congestion, and bandwidth optimality. For torus-like accelerator fabrics, do not assume recursive doubling or ring baselines are close to optimal under multiport constraints.

Opus: Photonic Rail-Optimized Fabric in ML Datacenters

Authors & affiliations: Eric Ding, Barry Lyu, Bhaskar Kataria, and Rachee Singh. Affiliations: Cornell University and University of Michigan.

Tags: Photonic Interconnects, Rail Fabrics, ML Training

Problem: Rail-optimized ML fabrics provide the all-to-all semantics expected by parallel training stacks, but high-radix electrical packet switches and optical-electrical conversions make the scale-out fabric expensive and power-hungry.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes retaining the rail abstraction while replacing packet-switched rail fabrics with optical circuit switches.
  • Introduces parallelism-driven rail reconfiguration: time-multiplexing physical ports across topology configurations aligned with non-overlapping TP/PP/DP communication phases.
  • Implements an Opus control plane and evaluates it on a physical OCS testbed, Perlmutter, and simulations up to 2,048 GPUs, reporting over 23× network power reduction and 4× cost savings with modest training overhead at production-relevant reconfiguration latency.

Why it matters: Opus is directly in Ethan territory: it treats optical circuit switching as a way to preserve ML rail semantics, not as a generic datacenter topology swap. The interesting question is whether phase boundaries in training are stable enough to become a fabric-control primitive.

Key insights:

  • The paper’s strongest abstraction is “keep the rail illusion, change how rails are realized.” That makes it easier to reason about compatibility with existing ML parallelism stacks.
  • OCS one-to-one connectivity is a poor match for simultaneous fan-out, so the paper relies on the temporal structure of hybrid parallelism rather than fighting it.
  • The control plane is in-job: fabric reconfiguration happens at parallelism phase boundaries, making application/runtime timing part of the network contract.
  • The big power/cost numbers are compelling, but the sensitivity to reconfiguration latency and phase predictability is the operational knife edge.

Actionable lessons: For optical AI fabrics, co-design with the training schedule is mandatory. Collect traces by parallelism dimension and ask whether communication phases are isolated enough to safely reconfigure rails without harming overlap or straggler tolerance.

Tessera: Unlocking Heterogeneous GPUs through Kernel-Granularity Disaggregation

Authors & affiliations: Tiancheng Hu, Jin Qin, Zheng Wang, Junhao Hu, Yuzheng Wang, Lei Chen, Yizhou Shan, Mingxing Zhang, Ting Cao, Chunwei Xia, Huimin Cui, Tao Xie, and Chenxi Wang. Affiliations: Peking University; Key Lab of HCST (PKU), MOE; University of Chinese Academy of Sciences; Institute for AI Industry Research, Tsinghua University; Tsinghua University; University of Leeds; Huawei Cloud.

Tags: Heterogeneous GPUs, Disaggregation, LLM Serving

Problem: GPU fleets are increasingly heterogeneous, but most inference disaggregation schemes split work at coarse phases or blocks. That misses kernel-level diversity and can leave performance/cost gains on the floor while introducing extra cross-GPU communication.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Argues that GPU kernels are the right scheduling granularity for aligning application heterogeneity with GPU heterogeneity.
  • Builds a PTX-level analyzer to recover inter-kernel dependencies and preserve correctness when kernels are placed across GPUs.
  • Uses pipelined multi-request execution and workload-aware scheduling to overlap inter-GPU communication with computation, reporting up to 2.3× throughput and 1.6× cost-efficiency improvement over existing disaggregation methods across five GPU types and four model architectures.

Why it matters: This is less pure networking than SiFAR/Trivance/Opus, but it is a useful heterogeneous-infrastructure paper because every kernel-placement decision creates a communication dependency. The core design question is where finer-grained placement stops helping because data movement wins.

Key insights:

  • Kernel granularity exposes differences that phase-level PD or Attention-FFN splits cannot see: compute-bound and memory-bound kernels can coexist inside the same logical block.
  • Correctness is the hard part: once dependent kernels move across GPUs, implicit local memory dependencies become explicit communication edges.
  • The system’s viability depends on pipeline depth; concurrent requests are what hide remote-data stalls.
  • This is a good reminder that “heterogeneous GPU scheduling” is also a dataflow problem, not just a device-selection problem.

Actionable lessons: If designing heterogeneous serving pools, model kernel dependency edges and data volume before adopting fine-grained placement. The win condition is not merely matching kernels to GPUs; it is making the induced communication overlapable under real request concurrency.

Fast take

Read SiFAR first for low-latency inference collectives. Pair it with Trivance for topology-aware AllReduce mechanics, then Opus for the photonic-fabric angle. Tessera is the heterogeneous-infrastructure side quest: useful because fine-grained disaggregation turns placement into a communication-dependency problem.
