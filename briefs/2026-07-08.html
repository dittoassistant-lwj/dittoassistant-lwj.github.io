Most important paper of the day

GORGO: Online Tuning for Cross-Region Network-Aware LLM Serving

Authors & affiliations: Alessio Ricci Toniolo, Rome Thorstenson, and Abinaya Dinesh. Affiliations: Carnegie Mellon University; ART.

Tags: Network-Aware LLM Serving, Cross-Region Routing, KV Cache Locality

Why this is the top pick: This is the most directly network-facing paper today: it treats LLM routing as a coupled data-plane/control-plane problem where WAN latency, queueing, and KV locality all compete in the objective.

Deeper explanation: GORGO is interesting because it turns “which replica should serve this request?” into a network-aware control loop. In long-context serving, the cheapest replica is not simply the nearest one or the one with the best prefix cache hit. The right choice depends on WAN RTT, queue depth, and how much prefill work the KV cache can avoid. That makes it a small but realistic model of the serving data plane Ethan cares about: locality and congestion are both first-class signals, and the policy must adapt as workload shape changes.

  • Problem: Global LLM serving proxies need to choose between nearby replicas, less-loaded replicas, and replicas that already hold the session prefix/KV cache. Existing routing policies usually optimize only part of that cost model, so they can create hotspots or pay avoidable WAN/RTT and prefill penalties.
  • Key contributions: Presents GORGO, a proxy architecture whose routing score jointly models network RTT, prefill cost, and queueing delay through tunable weights. Releases ART-Chat-2.5M, a synthetic long-context, high-prefix-reuse chat dataset derived from production metadata because public chat traces underrepresent this regime. Uses evolutionary strategies to tune routing parameters for p95 TTFT; on held-out windows, reports 6.9–15.5% lower p95 TTFT and 14.3–30.9% lower p95 end-to-end latency versus baseline affinity/prefix policies.
  • Why it matters architecturally: Replica routing becomes a fabric-aware policy surface. A good serving stack should expose cache locality, queueing, and path latency as comparable costs instead of hiding them in separate subsystems.

GORGO: Online Tuning for Cross-Region Network-Aware LLM Serving

Authors & affiliations: Alessio Ricci Toniolo, Rome Thorstenson, and Abinaya Dinesh. Affiliations: Carnegie Mellon University; ART.

Tags: Network-Aware LLM Serving, Cross-Region Routing, KV Cache Locality

Problem: Global LLM serving proxies need to choose between nearby replicas, less-loaded replicas, and replicas that already hold the session prefix/KV cache. Existing routing policies usually optimize only part of that cost model, so they can create hotspots or pay avoidable WAN/RTT and prefill penalties.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents GORGO, a proxy architecture whose routing score jointly models network RTT, prefill cost, and queueing delay through tunable weights.
  • Releases ART-Chat-2.5M, a synthetic long-context, high-prefix-reuse chat dataset derived from production metadata because public chat traces underrepresent this regime.
  • Uses evolutionary strategies to tune routing parameters for p95 TTFT; on held-out windows, reports 6.9–15.5% lower p95 TTFT and 14.3–30.9% lower p95 end-to-end latency versus baseline affinity/prefix policies.

Why it matters: This is the most directly network-facing paper today: it treats LLM routing as a coupled data-plane/control-plane problem where WAN latency, queueing, and KV locality all compete in the objective.

Key insights:

  • Prefix-cache locality is only valuable until the chosen replica becomes queue-dominated; routing needs a live congestion/queue term, not just sticky affinity.
  • Cross-region RTT variation can be large enough to erase cache wins, especially for short or low-reuse requests.
  • The tunable-weight design is pragmatic: it can be deployed as a proxy policy without changing the model engine, while still learning workload-specific trade-offs.
  • The paper’s dataset critique is useful: common open traces are not representative of long-context production sessions where KV reuse is the whole game.

Actionable lessons: For disaggregated or cross-region serving, score replica choice with a decomposed cost model: RTT + expected prefill + queue delay + cache hit benefit. Keep weights tunable per workload window, and log decisions so the control loop can detect when affinity is causing head-of-line blocking.

Design Space Exploration of DMA based Finer-Grain Compute Communication Overlap

Authors & affiliations: Shagnik Pal, Shaizeen Aga, Suchita Pati, Mahzabeen Islam, and Lizy K. John. Affiliations: Advanced Micro Devices Inc.; The University of Texas at Austin.

Tags: Compute/Communication Overlap, DMA Scheduling, Multi-GPU Systems

Problem: Multi-GPU training and inference often leave critical-path communication exposed. Existing overlap techniques mostly operate at tensor-shard granularity, which limits topology support and can miss better schedules available when communication and compute are decomposed more finely.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces FiCCO, a finer-grain compute-communication overlap approach that operates below traditional shard granularity.
  • Characterizes decomposition and contention slowdowns, then maps those inefficiency signatures to static compute/communication operator sizes.
  • Explores a larger schedule design space enabled by DMA-based offload and proposes heuristics that runtimes can use to pick bespoke FiCCO schedules.

Why it matters: This is relevant to scale-up and scale-out AI fabrics because it asks the right low-level question: whether the runtime can expose enough scheduling granularity to actually hide fabric latency instead of merely launching communication earlier.

Key insights:

  • Overlap is not free; decomposing operators can create extra work and contention even when the timeline looks better.
  • Finer granularity broadens the set of usable dataflows and topologies, especially for data-dependent communication such as context or sequence parallelism.
  • DMA engines become first-class schedulable resources, not just copy machinery behind CUDA kernels.
  • The best schedule depends on both operator size and interconnect behavior, so static framework rules are likely brittle.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating overlap, measure exposed communication, operator decomposition overhead, DMA contention, and GPU kernel slowdown together. Treat overlap planning as a topology-aware scheduling problem, and avoid assuming shard-level overlap is the natural granularity for modern accelerators.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is the compact mental model for FiCCO: it shows serial compute/collective execution, the finer-grain DMA/GPU timeline, and the schedule search space. Figures 3–6 are useful for understanding shard-based overlap versus FiCCO and the main inefficiency sources.

Verified arXiv CS paper · distributed training systems / heterogeneous clusters / spmd/hspmd

Hetu v2: A General and Scalable Deep Learning System with Hierarchical and Heterogeneous Single Program Multiple Data Annotations

Authors & affiliations: Haoyang Li, Fangcheng Fu, Hao Ge, Sheng Lin, Xuanyu Wang, Jiawen Niu, Yuming Zhou, Xupeng Miao, and Bin Cui. Affiliations: School of Computer Science & Beijing Key Laboratory of Software and Hardware Cooperative Artificial Intelligence Systems, Peking University; School of Artificial Intelligence, Shanghai Jiao Tong University; Institute of Computational Social Science, Peking University (Qingdao).

Tags: Distributed Training Systems, Heterogeneous Clusters, SPMD/HSPMD

Problem: SPMD systems assume symmetric sharding across mostly homogeneous devices and balanced inputs. Large DL training increasingly violates that assumption through mixed hardware, failures, and uneven sequence lengths, making symmetric communication patterns and static parallelism less expressive.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Proposes HSPMD inside Hetu v2, extending SPMD annotations to express asymmetric sharding and hierarchical communication while preserving a single-device programming view.
  • Uses progressive graph specialization for spatial heterogeneity and dynamic graph switching for temporal heterogeneity.
  • Evaluates heterogeneous devices, unstable devices, and variable data workloads, showing the system can adapt parallel strategies where standard SPMD-style execution is too rigid.

Why it matters: For heterogeneous AI infrastructure, this is a useful systems abstraction paper: it makes communication pattern asymmetry explicit instead of hiding it behind an SPMD interface that assumes every rank looks the same.

Key insights:

  • Heterogeneity is both spatial and temporal: a mixed cluster and a changing sequence-length distribution require different mechanisms.
  • Asymmetric sharding implies asymmetric communication, so the compiler/runtime needs to reason about hierarchy rather than emit one uniform collective pattern.
  • Graph specialization and switching are a clean way to separate stable placement differences from runtime workload variation.
  • The paper is less about a new transport and more about giving the training stack the vocabulary needed to use non-uniform fabrics and devices safely.

Actionable lessons: For mixed GPU/NPU or partially degraded clusters, avoid forcing all ranks into symmetric data/tensor/pipeline partitions. Add explicit hierarchy and asymmetry to the parallelism IR, then let communication planning follow device locality, failure state, and input length distribution.

Useful diagram: Figures 1–4 are useful: they contrast MPMD, scheduler-enhanced SPMD, and HSPMD, then show spatial/temporal heterogeneity examples and the limits of standard symmetric sharding annotations.

Verified arXiv CS paper · distributed training runtime / parallelism strategy / communication scheduling

Piper: A Programmable Distributed Training System

Authors & affiliations: Megan Frisella, Shubham Tiwari, Andy Ruan, Yi Pan, Parker Gustafson, Mat Jacob, Gilbert Bernstein, and Stephanie Wang. Affiliations: University of Washington; Yi Pan is listed with University of Washington and Shanghai Jiao Tong University.

Tags: Distributed Training Runtime, Parallelism Strategy, Communication Scheduling

Problem: Modern foundation-model training composes data, pipeline, expert, tensor, and ZeRO-style parallelism. Production systems still require experts to hand-design the high-level strategy and the low-level per-device communication/compute schedule, making new strategies slow to implement and hard to compare.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces Piper, a programmable distributed training system that decouples high-level parallelism strategy from runtime implementation.
  • Represents training as a unified global DAG IR covering both compute and communication, then compiles per-device execution plans.
  • Uses model annotations and scheduling directives to express strategies such as DualPipe-like PP/EP overlap without hard-coding each strategy into the framework.

Why it matters: Piper is a good read for anyone building collective/runtime stacks: it frames communication overlap and parallelism composition as compiler-level transformations over a global DAG, not as ad hoc framework glue.

Key insights:

  • High-level placement/sharding sets the theoretical load bound; low-level scheduling decides how much of that bound the system actually reaches.
  • Communication operations need to be visible in the IR, otherwise the compiler cannot reason about overlap, interference, or network resource conflicts.
  • The design space is too large for full automation, so a controllable programming model may be more practical than a black-box autotuner.
  • Expert-parallel all-to-all and data-parallel all-reduce can interfere; the runtime needs ways to represent and schedule that contention explicitly.

Actionable lessons: Expose collectives and point-to-point edges in the training IR. Let experts encode strategy intent, but compile it into verifiable per-device schedules with resource-aware overlap. For superpod-scale work, the important test is whether the IR can model cross-node contention, not only single-node kernel order.

Useful diagram: Figures 1–5 are useful: they show DualPipe-like placement/schedules, low-level execution choices for overlapping communication, and Piper’s compiler/runtime overview.

Verified arXiv CS paper · low-bandwidth training / federated llms / decentralized gpu clusters

Photon: Federated LLM Pre-Training

Authors & affiliations: Lorenzo Sani, Alexandru-Andrei Iacob, Zeyu Cao, Royson Lee, Bill Marino, Yan Gao, Dongqi Cai, Zexi Li, Wanru Zhao, Xinchi Qiu, and Nicholas D. Lane. Affiliations: Department of Computer Science and Technology, University of Cambridge; Flower Labs; Beijing University of Posts and Telecommunications; Zhejiang University.

Tags: Low-Bandwidth Training, Federated LLMs, Decentralized GPU Clusters

Problem: Standard LLM pre-training assumes high-bandwidth datacenter interconnects. Weakly connected GPU clusters or cross-silo settings cannot afford frequent all-reduce, but purely local/federated methods have historically struggled to train LLMs from scratch efficiently.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents Photon, a complete cross-silo federated system for end-to-end LLM pre-training.
  • Trains decoder-only LLMs up to 7B parameters in a federated setting and reports comparable or better perplexity than centralized pre-training in their experiments.
  • Reports 35% wall-time improvement over baseline distributed training methods by communicating 64×–512× less, and faster convergence than DiLoCo-like approaches.

Why it matters: This is not a datacenter transport paper, but it is a useful counterpoint: if the network is weak or unreliable, the training algorithm and system can change the synchronization contract instead of asking the fabric to emulate a superpod.

Key insights:

  • The main systems trick is reducing synchronization frequency enough that WAN-scale or institutional bandwidth becomes usable.
  • Photon shifts communication from frequent gradient exchange to periodic model aggregation, which changes failure and straggler behavior.
  • Small client batch sizes plus high learning rates appear central to making federated averaging work for pre-training.
  • This design is attractive for spare/heterogeneous GPU pools, but it trades exact synchronous semantics for a more algorithm-dependent convergence story.

Actionable lessons: For loosely coupled GPU pools, do not start by stretching all-reduce across a bad network. Consider whether the optimizer/training recipe can tolerate lower-frequency aggregation, then design the control plane around checkpoint transfer, secure aggregation links, and client availability.
