Most important paper of the day

Characterizing the Scalability and Performance of Large-Scale AI Training Under Multi-Tenancy

Authors & affiliations: Jacopo Raffi, Thomas Pasquali, Lorenzo Piarulli, Filippo Spiga, Marco Faltelli, Andreas Herten, Domenico Siracusa, Daniele De Sensi, and Flavio Vella. Affiliations: University of Trento; Sapienza University of Rome; NVIDIA; ENEA; Forschungszentrum Jülich GmbH.

Tags: AI Training, Interconnects, Multi-Tenancy

Why this is the top pick: This is the strongest signal today because it studies training as a cluster/fabric phenomenon, not just a GPU benchmark. It puts numbers around the exact design question Ethan cares about: when does the interconnect become the limiter, and how badly does multi-tenancy perturb supposedly predictable training jobs?

Deeper explanation: The useful move is that the paper treats “training performance” as a fabric-sensitive systems property. It separates scale-up, rack-scale, and scale-out behavior, then asks how different parallelization strategies and co-scheduled jobs perturb the communication path. That is exactly the kind of evidence needed when deciding whether to spend effort on a better collective, a topology-aware scheduler, a larger scale-up island, or stricter isolation for noisy neighbors.

  • Problem: Modern AI training performance is shaped by more than model FLOPs: parallelization strategy, allocation shape, network congestion, and interconnect topology all interact, especially when multiple jobs share a large system.
  • Key contributions:
  • Builds a benchmark suite covering multiple AI models and five parallelization strategies, then evaluates scaling behavior across Alps, Leonardo, LUMI, JUPITER, NVIDIA GB300 NVL72, and DGX A100-like settings.
  • Quantifies communication overheads across scale-up, rack-scale, and scale-out configurations, including how parallelization choices move time into collectives and inter-node traffic.
  • Introduces a realistic co-scheduling/noise model to study multi-tenant interference and reports slowdown distributions for concurrent training jobs.
  • Why it matters architecturally: For superpod planning, benchmark the full parallelization stack under placement and co-scheduling regimes, not just isolated collectives. Treat communication fraction and slowdown distribution as scheduling inputs. A good cluster controller should know when a job is scale-up-sensitive, scale-out-sensitive, or interference-sensitive before it places it.

Figures 2–9 then connect that topology context to communication fractions, slowdown distributions, and AllReduce behavior.

Characterizing the Scalability and Performance of Large-Scale AI Training Under Multi-Tenancy

Authors & affiliations: Jacopo Raffi, Thomas Pasquali, Lorenzo Piarulli, Filippo Spiga, Marco Faltelli, Andreas Herten, Domenico Siracusa, Daniele De Sensi, and Flavio Vella. Affiliations: University of Trento; Sapienza University of Rome; NVIDIA; ENEA; Forschungszentrum Jülich GmbH.

Tags: AI Training, Interconnects, Multi-Tenancy

Problem: Modern AI training performance is shaped by more than model FLOPs: parallelization strategy, allocation shape, network congestion, and interconnect topology all interact, especially when multiple jobs share a large system.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds a benchmark suite covering multiple AI models and five parallelization strategies, then evaluates scaling behavior across Alps, Leonardo, LUMI, JUPITER, NVIDIA GB300 NVL72, and DGX A100-like settings.
  • Quantifies communication overheads across scale-up, rack-scale, and scale-out configurations, including how parallelization choices move time into collectives and inter-node traffic.
  • Introduces a realistic co-scheduling/noise model to study multi-tenant interference and reports slowdown distributions for concurrent training jobs.

Why it matters: This is the strongest signal today because it studies training as a cluster/fabric phenomenon, not just a GPU benchmark. It puts numbers around the exact design question Ethan cares about: when does the interconnect become the limiter, and how badly does multi-tenancy perturb supposedly predictable training jobs?

Key insights:

  • Communication fraction is highly strategy-dependent: expert, pipeline, tensor, and data parallel combinations stress different parts of the fabric rather than scaling uniformly.
  • Allocation geometry matters; intra-node, rack-scale, and inter-node placement change whether communication remains a tolerable tax or becomes the dominant term.
  • Multi-tenancy introduces workload-dependent slowdown variance, so average utilization is too weak a signal for admission or placement decisions.
  • The inclusion of NVL72/GB300-style scale-up context makes the paper useful for thinking about the boundary between supernode fabrics and scale-out networks.
  • AllReduce microbenchmarks are useful but insufficient; the paper ties collective behavior back to end-to-end model training runtime.

Actionable lessons: For superpod planning, benchmark the full parallelization stack under placement and co-scheduling regimes, not just isolated collectives. Treat communication fraction and slowdown distribution as scheduling inputs. A good cluster controller should know when a job is scale-up-sensitive, scale-out-sensitive, or interference-sensitive before it places it.

Figures 2–9 then connect that topology context to communication fractions, slowdown distributions, and AllReduce behavior.

DART: Aiming for Tail-Delay Control in Reconfigurable Networks

Authors & affiliations: Hossein Mohammadalizadeh and Holger Karl. Affiliations: Hasso-Plattner-Institute, Internet Technologies and Softwarization, Potsdam, Germany.

Tags: Reconfigurable Networks, Tail Latency, Scheduling

Problem: Reconfigurable systems have to choose target configurations, paths through intermediate configurations, and whether to serve jobs along the way. Under P99 objectives, rare or pass-through configurations can accumulate the jobs that dominate tail delay.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces DART, an opportunistic commitment policy that commits to a target configuration while allowing justified service pauses at traversed configurations.
  • Models asymmetric, heterogeneous, and heavy-tailed reconfiguration times where reaching a target may require intermediate states.
  • Reports the lowest P99 sojourn time across six stress topologies, with up to 23% improvement over the strongest baseline in the paper’s experiments.

Why it matters: For AI fabrics, the paper is not a direct NCCL result, but the control problem rhymes strongly with optical/reconfigurable accelerator networks: every reconfiguration choice trades setup delay against queued work. DART is a clean reminder that tail latency depends on both where the fabric goes and what it opportunistically serves en route.

Key insights:

  • A myopic “serve the next urgent queue” policy can hurt tails by creating detours that delay rare but aging classes.
  • Commitment matters because reconfiguration paths have state and direction-dependent costs; choosing the next state is not independent of the eventual target.
  • Serving intermediate configurations is valuable only when the backlog or weighted delay justifies spending extra time there.
  • The right objective is weighted tail sojourn time, not just mean delay or switch utilization.

Actionable lessons: If evaluating OCS or other reconfigurable AI-cluster fabrics, model reconfiguration paths explicitly. Admission and topology controllers should expose a tail-aware policy: when the fabric is already crossing a useful intermediate state, opportunistic service can be a win, but only if it does not destroy commitment to the real target.

verified arXiv paper · llm serving, moe communication, prefill/decode

AInfer-PD: Communication-Safe In-Place Prefill–Decode Multiplexing for Distributed MoE Rollouts

Authors & affiliations: Guowei Wang, Chaokun Yang, Zhenxuan Pan, Yuhong Guo, Minghua Zhu, Zhechuan Zhang, Shuo Wan, and Xiaowei Zhu. Affiliations: Ant Group, China.

Tags: LLM Serving, MoE Communication, Prefill/Decode

Problem: Agentic RL rollouts alternate decode, environment/tool waiting, and continuation prefill. In distributed MoE serving, simply multiplexing prefill and decode on the same devices can create communication-order hazards across attention/tensor/expert parallel paths.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Identifies crossed collective-order failures that can arise when prefill and decode share distributed MoE execution resources.
  • Proposes AInfer-PD, which uses turnstile-style segmentation and rank-aligned ordering to make in-place P/D multiplexing communication-safe.
  • Evaluates the design on anonymized internal RL rollout traces and reports improved fixed-workload makespan and request completion behavior under high prefill pressure.

Why it matters: This is a nice systems paper for the “serving is now a distributed communication protocol” bucket. The interesting part is not just P/D scheduling; it is the correctness boundary between runtime scheduling and collective communication order in MoE rollouts.

Key insights:

  • In-place multiplexing saves capacity and avoids explicit KV transfer compared with full P/D disaggregation, but it inherits communication isolation problems.
  • Distributed MoE paths combine attention DP, optional TP, and expert parallel communication; prefill and decode can interact across more than one collective stream.
  • The turnstile idea is essentially a communication-ordering contract: split prefill into safe rounds so decode can make progress without deadlocking or corrupting collective order.
  • Agentic workloads make the issue sharper because tool latency and variable turn length create naturally interleaved prefill/decode phases.

Actionable lessons: When designing MoE serving runtimes, do not treat prefill/decode multiplexing as only a local GPU scheduling problem. Specify the collective ordering contract, buffer ownership, and rank alignment explicitly; otherwise an optimization that looks good in one process can break globally.

Compressing AI Traffic: Standardized Neural Network Coding of Visual-Token Representations in Split Vision–Language Inference

Authors & affiliations: Reza Heidari, Hamed R. Tavakoli, and Juho Kannala. Affiliations: Aalto University; Nokia Technologies.

Tags: Split Inference, AI Traffic, Activation Compression

Problem: When a VLM’s visual encoder and language decoder run on different compute nodes, visual-token embeddings become network payload. The paper asks how aggressively that machine-consumed tensor traffic can be compressed without retraining the model.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Applies ISO/IEC 15938-17 Neural Network Coding round trips to the full visual interface of a Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct video QA pipeline.
  • Sweeps rate/quantization settings and reports that Video-MME accuracy stays close to the BF16 baseline up to roughly a 98% transmitted-tensor reduction before collapse.
  • Shows that downstream reasoning can tolerate large numerical distortion, suggesting rate–task optimization rather than conventional rate–distortion optimization for intermediate AI traffic.

Why it matters: This one is adjacent but relevant: it treats activations as first-class network traffic. If split VLM/LLM inference spreads across edge, rack, or disaggregated accelerators, the fabric payload is not HTTP tokens; it is structured tensors with task-dependent compressibility.

Key insights:

  • Intermediate embeddings can be far more compressible than their raw byte size suggests, at least for the tested VLM/video QA pipeline.
  • Accuracy collapse is threshold-like: there is a broad plateau, then a sharp degradation once coarse structure is no longer preserved.
  • Large L2 reconstruction error does not necessarily imply bad task accuracy, so network compression metrics should be tied to downstream model behavior.
  • Standardized training-free codecs may be useful as a deployment knob before investing in learned compression or model retraining.

Actionable lessons: For disaggregated inference, measure bytes-on-wire per internal tensor and build rate controls around task quality, not just tensor fidelity. A transport or serving layer could expose compression tiers for activation/KV traffic, but the safe operating region must be calibrated per model and workload.
