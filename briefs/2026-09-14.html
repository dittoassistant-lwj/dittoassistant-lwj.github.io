Today’s useful signal is less “new model trick” and more “real infrastructure friction”: collective traffic fighting storage on one fabric, heterogeneous disaggregation for newer attention designs, MoE offload pipelines, and better measurement around GPU execution regions.

Most important paper of the day

Sharing a Fabric with Collective Communication: Two Storage Penalties in Deep Learning Training

Authors & affiliations: Chen Wang and Wenzhao Wu — College of Computing and Data Science, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore; Hyojin Kim and Jae-Seung Yeom — Center for Applied Scientific Computing, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, USA.

Tags: Collective Communication, HPC/AI Fabrics, Storage–Network Contention

Why this is the top pick: This one is very much in Ethan’s lane: it studies what happens when NCCL/RCCL collectives and parallel file-system traffic share the same high-speed fabric, then separates two failure modes that are easy to blur together in cluster dashboards.

Deeper explanation: The paper uses a real GNN training workload on a Slingshot-11 system and shows that “I/O hurts training” is not one bottleneck. First, storage fetches can create heavy-tailed DataLoader stalls that expose the input path to the GPU timeline. Second, even when GPU compute is otherwise healthy, parallel-file-system traffic can contend with collective communication and blow up all-reduce latency in microbenchmarks. That distinction matters architecturally: one problem wants data staging, caching, and prefetch shaping; the other wants fabric isolation, traffic classes, QoS, or topology-aware placement. Treating both as generic storage slowness would lead to the wrong fix.

  • Problem: Distributed DL jobs on HPC systems often use the same network fabric for GPU collectives and storage I/O, but operators need to know whether slowdowns come from exposed input stalls, direct collective interference, or both.
  • Key contributions:
  • Characterizes a real distributed GNN training workload on a four-node, 16-GPU Slingshot-11 setup with Lustre, VAST, and DYAD-style staging paths.
  • Separates two penalties: heavy-tailed DataLoader wait times in full training and traffic-class contention that can inflate all-reduce by up to 145× in isolated I/O/collective interference tests.
  • Shows that byte-range staging can hide much of the input path within the GPU compute window, while fabric contention requires a separate mitigation path.
  • Why it matters architecturally: The paper argues for treating storage and collective traffic as co-tenants of the same scarce fabric resource, not as independent subsystems.
  • Useful diagram: Figure 2 shows the Tuolumne system architecture; Figure 1 is especially useful for seeing the separation between DataLoader stalls and backward/all-reduce time across thousands of iterations.

Sharing a Fabric with Collective Communication: Two Storage Penalties in Deep Learning Training

Authors & affiliations: Chen Wang and Wenzhao Wu — College of Computing and Data Science, Nanyang Technological University, Singapore; Hyojin Kim and Jae-Seung Yeom — Center for Applied Scientific Computing, Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, USA.

Tags: Collective Communication, HPC/AI Fabrics, Storage–Network Contention

Problem: Distributed DL jobs on HPC systems often use the same network fabric for GPU collectives and storage I/O, but operators need to know whether slowdowns come from exposed input stalls, direct collective interference, or both.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Characterizes a real distributed GNN training workload on a four-node, 16-GPU Slingshot-11 setup with Lustre, VAST, and DYAD-style staging paths.
  • Separates two penalties: heavy-tailed DataLoader wait times in full training and traffic-class contention that can inflate all-reduce by up to 145× in isolated I/O/collective interference tests.
  • Shows that byte-range staging can hide much of the input path within the GPU compute window, while fabric contention requires a separate mitigation path.

Why it matters: This is a concrete reminder that the AI fabric is not just for collectives. Storage, checkpoint, replay, and input pipelines can become fabric tenants whose tail behavior leaks straight into GPU utilization and collective latency.

Key insights:

  • The paper usefully separates two mechanisms: DataLoader stalls are exposed as input-path latency, while all-reduce interference is a shared-fabric contention problem.
  • Mean input latency is misleading. The steady-state median can look small while a large minority of iterations suffer multi-second stalls.
  • Traffic-class contention can be invisible if you only profile end-to-end training; the isolated benchmark shows the collective itself can become much slower under concurrent storage traffic.
  • Byte-range staging helps because it changes the granularity and timing of I/O, keeping more fetches inside the compute window rather than adding a pre-training staging wall.

Actionable lessons: In AI superpods, profile collectives and storage as a coupled fabric workload. Separate “input not ready” from “collective slowed by competing traffic,” then apply different fixes: staging/prefetch for the former, QoS/traffic-class isolation/topology placement/fabric admission control for the latter.

Useful diagram: Figure 2 shows the Tuolumne architecture, including the shared fabric context. Figure 1 is the operationally useful plot: per-iteration DataLoader wait and backward/all-reduce time make the two penalties visible instead of collapsing them into one slow-iteration bucket.

Rethinking Heterogeneous System Disaggregation for Subquadratic Attention

Authors & affiliations: Arya Tschand — Harvard University, with work done during an internship at NVIDIA Research; Yaosheng Fu, Vikram Sharma Mailthody, Nicolai Oswald, Po-An Tsai, Ritchie Zhao, Oreste Villa, and Karu Sankaralingam — NVIDIA; Vijay Janapa Reddi — Harvard University.

Tags: Heterogeneous Infrastructure, LLM Serving, Disaggregated Attention

Problem: Existing disaggregated serving schemes are mostly designed around dense attention assumptions. Subquadratic attention changes the memory footprint and arithmetic-intensity split, so the old prefill/decode or attention/FFN boundaries may move bytes across devices in the wrong places.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces SQD, a subquadratic-attention-aware disaggregation scheme that splits decode by quadratic and subquadratic attention behavior rather than using a dense-attention template.
  • Models heterogeneous Rubin+LPX-style systems with DRAM-based GPUs and SRAM-only accelerators, showing where subquadratic attention and FFN should run.
  • Proposes mechanisms such as IndexShare to exploit top-k selection locality and hide selected-KV movement behind later-layer execution.

Why it matters: This is relevant because attention architecture is starting to change the systems boundary. If the serving stack assumes dense-attention KV movement forever, it may overpay for transfers or pin the wrong stage to the wrong accelerator.

Key insights:

  • Subquadratic attention can have fixed or much smaller state access than dense attention, so it may belong on a different accelerator class than quadratic attention.
  • The paper’s key systems point is that the best disaggregation boundary is algorithm-dependent: prefill/decode is too coarse when attention families diverge internally.
  • Index selection locality creates a cacheable side channel: selected-KV misses can be prefetched or hidden if the runtime understands decode-layer offsets.
  • The design is speculative in hardware assumptions but useful as a framework for asking what a future heterogeneous inference node should expose to the runtime.

Actionable lessons: For heterogeneous serving, make the planner aware of per-operator state footprint and transfer exposure, not just FLOPs. Keep dense attention, sparse/top-k attention, FFN, and indexer behavior separately modeled so future accelerator pools do not inherit yesterday’s disaggregation boundary.

Useful diagram: Figure 2 is the best architecture figure: it contrasts standard prefill/decode disaggregation, attention–FFN disaggregation, and SQD’s subquadratic-centric split. Figure 7 then shows the proposed placement of quadratic attention, subquadratic attention, and FFN across GPU and SRAM-only accelerator resources.

SeqMoE: Toward Full-Load Performance via Predictive and Graph-Compatible MoE Offloading

Authors & affiliations: Zihan Wang, Yuqi Wang, Lei Gong, Cheng Tang, Chao Wang, and Xuehai Zhou — University of Science and Technology of China; Wenqi Lou and Teng Wang — Suzhou Institute for Advanced Research, University of Science and Technology of China.

Tags: MoE Serving, Offload Runtime, Heterogeneous Memory

Problem: MoE inference only activates a subset of experts, which should make offloading attractive, but practical systems still lose performance when expert transfers miss the compute window or break CUDA graph capture.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Recasts expert activation prediction as a sequence-to-sequence forecasting problem to predict multiple future layers and steps.
  • Builds a future-aware expert residency and prefetching policy for deciding what to keep, evict, and load before expert computation arrives.
  • Designs the offload path to remain compatible with end-to-end graph capture, avoiding one of the common runtime overhead traps in dynamic serving systems.

Why it matters: This is not network-native, but it is infrastructure-relevant: MoE serving creates a data-movement scheduler between accelerator memory and host/storage tiers, and that scheduler has to hit deadlines just like a network transport.

Key insights:

  • The important metric is not just expert prediction accuracy; it is whether the predicted expert arrives before the layer needs it.
  • Future-aware eviction matters because one wrong eviction can cause a later transfer bubble even if the current layer looks optimal.
  • Graph compatibility is a serious systems constraint. A theoretically good dynamic offload policy can still lose if it forces expensive launch/runtime overheads.
  • The design treats MoE sparsity as a predictable sequence, which is a useful mental model for both memory-tier and fabric-tier expert movement.

Actionable lessons: For MoE infrastructure, treat expert movement as deadline scheduling. Record prediction horizon, transfer time, hit/miss timing, and graph-capture compatibility together; optimizing any one of them alone can produce fake wins.

Useful diagram: Figure 5 gives the SeqMoE system overview, while Figures 2–4 explain the sequence dependence, future-aware eviction, and graph-capture interaction that make the runtime design more than a simple cache.

Argus: Orchestrating Cross-Layer GPU Performance Measurements around Semantic Regions

Authors & affiliations: Jianzhu Yao and Pramod Viswanath — Princeton University; Yue Guan, Xinwei Qiang, and Yufei Ding — UC San Diego; Srivatsan Ramesh, Yuanwei Fang, Jian Jiao, Boda Li, Yueming Hao, Bill Yoshimi, Alexey Loginov, Shane Nay, and Adnan Aziz — Meta.

Tags: GPU Observability, LLM Inference, Performance Attribution

Problem: GPU performance questions are usually asked at semantic boundaries such as an operator, layer, or pipeline stage, but measurements come from fragmented profilers, counters, traces, and compiler/runtime variants that are hard to align.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces Argus, a region-centric measurement planner and runtime for collecting cross-layer GPU evidence around explicitly marked semantic regions.
  • Preserves region identity across compilation, execution, and measurement variants so evidence can be mapped back to the code region the developer actually cares about.
  • Automates construction of measurement variants and probes to reduce manual profiler glue work for neural-network kernels and pipeline stages.

Why it matters: AI cluster debugging often fails because the evidence is at the wrong abstraction level: counters say something happened, but not which semantic stage caused it. Argus is useful as an observability pattern for GPU-side attribution before blaming the fabric or scheduler.

Key insights:

  • Semantic-region identity is a first-class systems object. Without it, traces and counters become expensive but hard to act on.
  • Cross-layer measurements need orchestration because different tools perturb execution differently and expose different scopes.
  • The approach is complementary to cluster/network telemetry: it helps determine whether a slowdown starts inside a GPU region before chasing external causes.
  • For automated optimizers, measurement reproducibility is part of the search space; noisy or misattributed evidence can send the optimizer toward the wrong architecture.

Actionable lessons: Instrument AI systems around semantic regions, not only around process-level metrics. For training or serving incidents, correlate network/collective telemetry with region-level GPU evidence so “slow all-reduce,” “slow kernel,” and “slow input path” are separable.

Useful diagram: The PDF front matter and system sections describe the region-centric planner/runtime pipeline; no single architecture figure dominates the paper, but the workflow diagrams around region marking and measurement orchestration are the useful pieces to inspect.
