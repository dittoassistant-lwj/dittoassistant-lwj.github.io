AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s strongest signal is MoE serving and training as a cluster co-design problem: expert dispatch needs hardware-calibrated makespan models, sparse-MoE scaling needs fabric-aware constraints, and KV cache movement is turning into an explicit network scheduling object.

Most important paper of the day

TEMPO: Makespan-Aware Expert-Parallel Load Balancing Across Memory- and Compute-Bound Regimes

Authors & affiliations: Jie Li, Chenxin Jia, Jinliang Shen, Cunzhuang Liu, Ruiyi Ding, Jianwen Xian, Kang He, and Chengru Song. Affiliations: KlingAI Research.

Tags: MoE Serving, Expert Parallelism, Load Balancing

Why this is the top pick: TEMPO is the most directly useful paper for Ethan’s AI-cluster networking lane: it connects MoE expert dispatch, grouped GEMM hardware behavior, synchronization tails, and topology-aware cross-node replica selection instead of treating load balancing as a generic scheduling problem.

Deeper explanation: The paper’s central claim is deliberately nuanced. Existing MoE dispatchers balance proxies, but MoE block time is a synchronized makespan over GPUs. Below roughly 156–168 tokens per expert, HBM weight streaming dominates and the cost attaches to activating expert replicas; above that, grouped GEMM tile rounding makes tokens matter in a staircase-like way. TEMPO fits this with a max-affine profile and uses it to solve per-batch dispatch. The networking-relevant part is the negative result as much as the positive one: when a model is communication-dominated, better compute-side dispatch does not help. That gives a clean diagnostic boundary between “fix the MoE placement/dispatch objective” and “the all-to-all/fabric path is the boss fight.”

  • Problem: Expert-parallel MoE layers synchronize at the slowest GPU, but token-count and activation-count dispatch proxies misprice the real execution regimes.
  • Key contributions: Measures the two-regime expert cost curve; formulates dispatch as fixed-charge makespan minimization; integrates TEMPO with SGLang-style serving and reports Qwen3-235B throughput and p99 latency gains where the phase diagram predicts a win.
  • Why it matters architecturally: It is a practical template for separating compute/memory imbalance from communication-dominated behavior before spending time on fabric tuning.
  • Diagram note: Figure 1 explains the two-regime expert cost and proxy-dispatch failure mode; Figure 6’s phase diagram is the map I’d keep open when thinking about whether a serving bottleneck is dispatch-fixable.

TEMPO: Makespan-Aware Expert-Parallel Load Balancing Across Memory- and Compute-Bound Regimes

Authors & affiliations: Jie Li, Chenxin Jia, Jinliang Shen, Cunzhuang Liu, Ruiyi Ding, Jianwen Xian, Kang He, and Chengru Song. Affiliations: KlingAI Research.

Tags: MoE Serving, Expert Parallelism, Load Balancing

Problem: Expert-parallel MoE serving synchronizes every layer at the slowest GPU, but common dispatchers balance simplified proxies such as token count or activated-expert count. TEMPO shows those proxies break because per-expert time switches between HBM weight-streaming and grouped-GEMM tile regimes.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Measures per-expert MoE execution on two datacenter GPU generations and fits a max-affine cost profile that captures both activation-floor and token-linear regimes.
  • Formalizes per-batch dispatch as a fixed-charge makespan problem and builds TEMPO, a makespan-aware dispatcher that solves off the critical path and integrates with SGLang-style serving.
  • Reports that Qwen3-235B in the predicted win region gains 4–6% throughput, cuts p99 inter-token latency by about 15.6%, and gains 4–7% on 2-node EP16 with a topology-aware source split; DeepSeek-V3 outside the region is communication-dominated and sees essentially no mechanism gain.

Why it matters: This is the strongest paper today because it turns MoE dispatch from proxy balancing into hardware-calibrated makespan minimization, then explicitly explains when the optimization should and should not help.

Key insights:

  • A single decode batch can contain hot experts in the token-linear regime and cold experts in the activation-floor regime at the same time, so token balancing and activation balancing can each be right in different parts of the same batch.
  • Grouped GEMM tile granularity matters: splitting an expert can manufacture padded compute even if it looks balanced in token space.
  • The paper’s phase diagram is more useful than a headline speedup; it predicts when MoE dispatch is compute/memory dominated versus when communication dominates and the balancer cannot help.
  • The topology-aware EP16 split is the most network-facing piece: once expert replicas span nodes, source-to-replica assignment becomes part of the dispatch objective.

Actionable lessons: For production MoE serving, instrument per-expert execution time against both token count and activation count, then choose dispatch policy from a calibrated phase map. Do not assume a token-balanced all-to-all produces the lowest synchronized layer time, and separate compute-side imbalance from true communication-dominated cases before tuning the network.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 is the useful architecture-level visual: it overlays the measured flat/linear per-expert cost regimes and a toy dispatch showing why token balancing can pay too many activation floors. Figure 6’s phase diagram is also worth inspecting because it marks where TEMPO should beat fixed proxy policies.

Compute-Optimal Is Not Cluster-Optimal: Systems-Aware Scaling for Sparse Mixture-of-Experts

Authors & affiliations: Soumajyoti Sarkar, Yuxin Tang, and Sheng Zha. Affiliations: Amazon AGI Foundations.

Tags: MoE Training, Systems Co-Design, Cluster Optimization

Problem: Sparse MoE scaling is usually optimized under model-FLOPs loss laws first, then mapped onto hardware afterward. The paper argues this ordering is wrong because expert count, sparsity, and model geometry directly affect MFU, communication cost, memory footprint, and feasible parallel layouts.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces MOSAIC, an optimization framework coupling a predictive sparse-MoE scaling law with a calibrated performance model for MFU, communication, memory, and parallel-layout choice.
  • Fits scaling laws over sparse MoE models spanning active parameters from 104M to 2.7B and total model sizes up to 79B parameters.
  • Shows that a model-FLOPs-only objective pushes toward boundary sparsity, while a cluster-constrained objective creates an interior optimum determined by deliverable FLOPs and systems constraints.

Why it matters: This is relevant for superpod architecture because it reframes model architecture as a joint optimization with the cluster, not a fixed workload handed to the infrastructure team after the fact.

Key insights:

  • The paper distinguishes model FLOPs from deliverable cluster FLOPs; the latter includes real MFU losses from communication, sharding, memory, and pipeline effects.
  • Sparse MoE knobs are not purely algorithmic. Increasing sparsity or expert split factor can improve loss under a FLOPs budget while making the chosen geometry harder to execute efficiently.
  • The framework searches parallel layouts together with model geometry, which is closer to how frontier training deployments actually fail or succeed.
  • The result is a warning against treating scaling-law outputs as fabric-independent; the network and memory system can move the architecture optimum.

Actionable lessons: When evaluating MoE training candidates, include communication cost, memory footprint, and feasible parallelism in the architecture search loop. A useful design review question is: “What is the cluster-optimal point under the actual fabric and memory hierarchy, not just the compute-optimal point under model FLOPs?”

Useful diagram: Figure 2 shows the MOSAIC workflow from cluster/device inputs through geometry candidates, performance model, scaling law, and final model-plus-parallel-layout selection. Figure 8 is useful for seeing the tension between model-scaling gains and systems cost.

ImpactHO: Importance-Aware KV Cache Transfer for Multi-User Edge LLM Handover

Authors & affiliations: Minwoo Kim, Soochang Song, Namyoon Lee, Bang Chul Jung, and Yongjune Kim. Affiliations: Department of Electrical Engineering, Pohang University of Science and Technology (POSTECH); Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering, Ajou University.

Tags: KV Cache Transfer, Edge LLM, Backhaul Scheduling

Problem: Edge LLM serving needs to preserve user context across handover, but simultaneous KV-cache transfers can saturate the backhaul. Moving the whole cache is often too expensive, while naively dropping cache entries risks accuracy collapse.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Orders each user’s KV cache by importance and transmits the most informative fraction first, converting token-level cache sparsity into communication savings.
  • Models partial-cache accuracy as a sigmoid utility fitted on RULER measurements with R² above 0.99 across models and context lengths.
  • Derives an online weighted water-filling allocator for multi-user backhaul sharing and reports over 93.7% average accuracy in a 500 ms transfer window, within 0.5 percentage points of the full-cache ceiling.

Why it matters: Although this is edge-focused rather than superpod-focused, the abstraction is broadly useful: KV cache is a network-transfer object with unequal byte value, so byte scheduling should be utility-aware.

Key insights:

  • The key systems move is ranking cache entries before transfer, so the first bytes on the wire carry most of the recoverable accuracy.
  • Backhaul allocation becomes a concave utility-maximization problem only because importance ordering front-loads value; random ordering destroys that shape.
  • The paper separates cache-transfer scheduling from model placement: models are assumed already resident at the target edge node, and only user-specific KV state moves.
  • The same mental model applies to rack-scale prefill/decode disaggregation: not every KV byte has equal marginal value under a transfer deadline.

Actionable lessons: For disaggregated or mobile LLM serving, measure marginal accuracy or quality per transferred KV byte. Admission control and bandwidth allocation should operate on utility curves, not just cache size, especially when multiple sessions contend for the same link.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 clearly illustrates the ImpactHO path: source edge node prioritizes KV entries, sends a prefix over the backhaul, and the target reconstructs enough state to resume inference. Figures 3–5 are useful for allocator behavior under bandwidth and handover-rate stress.

Agentic Coding in the Wild: Characterizing GitHub Copilot Traces at Production Scale

Authors & affiliations: Banruo Liu, Haoran Qiu, Íñigo Goiri, Rodrigo Fonseca, Ricardo Bianchini, and Esha Choukse. Affiliations: UIUC; Microsoft Azure Research; Microsoft Azure. Banruo Liu’s work was done while interning at Microsoft Azure Research.

Tags: LLM Serving, Production Traces, KV Cache

Problem: Agentic coding workloads are not simple chat requests: they interleave LLM calls with tools, long contexts, retries, idle gaps, model switches, and compaction. Existing serving assumptions can be wrong without production trace data.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Characterizes sampled GitHub Copilot coding-agent traces from June 2026: 3.2M users, 13M sessions, 761M LLM calls, 95T tokens, and 775M tool invocations.
  • Finds that agent loops are heavily tool-coupled, with about an 87% agent-initiated share and nearly 1:1 LLM-call/tool-call behavior.
  • Shows KV-cache reuse is highly session-structured: average hit rate reaches about 90% within a turn but falls near turn boundaries, drops to about 8% after model switches, and is often cold-started by context compaction.

Why it matters: This is less network-specific, but it gives real workload signals for LLM serving infrastructure: cache residency, idle-time prediction, tool failure loops, and model switching all change capacity planning.

Key insights:

  • Prompt tokens dominate output tokens by orders of magnitude, making prefix/KV reuse the primary serving lever for agentic workloads.
  • Turn boundaries create long idle periods: the paper reports minutes-long gaps that can justify proactive container or KV-cache reclamation if predicted well.
  • Model switches and context compaction behave like cache-destroying events, which matters for multi-model routing and memory-tier policy.
  • Tool failures amplify cost: retry loops can multiply LLM calls and token use, so serving systems need failure-aware workload models.

Actionable lessons: For agent-serving clusters, expose session/turn boundaries to the inference layer. Use idle-time prediction for KV retention/offload decisions, avoid gratuitous model switching when prefix reuse matters, and track compaction as a first-class cache invalidation event.
