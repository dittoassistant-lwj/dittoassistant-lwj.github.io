AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s batch is unusually practical: a reconfigurable topology paper for mixed training/inference fabrics, a PCIe-specific MoE communication path, SmartNIC latency instrumentation, and two serving-infrastructure papers where KV state and routing semantics become first-class system contracts.

Most important paper of the day

MoSE: Mode-Switching Expander for Mixed LLM Training and Inference

Authors & affiliations: Fan Yang and Binglei Wang — Southern University of Science and Technology; Fan Yang, Binglei Wang, Zhenjie Zhou, and Jialong Li — Faculty of Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence, Shenzhen University of Advanced Technology; Ying Zhou — School of Electronic and Information Engineering, Beijing Jiaotong University.

Tags: Optical/Expander Topologies, LLM Training & Inference, KV Cache Traffic

Why this is the top pick: This is the cleanest network-facing paper today because it treats mixed training/inference clusters as a topology-control problem, not just a scheduler problem. The core idea maps directly onto OCS/reconfigurable-fabric questions for AI superpods: the same physical radix can serve different communication phases if the topology template changes at the right granularity.

Deeper explanation: MoSE is the best Ethan-fit today because it connects LLM workload phase behavior to actual fabric shape. Inference with P-D disaggregation concentrates KV-cache movement across a relatively structured interface, while training collectives and expert-parallel traffic want global expansion and low load concentration. The paper’s key move is to treat reconfigurable optical links as a fixed budget that can be reassigned between these two communication geometries. That is exactly the kind of systems question that matters for future AI clusters: not “can the scheduler place jobs,” but “does the network topology itself expose the right mode knob, and at what timescale?”

  • Problem: Shared AI fabrics see two very different traffic regimes: disaggregated inference wants strong prefill-to-decode KV-cache connectivity, while training wants near-uniform global expansion for collectives and all-to-all traffic. A static sparse topology fits one mode badly when the workload mix shifts.
  • Key contributions:
  • Formulates reconfigurable topology design as a fixed-degree edge-allocation problem rather than as adding more ports or links.
  • Introduces MoSE, which switches the same sparse optical edge budget between a P-D-biased topology for inference-heavy periods and a uniform random regular expander for training-heavy periods.
  • Evaluates a 1024-group flow-level topology model over 20 seeds, reporting 90.8% average and 91.9% P95 reduction in load-aware KV communication cost versus a static training topology in inference-heavy mode, and 22.7% average / 27.6% P95 training-cost reduction versus stale static-inference topology in training-heavy mode.
  • Why it matters architecturally: For shared training/inference fabrics, expose workload mode to topology control. If an OCS or reconfigurable tier exists, use it to reallocate a fixed edge budget between KV-transfer locality and global expansion instead of assuming one sparse topology should serve every phase.
  • Useful diagram: Figure 1 is the useful architecture diagram: it shows training and inference sharing one optical fabric, then illustrates how MoSE reallocates links as inference demand increases. Figures 2–5 quantify bottleneck load and KV/training communication cost under the two modes.
verified arXiv paper · Optical/Expander Topologies, LLM Training & Inference, KV Cache Traffic

MoSE: Mode-Switching Expander for Mixed LLM Training and Inference

Authors & affiliations: Fan Yang and Binglei Wang — Southern University of Science and Technology; Fan Yang, Binglei Wang, Zhenjie Zhou, and Jialong Li — Faculty of Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence, Shenzhen University of Advanced Technology; Ying Zhou — School of Electronic and Information Engineering, Beijing Jiaotong University.

Tags: Optical/Expander Topologies, LLM Training & Inference, KV Cache Traffic

Problem: Shared AI fabrics see two very different traffic regimes: disaggregated inference wants strong prefill-to-decode KV-cache connectivity, while training wants near-uniform global expansion for collectives and all-to-all traffic. A static sparse topology fits one mode badly when the workload mix shifts.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Formulates reconfigurable topology design as a fixed-degree edge-allocation problem rather than as adding more ports or links.
  • Introduces MoSE, which switches the same sparse optical edge budget between a P-D-biased topology for inference-heavy periods and a uniform random regular expander for training-heavy periods.
  • Evaluates a 1024-group flow-level topology model over 20 seeds, reporting 90.8% average and 91.9% P95 reduction in load-aware KV communication cost versus a static training topology in inference-heavy mode, and 22.7% average / 27.6% P95 training-cost reduction versus stale static-inference topology in training-heavy mode.

Why it matters: This is the cleanest network-facing paper today because it treats mixed training/inference clusters as a topology-control problem, not just a scheduler problem. The core idea maps directly onto OCS/reconfigurable-fabric questions for AI superpods: the same physical radix can serve different communication phases if the topology template changes at the right granularity.

Key insights:

  • The paper makes the P-D disaggregation versus all-to-all/collective tension explicit: optimizing KV paths can degrade global expansion, and optimizing expansion can leave P-D flows stretched across the fabric.
  • MoSE is intentionally coarse-grained. It does not require per-flow optical reconfiguration; it assumes workload-mode boundaries where switching a topology template is plausible.
  • The useful metric is load-aware path cost, not just hop count. A topology that creates shorter P-D paths but overloads a cut can still lose.
  • The model is flow-level rather than packet-level, so the result is best read as a topology signal that should later be tested with realistic transport, routing, failure, and reconfiguration overheads.

Actionable lessons: For shared training/inference fabrics, expose workload mode to topology control. If an OCS or reconfigurable tier exists, use it to reallocate a fixed edge budget between KV-transfer locality and global expansion instead of assuming one sparse topology should serve every phase.

verified arXiv paper · MoE Inference, PCIe GPU Systems, Expert-Parallel Communication

Efficient Expert-Parallel Communication on PCIe-Connected Consumer GPUs

Authors & affiliations: Jaehwan Lee, Sangmin Lee, Chaewon Kim, Junsik Shin, and Jaejin Lee — Seoul National University; Jaejin Lee is also affiliated with the Graduate School of Data Science, Seoul National University.

Tags: MoE Inference, PCIe GPU Systems, Expert-Parallel Communication

Problem: Expert-parallel MoE inference needs dispatch and combine at every MoE layer, but consumer PCIe GPU systems often lack direct GPU-to-GPU access. Existing EP libraries usually assume stronger scale-up fabrics, while NCCL’s CPU-staged path adds relay traffic and competes with computation.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Presents ThunderEP, a communication design for PCIe-connected consumer GPUs that removes traditional ring relay hops and uses DMA engines instead of GPU compute resources for data movement.
  • Reduces synchronization latency by lowering polling overhead on completion flags in CPU memory and pipelines bidirectional chunk transfer over PCIe.
  • Integrates the design into vLLM and evaluates three MoE models on RTX 4090 and RTX 5090 systems, reporting 2.00× average dispatch speedup, 1.53× combine speedup over NCCL, and up to 1.66× end-to-end speedup over state-of-the-art MoE inference frameworks.

Why it matters: This is useful because not every inference fleet is an NVSwitch palace. PCIe-only consumer or workstation-class clusters are increasingly attractive for cost-sensitive MoE serving, and their communication bottleneck is qualitatively different from NVLink/NVSwitch assumptions.

Key insights:

  • The paper separates MoE communication into dispatch and combine paths and optimizes each for the PCIe hierarchy instead of relying on a general collective.
  • CPU memory is not just a relay; it becomes the synchronization and staging bottleneck. Completion-flag polling overhead is first-order at MoE layer granularity.
  • DMA-engine movement can be better than GPU-kernel communication when GPU compute resources are the scarce resource and PCIe paths dominate anyway.
  • The design reinforces a broader point: EP communication libraries should specialize to the real scale-up fabric, including “weak” fabrics like PCIe-only systems.

Actionable lessons: When designing MoE serving on PCIe GPU nodes, profile dispatch/combine separately and avoid blindly importing NVLink-oriented AllGather/ReduceScatter assumptions. A topology-specific DMA path with careful completion signaling can beat generic collectives.

Useful diagram: Figures 4–10 are especially relevant: they show communication between GPUs in a PCIe node, compare NCCL versus ThunderEP dispatch/combine algorithms, and explain the DMA-based data path plus synchronization protocol.

verified arXiv paper · SmartNICs, P4/FPGA Datapaths, Latency Measurement

LatencyLab: A DPDK-Based P4 Pipeline Latency Measurement Framework for FPGA SmartNICs

Authors & affiliations: Pavani Kuppili and Robert Ricci — University of Utah; Michael Zink — University of Massachusetts Amherst; Zhaoyang Han, Yicheng Qian, Suranga Handagala, and Miriam Leeser — Northeastern University.

Tags: SmartNICs, P4/FPGA Datapaths, Latency Measurement

Problem: P4-programmable FPGA SmartNICs put packet processing on the wire, but open FPGA P4 toolflows often do not expose timestamping at the pipeline boundary. That makes it hard to measure the actual per-packet latency added by a P4 program.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds LatencyLab, a DPDK-based differential measurement framework that avoids PHC/PTP requirements and does not need datapath clock synchronization.
  • Uses a switch multicast setup where one probe copy traverses the VitisNetP4 pipeline and another traverses a matched bypass path; a busy-polling DPDK receiver timestamps both and subtracts arrival times after calibration.
  • Evaluates four VitisNetP4 programs on an AMD Alveo U280 and reports tight, reproducible latency distributions, including 99% of packets within 20 ns of the median and program medians such as FiveTuple 107/137 ns, Forward 149 ns, RemoveHeader 177 ns, and Checksum 364 ns at 250 MHz.

Why it matters: This is not AI-specific, but it is directly relevant to SmartNIC/DPU work in AI fabrics: if we want in-network functions for telemetry, congestion control, packet steering, or collective support, we need a credible way to measure nanosecond-scale pipeline cost.

Key insights:

  • The method cleverly cancels transmit time by comparing pipeline and bypass copies of the same probe, avoiding a synchronized one-way timing setup.
  • Latency distributions matter more than averages; the FiveTuple program can settle into two latency modes 31 ns apart depending on bring-up state.
  • The framework is practical because it uses DPDK and open FPGA SmartNIC tooling instead of custom timestamping hardware inserted into the data plane.
  • For AI-cluster datapaths, a few hundred nanoseconds per programmable stage can be acceptable or disastrous depending on whether the function sits on every packet of a latency-sensitive collective or only on sampled telemetry.

Actionable lessons: For SmartNIC or programmable-switch proposals, require a differential latency measurement, not just throughput. Also check bring-up-to-bring-up stability; a datapath that has multiple latency modes can create hard-to-debug tail behavior.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 shows the core measurement setup, and Figure 2 shows the Open-NIC-Shell datapaths used by LatencyLab. Those diagrams are the fastest way to understand how the bypass and P4 paths are compared.

verified arXiv paper · LLM Serving, Shared KV Cache, Correctness/Security

Preserving Provenance in Shared KV Caches for LLM Serving

Authors & affiliations: Wei Song and Leo Zhang — Griffith University; Yuxin Cao — National University of Singapore; Xi Zheng and Xiao Cheng — Macquarie University.

Tags: LLM Serving, Shared KV Cache, Correctness/Security

Problem: Production serving stacks often combine an engine-local prefix cache with a shared KV-cache tier. Local caches distinguish adapter, weights, and sharing domain, but the shared tier may key only by token content and coarse model metadata, allowing incompatible KV states to collide.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Identifies and studies provenance-blind reuse across vLLM connectors and SGLang releases, spanning 12 models from 7 families and more than 160 configurations.
  • Shows severe correctness and privacy failures: cross-adapter collisions reduce accuracy from 0.94 to 0.64, incompatible KV representations can reduce reasoning accuracy to zero, and missing salt enables 93% prompt identification from timing.
  • Defines a KV provenance contract and implements canonical provenance descriptors plus a differential checker in vLLM and SGLang cache paths, eliminating unsafe reuse while keeping hit-path latency changes within 0.34 ms.

Why it matters: For disaggregated serving, KV cache movement is effectively a data-plane protocol. This paper is a reminder that cache-key design is part of the transport contract: if provenance is stripped at the shared tier, the system can be fast and wrong.

Key insights:

  • The failure sits at a composition boundary: each cache layer can look correct locally while the combination violates the assumptions of both.
  • Adapter identity, weight configuration, KV representation, and sharing-domain salt are not metadata decoration; they are part of the semantic identity of the cached tensor.
  • Timing leakage from shared-cache hits turns cache provenance into both a correctness and privacy issue.
  • The proposed descriptor approach is attractive because it avoids bespoke connector logic for every new provenance dimension.

Actionable lessons: Treat KV-cache keys like protocol headers with a formal compatibility contract. In multi-tenant or adapter-heavy serving, shared-cache systems should reject provenance-ambiguous hits by construction, even if that lowers the nominal hit rate.

verified arXiv paper · Distributed LLM Serving, QUIC Mesh, Heterogeneous Edge/Fleet Infrastructure

Cascadia: A Control-Plane-Free Alternative to Hyperconverged AI Infrastructure

Authors & affiliations: Matias Parij, Pawan Paudel, and Tate Berenbaum — Not Community Labs Inc.; Muthaiah Venkatachalam — Intel Corporation.

Tags: Distributed LLM Serving, QUIC Mesh, Heterogeneous Edge/Fleet Infrastructure

Problem: Fleets of commodity AI PCs have CPU, integrated GPU, NPU, and memory resources, but they do not look like a normal datacenter cluster: nodes are heterogeneous, intermittently available, often behind NAT, and cannot rely on a dedicated routing or scheduling control plane.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds Cascadia, where every node embeds ingress, scheduling, and execution, joins a libp2p QUIC mesh, gossips signed capabilities, and routes OpenAI-compatible requests without a dedicated inference-path control plane.
  • Supports whole-model execution, load-balanced replicas, and pipeline-sharded chains behind one interface, with optional KV-cache mobility after routing moves.
  • Reports a three-node Phi-3.5-mini NPU testbed reaching 3.10× the response throughput of one node under ten concurrent requests and a separate four-node deployment reaching 4.06× direct single-node throughput.

Why it matters: This is lower priority than the fabric papers, but still relevant to heterogeneous AI infrastructure. It explores a very different failure/control model: distributed serving over an authenticated QUIC mesh rather than a centrally orchestrated GPU cluster.

Key insights:

  • The “control-plane-free” framing is really about removing dedicated routing services from the inference path; certificate issuance and fleet management still exist outside it.
  • Every node being ingress-capable changes failure semantics: a request can enter anywhere, but correctness depends on signed capabilities and live-load exchange.
  • KV-cache mobility appears as an optional optimization, which is the right instinct because migration across weak networks can otherwise erase serving gains.
  • This is more edge/fleet serving than hyperscale training, so its lessons transfer mainly to resilient admission, decentralized routing, and heterogeneous resource discovery.

Actionable lessons: For heterogeneous serving substrates, make control-plane assumptions explicit. If nodes are not in a stable cluster boundary, capability gossip, admission certificates, and auditable routing decisions become part of the serving data plane.
