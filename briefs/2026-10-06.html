AI cluster networking & infrastructure brief

Today’s strongest signal is MoE communication becoming a physical-fabric scheduling problem: rail imbalance, receiver incast, expert migration, and elastic cross-facility training all show that “distributed AI” performance lives below the framework abstraction.

Most important paper of the day

RailWave: Adaptive Spatial and Temporal Scheduling for Expert-Parallel Communication

Authors & affiliations: Chutian Wang, Wenhao He, Jingmin Zhu, Qingyu Yin, Heng Xu, and Xiuyu Li — Sun Yat-sen University; Southeast University; Monash University; National University of Singapore; Shenzhen University of Advanced Technology; Renmin University of China.

Tags: Expert-Parallel Communication, AI Cluster Networking, MoE Training

Why this is the top pick: This is the top signal because it attacks a very practical scale-up/scale-out boundary: MoE communication bottlenecks are not only about which expert receives tokens, but also about which physical rail and receiver are hot at a given phase.

Deeper explanation: RailWave is especially relevant because it makes the distinction between logical MoE routing and physical fabric behavior explicit. The paper keeps token-to-expert demand fixed, then improves how that demand enters parallel rails and overlaps at receivers. That is exactly the kind of below-the-collective control plane that matters in large AI clusters where “balanced” at the model layer can still mean hot rails and incast in the network.

  • Problem: Expert-parallel MoE training relies on irregular dispatch/combine All-to-All. Even when expert routing and placement are fixed, physical execution can still overload specific source rails or create receiver incast, leaving parallel network rails underused.
  • Key contributions:
  • Introduces RailWave, a DeepEP-based communication layer that jointly controls rail allocation and node-pair overlap without changing the logical MoE routing demand.
  • Designs RailBalance, a source-local mechanism that redistributes traffic across eligible rails, plus a reusable topology-derived permutation schedule that bounds incast across changing communication phases.
  • Evaluates real GLM-4.5-Air training-derived communication workloads on 32-GPU H800 and H20 clusters, reporting up to 5.84× and 4.36× communication speedups over native execution.
  • Why it matters architecturally: Expose rail-level path choices and receiver concurrency to the EP communication layer. For hyperscale MoE clusters, measure imbalance at the physical rail and receiver level, not just at expert, rank, or node granularity. Treat DeepEP/NCCL-style backends as schedulable datapaths, not opaque pipes.

RailWave: Adaptive Spatial and Temporal Scheduling for Expert-Parallel Communication

Authors & affiliations: Chutian Wang, Wenhao He, Jingmin Zhu, Qingyu Yin, Heng Xu, and Xiuyu Li — Sun Yat-sen University; Southeast University; Monash University; National University of Singapore; Shenzhen University of Advanced Technology; Renmin University of China.

Tags: Expert-Parallel Communication, AI Cluster Networking, MoE Training

Problem: Expert-parallel MoE training relies on irregular dispatch/combine All-to-All. Even when expert routing and placement are fixed, physical execution can still overload specific source rails or create receiver incast, leaving parallel network rails underused.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Introduces RailWave, a DeepEP-based communication layer that jointly controls rail allocation and node-pair overlap without changing the logical MoE routing demand.
  • Designs RailBalance, a source-local mechanism that redistributes traffic across eligible rails, plus a reusable topology-derived permutation schedule that bounds incast across changing communication phases.
  • Evaluates real GLM-4.5-Air training-derived communication workloads on 32-GPU H800 and H20 clusters, reporting up to 5.84× and 4.36× communication speedups over native execution.

Why it matters: This is the top signal because it attacks a very practical scale-up/scale-out boundary: MoE communication bottlenecks are not only about which expert receives tokens, but also about which physical rail and receiver are hot at a given phase.

Key insights:

  • Logical load balance is insufficient: balanced expert and destination-node loads can still concentrate traffic on a hot source rail.
  • Receiver incast remains possible even when source rails look balanced, so spatial rail selection and temporal overlap need to be controlled together.
  • RailWave keeps the demand matrix unchanged; that separation is useful because network-facing optimization can sit under routing/expert-placement policies.
  • A calibrated selector matters because different phases prefer different execution paths; static “one collective strategy” behavior is too blunt for MoE traffic.

Actionable lessons: Expose rail-level path choices and receiver concurrency to the EP communication layer. For hyperscale MoE clusters, measure imbalance at the physical rail and receiver level, not just at expert, rank, or node granularity. Treat DeepEP/NCCL-style backends as schedulable datapaths, not opaque pipes.

Cross-Facility LLM Pre-training on HPC: Elastic Aggregation, Data Leasing, and Queue-Aware Placement

Authors & affiliations: Zarè Palanciyan, Thomas van Osch, Douwe van der Wal, Olivera Kotevska, and Tim Kok — SURF, Amsterdam, The Netherlands; Oak Ridge National Laboratory, Oak Ridge, TN, USA.

Tags: Geo-Distributed Training, HPC/AI Infrastructure, Queue-Aware Placement

Problem: Academic and public HPC allocations are fragmented across facilities with different accelerators, queues, filesystems, software stacks, and no shared high-speed network, making a single LLM pre-training run operationally difficult.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Builds an elastic DiLoCo-style two-loop training setup with token-weighted Nesterov aggregation where zero, one, or many live sites are normal states.
  • Introduces DARL, a heartbeat-backed data-leasing protocol that keeps pre-tokenized corpus blocks disjoint despite crashes, late joins, and work stealing.
  • Adds live queue-aware planning using unprivileged scheduler probes, and demonstrates Qwen3-0.6B pre-training across Snellius, LUMI, and Frontier over intercontinental distances up to 7,400 km.

Why it matters: The paper is not a datacenter fabric design, but it is a concrete systems answer to “what if the cluster is actually several incompatible clusters?” That is increasingly relevant for heterogeneous AI infrastructure and disaster/failover-style training designs.

Key insights:

  • The system explicitly accepts site churn instead of pretending all workers are simultaneously available.
  • Data correctness becomes a distributed-systems problem: no duplicated or lost samples within an epoch is a first-class invariant.
  • Weight exchange/checkpoint overhead is roughly fixed per round, so increasing local steps amortizes WAN/HPC coordination overhead.
  • Queue-aware placement can beat waiting for all sites because queue delay dominates clean synchronous starts in shared HPC environments.

Actionable lessons: If cross-site training is unavoidable, design for elasticity and data leases first, then tune communication frequency. Model queue delay and facility availability as part of the training plan; the fastest accelerator is irrelevant if it sits in a batch queue at the wrong time.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 plots perplexity against tokens and wall-clock time for centralized and federated runs. Figure 2 is also useful because it shows how calibrated accumulation reduces idle time between heterogeneous sites.

verified arXiv paper · Heterogeneous AI Infrastructure, GPU Sharing, AI-RAN

Beaver: Elastic GPU Sharing between ML and Latency-Critical vRAN Workloads

Authors & affiliations: Yuncheng Yao, Zhenzhou Qi, Junyao Zheng, Chung-Hsuan Tung, Danyang Zhuo, and Tingjun Chen — Departments of Computer Science and Electrical & Computer Engineering, Duke University.

Tags: Heterogeneous AI Infrastructure, GPU Sharing, AI-RAN

Problem: AI-RAN co-locates latency-critical vRAN processing and best-effort ML/LLM workloads on the same GPU, but the workloads have asymmetric objectives: vRAN has strict slot deadlines while ML wants throughput from spare compute and memory bandwidth.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Characterizes sharing opportunities in NVIDIA Aerial vRAN pipelines, including under-utilized SM and HBM resources across slots and pipeline stages.
  • Designs Beaver with workload-aware SM sizing, slot-granular SM repartitioning, and a cooperative HBM bandwidth guard that rewrites compiled ML kernels to yield bandwidth during vRAN memory-critical phases.
  • Evaluates with heterogeneous multi-cell workloads, cellular traces, production inference kernels, and LLM serving; on H200 it keeps vRAN p99.9 latency within a 1.5 ms uplink deadline while retaining 74% of Llama-3.3-70B serving throughput.

Why it matters: This is useful as a heterogeneous-infrastructure paper: it treats GPUs as shared real-time resources, not just batch accelerators. The network angle is AI-RAN/vRAN co-tenancy, where serving throughput must respect radio deadlines.

Key insights:

  • Spatial GPU partitioning alone is too coarse for slot-level vRAN variability.
  • HBM bandwidth, not only SM count, needs an explicit guard because vRAN stages have memory-critical intervals.
  • The right isolation boundary is temporal and workload-aware: reclaim slack when vRAN is light, yield quickly when radio processing enters a critical phase.
  • LLM serving can be a well-behaved co-tenant if its kernels participate in resource yielding rather than blindly competing for the GPU.

Actionable lessons: For mixed AI plus real-time infrastructure, schedule at the timescale of the strict workload, not the ML workload. Track both compute and memory-bandwidth envelopes, and make best-effort kernels cooperative so isolation does not require leaving large GPU partitions idle.

verified arXiv paper · MoE Serving, Disaggregated LLM Serving, NVLink/Expert Migration

AFORE: Attention-FFN Disaggregation with Overlapped Reconfiguration of Experts

Authors & affiliations: Wenshuang Li, Youhe Jiang, You Peng, Jiawei Jiang, and Binhang Yuan — The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology; University of Cambridge; Wuhan University.

Tags: MoE Serving, Disaggregated LLM Serving, NVLink/Expert Migration

Problem: Attention-FFN disaggregation separates attention and feed-forward pools for MoE serving, but that can make expert load imbalance more harmful because overloaded experts directly slow the independent FFN pipeline stage.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Formulates temporal expert reconfiguration for AFD-based MoE serving as an online scheduling problem that decides when and which experts to migrate for upcoming microbatches.
  • Implements microbatch-level demand prefetching and overlaps expert migration with preceding in-flight microbatches; expert transfers use NVLink-based GPU-GPU migration.
  • Evaluates on a 110B-parameter GLM-4.5-Air MoE under four dynamic workloads, improving output throughput by 10.1–17.6% and reducing P95 inter-token latency by 7.1–9.5% versus the strongest baseline.

Why it matters: This is a serving-side counterpart to MoE communication scheduling: once the architecture disaggregates attention and FFN, expert placement becomes a live datapath decision rather than an offline layout.

Key insights:

  • AFD exposes near-future expert-token distributions before FFN execution, which creates a forecasting window for placement decisions.
  • Migration overhead can be hidden if expert movement is overlapped with in-flight microbatch computation.
  • Static expert placement turns route skew into FFN-stage critical-path delay; dynamic placement attacks the actual stage bottleneck.
  • The design depends on fast GPU-GPU transfer paths such as NVLink, so topology awareness is still lurking under the serving scheduler.

Actionable lessons: For disaggregated MoE serving, couple placement decisions to pipeline visibility. Do not migrate experts just because load changed; migrate only when the predicted FFN critical path improves after accounting for transfer overhead and topology.

verified arXiv paper · Distributed GPU Training, Communication Reduction, Data Movement

ByteSplat: Efficient Distributed 3D Gaussian Splatting Training via Intra- and Inter-GPU communication reduction

Authors & affiliations: Shuo Wu, He Zhu, Han Zhao, Xiaohui Zhang, Yaqian Zhao, Hui Wei, Ruyang Li, Hongzhi Shi, Lihua Lu, Jingwen Leng, Yu Feng, and Minyi Guo — Shanghai Jiao Tong University, China; IEIT SYSTEMS Co., Ltd., China.

Tags: Distributed GPU Training, Communication Reduction, Data Movement

Problem: Distributed 3D Gaussian Splatting training needs multiple GPUs for large scenes, but performance is constrained by repeated off-chip rasterization traffic and inter-GPU communication of partial Gaussian gradients.

Key contributions from the authors:

  • Fuses forward and backward rasterization into a single GPU kernel to retain intermediate results on chip and reduce redundant off-chip memory traffic.
  • Adds hardware-aware pruning that respects rendering quality and per-tile shared-memory constraints.
  • Uses gradient sparsity to elide zero partial gradients, compact remaining records, and aggregate received gradients on owner GPUs; reports 63.4% less intra-GPU off-chip traffic, 65.8% less backward inter-GPU communication volume, and up to 6.1× training speedup on eight GPUs.

Why it matters: This is not an LLM paper, but it is a clean data-movement paper for distributed GPU training. The relevant signal is the joint treatment of intra-GPU memory traffic and inter-GPU gradient exchange instead of optimizing one while ignoring the other.

Key insights:

  • Communication reduction starts inside the kernel: avoiding off-chip writes can also reduce the data that later needs cross-GPU aggregation.
  • Sparse gradient structure is a practical transport optimization opportunity when the encode/decode path is GPU-efficient.
  • On-chip storage constraints can limit fused execution, so pruning has to be hardware-aware rather than purely algorithmic.
  • World-model and 3DGS workloads may stress GPU memory movement differently from transformer training, but the systems pattern is transferable.

Actionable lessons: When optimizing distributed accelerator workloads, draw the full dataflow from SRAM/HBM to cross-GPU exchange. Compression or sparsity wins only matter if the packing, transfer, and aggregation kernels do not become the new bottleneck.

Useful diagram: Figure 1 gives a useful pipeline view of distributed 3DGS: owner GPUs send Gaussian attributes to rendering GPUs, rendering GPUs compute partial gradients, and backward all-to-all communication returns gradients for aggregation.
